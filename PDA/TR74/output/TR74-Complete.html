1.0 Introduction

Original Text

Reprocessing and reworking are tools that biological manufacturers can use when a manufacturing process step has operated outside of its validated operating ranges. For reprocessing, rather than proceeding with the next manufacturing process step, the product is rerun through the previous manufacturing process step operated within the acceptable operating ranges. For reworking, the product is subjected to one or more processing steps that are different from the established manufacturing process to obtain acceptable product.

In biological manufacturing, however, reprocessing, and especially reworking, should be considered only in exceptional circumstances. Reworking can be considered during events such as critical equipment failures, out of specifications of critical quality attributes of drug substance or drug product, and/or deviation from in-process controls that impact quality of the drug substance or drug product (further conditions to consider are stated in Section 5.0).

Reprocessing and reworking are deviations that are managed within the corrective and preventive actions (CAPA) of the manufacturer's quality management system. An essential prerequisite for the acceptance of a reprocessing or reworking step is a clear indication of the root cause. Due to the size and complexity of biological products, including the limitations in testing methods (especially measurement of functional biological activity, i.e., potency), reprocessing, and especially reworking, are limited for biologicals. Thus, they should be entered into with caution so as not to impact the product's critical quality attributes (CQA), and the costs and risks should be carefully evaluated and documented.

Effective reprocessing and reworking strategies should integrate supply chain resilience considerations, including forecasting, inventory buffers, and manufacturing scheduling. Combining quality risk management assessments (e.g., a FMEA assessment) with supply risk scoring can support balanced decisions that protect both compliance and patient access.

Reprocessing and reworking also require regulatory authority approval before the manufactured product can be released for human use. In limited cases, regulatory authority approval for reprocessing can be acquired ahead of time by presenting a comprehensive change management plan (proactive approach detailed in Section 3.6). This plan should explain the reason for reprocessing, such as refiltration or reconcentration due to technical failures or mechanical breakdown of a chromatography column (1). It must also specify the frequency and nature of the reprocessing procedures for potential events, as well as detail the steps for validation and ensuring product comparability.

1.2 Scope

Original Text

The previous version of TR 74 included only such biopharmaceutical products as recombinant proteins and polypeptides. Because the principles of reprocessing and reworking are applicable across a wider array of manufacturing processes, the scope has been expanded to include guidance on biological products. In addition to recombinant proteins and monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) (both as innovators/novel and biosimilars), biological products (2–4) encompass a wide range of other products such as vaccines and blood-derived proteins.

The quality of biologicals is defined by the chosen production and manufacturing process. Minor changes in the process can affect the quality of the drug product; therefore, the development of the manufacturing process is of paramount importance for biologicals, including the implementation of any reprocessing or reworking. Incorporating the topic of reworking further expands the scope of this technical report, but because the reworking of biologicals is far more challenging than reprocessing, the authors emphasize that extra caution should be taken.

The guidance in this technical report is general in nature, and the case studies illustrate how the general principles may be applied. No attempt is made to encompass all types of unit operations for which reprocessing or reworking could be considered.

This technical report is not intended to establish mandatory standards but serves as a single-source overview that complements existing guidance documents listed in Section 8.0.

The principles discussed in TR 74 are applicable to both clinical and commercial manufacturing processes, but the authors recognize that reprocessing and reworking during the clinical manufacturing of biologicals is often more challenging than during commercial manufacturing. This is primarily because the understanding of the product and the process is very limited, so extra caution is emphasized.

3.0 Reprocessing Considerations — Introduction ：

Original Text

Reprocessing (3, 9, 11–15), or repetition of an existing biopharmaceutical unit operation, requires a well-designed process, supportive data, and validation to augment the existing manufacturing process. Importantly, the supportive data must provide the manufacturer with a high degree of confidence (assurance) that the drug substance or drug product produced by reprocessing meets the predefined quality standards for the drug substance or drug product and is comparable to a nonreprocessed batch. The reprocessed and reworked biological drug substances and sterile drug products should adhere to all CQAs associated (including bioburden and endotoxins) to normal batch disposition and release. Enhanced testing protocols are often required to assess the quality attributes of the reprocessed material, ensuring that it remains consistent with the established manufacturing process.

Reprocessing in pharmaceutical manufacturing can occur under various scenarios that can be broadly categorized as proactive and reactive approaches.

In GMP, a risk-based approach (6), to reprocessing is incorporated by systematically assessing and managing potential risks associated with reactive reprocessing activities. This begins with identifying and evaluating the nature of deviations and their potential impact on product quality and patient safety. By prioritizing areas of greatest concern, manufacturers can focus resources and controls where they are most needed. Decisions regarding reprocessing are made based on these risk assessments, ensuring that only safe and effective practices are implemented.

For any event that requires reprocessing, the manufacturer must perform a formal root-cause investigation of the unit operation excursion if the excursion is not within the design space. Additional development studies may be required to supplement the root-cause investigation. Consideration of any proposed reprocessing by regulators will likely be based on the root-cause analysis and the impact of reprocessing on product quality.

3.1 Proactive Approach

Original Text

In a proactive scenario, the potential need for reprocessing is identified in advance through predetermined criteria. The execution of the reprocessing activities occurs through a predefined concurrent validation protocol with clear acceptance criteria or through previously validated procedures may be reviewed and approved by applicable regulatory agencies. This approach includes:

  1. Predefined Criteria: Manufacturers outline specific conditions or triggers that would necessitate reprocessing, allowing for a structured response to an event that may require reprocessing. Post reprocessing, there should be sufficient data to support a conclusion that any given reprocessed batch of a drug substance or drug product is uniform and meets the defined acceptance criteria.
  2. Validated Procedures: Reprocessing is executed according to a validated procedure that has undergone detailed review by the manufacturer's quality unit. This concurrent validation ensures that the process is capable of consistently producing a product that meets established quality standards. The validation protocol should include the scope of the manufacturing process that is being reprocessed, number of batches required, tests to be performed, and acceptance criteria. The proposed acceptance criteria for all tests should be appropriately justified. The concurrent validation approach should be described within the Validation Master Plan. An example of reprocessing that might be specifically covered by a Biological License Application (BLA) would be repeating viral filtration following a failed filter-integrity test. Because viral filters occasionally fail, manufacturers may be approved for refiltration of the protein drug substance or drug product (following successful validation). The approach to validation should be consistent with a lifecycle approach to validation, as discussed in the U.S. Food and Drug Administration Guidance for Industry: Process Validation: General Principles and Practices and PDA Technical Report No. 60 (Revised 2026) Process Validation: A Lifecycle Approach (10).

When reprocessing has been prospectively validated, it is appropriate to provide the full validation protocol and report in a regulatory submission and to follow the validated process where indicated. When reprocessing is anticipated to undergo concurrent validation, the protocol—inclusive of initial starting conditions and appropriate acceptance criteria to determine whether successful completion of a reprocessing step has been achieved—should be provided.

  1. Regulatory Guidance derived from Established Protocols: An essential prerequisite for the acceptance of a reprocessing step is a clear identification of the root cause. For biological products, these situations are usually restricted to some refiltration, reconcentration steps upon technical failure of equipment, or mechanical breakdown of a chromatography column. These steps should be appropriately described, validated, and included in the regulatory submission. Such documentation should include the description of conditions for which reprocessing could be applied and a demonstration that the reprocessing step(s) does not impact the quality of the drug substance and/or drug product.

The sequence of steps detailed in Section 3.6 provides a roadmap for implementing proactive reprocessing, ensuring that all necessary evaluations and approvals are addressed before action is taken.

3.2 Reactive Approach

Original Text

Reactive reprocessing occurs in response to unforeseen circumstances during the manufacturing process. The same categories of data and decisions required for a proactive reprocessing approach apply to this approach, except that less time and information may be available for performing assessments. As a result, reactive reprocessing is better suited for steps that are simple (such as microbial or virus filtration after failure of filter-integrity testing) and have a low likelihood of affecting product quality. The sequence of steps discussed in Section 3.6 may be used to guide the approach to reactive reprocessing, starting with the design of a reprocessing unit operation (see Section 3.6, Step 6).

Two distinct reactive reprocessing scenarios are described as follows:

  1. Nonprospectively Validated Reprocessing at an In-Process Hold Point
    1. In this scenario, a need arises for a nonprospectively validated reprocessing unit operation while a product is held at an in-process stage. This may involve scenarios such as freezing the product for an extended period.
    2. During the hold period, manufacturers can develop and execute a plan to validate the reprocessing step. This provides assurance that the product meets all the required quality attributes subsequent to the product concurrent release.
  2. Immediate Reprocessing During a Manufacturing Run
    1. This scenario occurs when a reprocessing need emerges unexpectedly during a manufacturing run. In such cases, the reprocessing step may need to be implemented before its validation.
    2. If a rapid design and implementation of the reprocessing option is feasible, it may be executed "at risk." This means that the reprocessing can occur with the understanding that supportive data must be generated and reviewed to ensure product quality before the batch is released to the market.

Of the two reactive reprocessing scenarios described above, the second scenario (#2) carries the highest degree of risk and is likely to be implemented only with relatively simple unit operations that affect a limited number of CQAs, such as dead-end filtration for drug substance or drug product. Release of the reprocessed batch using a reactive scenario should only be performed upon receiving regulatory approval. (See Section 4.0 for strategies on regulatory submissions).

A proactive process design approach that accounts for reprocessing could apply to more complex (such as ultrafiltration, diafiltration, concentration, chromatography, viral removal steps, and polishing) steps with significant benefit to the manufacturer.

In principle, almost any unit operation may be considered for potential reprocessing as long as it is demonstrated to consistently deliver a drug substance or drug product that meets the same specified quality commitments of the standard manufacturing process. Reprocessing simple unit operations is relatively well accepted and understood by regulators and the industry. Reprocessing a sterile drug substance and reprocessing and reworking of sterile-biological batches requires critical attention to maintaining sterility, endotoxin, and particulate controls. Reprocessing of nonsterile drug substances manufactured in low bioburden facilities also requires special attention to be paid to microbial and particulate controls.

3.3 Reprocessing Frequency

Original Text

A manufacturing process that requires frequent reprocessing is considered to be poorly controlled and not in a validated state. While there is no formal definition of "frequent," it would be reasonable to question the state-of-control for a unit operation that requires reprocessing as often as 10% of the time (16). (Ten percent is given here as an illustrative example and is not intended to be a prescriptive limit.)

It is important to distinguish between two types of reprocessing frequency:

  • Number of reprocessed unit operations within a batch
  • Number of batches that require reprocessing over a defined time period

Given that the Annual Product Review/Product Quality Review provides a one-year performance summary, it may be appropriate to evaluate reprocessing trends within that annual timeframe. Under circumstances where reprocessing becomes recurring or trends upward, a reassessment of the process design should be strongly considered. If reprocessing is carried out frequently or reaches a specified percentage of manufactured batches, it must be considered part of the normal process, described as such in the application, and receives approval.

3.3.1 Reprocessing Frequency per Batch

The frequency of reprocessing for the same batch at commercial-scale reprocessing should be restricted to a maximum number of reprocessing events according to process development data for small-scale reprocessing.

Validation of the reprocessing step should be supported by a scientifically justified number of demonstration runs. While no fixed minimum is mandated by regulatory agencies, performing at least two independent runs is common practice to demonstrate reproducibility. The exact number should be based on process complexity, risk assessment, and historical data to ensure the process consistently achieves predefined acceptance criteria.

The reprocessing frequency is established by the manufacturer based on appropriate studies at either small scale or same scale. Small-scale validation studies are commonly used to establish frequency for a reprocessing event for a particular unit operation. These studies may be conducted before implementation of a reprocessing approach at manufacturing scale. In a reprocessing event, product quality can change after repeated run exposures during filtration or chromatography processing steps and may impact the stability of long-term storage. There is no rule-of-thumb for frequency determination; the manufacturer must establish it for each project based on its impact on quality attributes. For example, a simple dead-end filtration, typically a maximum of three reprocessings, can be included in a small-scale reprocessing study. Some protein molecules may not be stable for a second reprocessing; in that case, maximum reprocessing may be restricted to only one reprocessing event.

These studies are used to obtain data on product quality attributes (e.g., aggregation) resulting from exposure to physical/shear stress during repeated unit operations. Small-scale validation-study design should be predictive of the commercial process. This will ensure that the data generated is relevant to large-scale. Small-scale reprocessing studies must use the same protein solution for the repeated number of runs and the samples to be withdrawn after each run. Product quality attributes of each run should be compared against the initial and intermediate feed-material to understand the impact of multiple exposures and to determine the frequency for the large-scale reprocessing event. All reprocessing events, including repeated runs, need to be completed within the allowed chemical and microbial hold time for intermediate pools or scientifically justified if there is any excursion from validated hold time.

When developing a proactive reprocessing step, a review of the development data and history may identify process steps with inherent variability that may, on rare occasions, lead to a need for reprocessing (e.g., failing in-process control (IPC) assay results, filter integrity failure, difficult separation step). The variability must be scientifically understood.

3.4 Continuation vs. Reprocessing (Repetition) of a Unit Operation vs.

Original Text

Most unit operations include some sort of in-process control measurement to determine whether the step meets the objective (e.g., a concentration, pH). An adjustment to bring a product within acceptable limits is not considered reprocessing and, instead, should be designed as a process "continuation." Procedures should account for continuation to perform an adjustment within defined limits. Without appropriate instructions, continuations in processing may be erroneously considered as reprocessing and lead to procedural deviations.

For example, during a step in the ultrafiltration-diafiltration operation, concentration is measured at different volumes to check for the desired target concentration. If the concentration does not meet the target specification, the process can be continued until the target specification is met, and it is considered a continuation of the process, not reprocessing. Similarly, in the titration process, the addition of titrant to achieve the desired pH or conductivity set point may be considered a continuation of the process. Likewise, adding a buffer dilution to achieve the target product concentration is considered a continuation of the process, not a reprocessing activity.

In the case of drug product manufacturing, when large-volume containers are thawed and only a partial amount is used for filling, the remaining formulated drug substance may need to be refrozen for subsequent use. This practice may be considered reprocessing if not included in an approved application. Supporting freeze-thaw stability data from reduced- or at-scale studies can help justify the impact on drug product quality.

3.5 Quality Concerns During the Reprocessing of Biologicals

Original Text

Protein aggregation is a key concern during manufacturing and quality assessment, particularly in the context of reprocessing. Most therapeutic proteins are only marginally stable in solutions and are susceptible to chemical instabilities (e.g., deamidation, oxidation, hydrolysis) as well as physical instabilities (e.g., denaturation and noncovalent aggregation).

During reprocessing, proteins may be exposed to various stress conditions. Process controls play a critical role in mitigating the effects of destabilizing factors, such as pH, buffer components, and denaturants, that can induce protein unfolding and subsequent aggregation.

Several stress factors during reprocessing can contribute to protein aggregation and instability. Key considerations include:

  • Increased Viscosity During Reprocessing: Higher protein concentration can increase viscosity, affecting subcutaneous formulation. Evaluate using rheology or dynamic light-scattering.
  • Thermal Stress: Elevated temperatures (e.g., during viral inactivation) can promote protein denaturation and aggregation. Effects are difficult to quantify and should be controlled.
  • High Ionic Strength: Use of high ionic strength eluents (e.g., in ion exchange or hydrophobic interaction chromatography) may induce aggregation.
  • pH Sensitivity: Eluents with pH near or far from the protein's isoelectric point can promote aggregation.
  • Filtration Stress: Filtration processes may lead to aggregation due to membrane contact, air bubbles, or shear stress. Ultrafiltration at high protein concentrations adds risk, especially before filling or during buffer mixing.
  • Formulation Handling: Aggregation can be triggered by agitation, freeze-thaw cycles, and prolonged storage of bulk solutions before formulation.
  • Air-Water Interfaces: Exposure to air-water interfaces during formulation can reduce protein stability and initiate aggregation pathways. Visual inspection may be required.

Note: For factors affecting reworking, refer to Section 5.9.

Failure to manage these factors may result in out-of-specification (OOS) drug substance or drug product, visible particulates, reduced storage stability, loss of efficacy, and potentially increased immunogenicity risk (17, 18). Therefore, before performing any reprocessing, evaluating and understanding the potential impact on product quality is essential. If a reactive reprocessing strategy is used, the sampling plan should include specific analytical tests to demonstrate that reprocessing does not adversely affect the quality, safety, or efficacy of the drug substance or drug product.

"Note: For reprocessing, product release may occur in parallel with ongoing stability studies, since the process remains within the validated manufacturing design space. For reworking, stability data must be available prior to release to define retesting intervals, as the introduction of new steps is equivalent to a modified synthesis with potentially higher risk (see Section 5.0)."

Original Text

A general outline for designing, validating, and filing a reprocessing procedure with the regulators using a proactive approach is provided in this section. Figure 3.6-1 provides an example of a generic sequence of activities that could support a proactive reprocessing approach. Steps 1–20 provide an illustration of reprocessing a biopharmaceutical product.

Section 6.0 (Appendix I) provides a comprehensive example of an approach to reprocessing using a hypothetical chromatography step that was created for TR 74 based on the publication A-Mab: A Case Study in Bioprocess Development (19).

In the ideal situation, a manufacturer would plan for reprocessing during development prior to licensure of the product. However, it may also be possible to prospectively define a new step using supplementary submissions created for reprocessing in manufacturing.

The following 20 steps are presented as recommended considerations for evaluating and implementing a reprocessing strategy for a biopharmaceutical product. While the steps are written predicated on a product that has already received marketing approval, many of the key concepts are equally applicable during the development of reprocessing step(s) for a new biopharmaceutical product. Importantly, these steps are not intended to prescribe a fixed sequence but may be adapted to align with the company's quality system, regulatory commitments, and the stage of the product lifecycle.

Step 1: Identifying and Evaluating Reprocessing Need
The need to reprocess may arise when a biological intermediate/drug substance/product does not conform to specifications or acceptable ranges. Perform an evaluation to narrow the field of feasible and practical reprocessing steps. Consider that any reprocessing step candidate should be based on simplicity of the step to operate in manufacturing, and past failures. Conduct a technical risk assessment (e.g., FMEA) that includes review of related past manufacturing or development issues that may be helpful in identifying a potential need for reprocessing. Also, consider the effect on drug supply and whether reprocessing is needed to prevent a drug shortage, especially if it is a breakthrough product or an orphan drug. Ensure that further development of a prospective reprocessing step would result in an acceptably low regulatory or quality risk.

Step 2: Establish a Project Charter (Optional)
Establish a project team and team charter if the evaluation determines the risks to further develop the prospective reprocessing step(s) are acceptable. Development of a reprocessing step involves many stakeholders and may require numerous, varying resources and skillsets including those from Research and Development, Process/Analytical Development, Manufacturing, MSAT/Process Science, Engineering, Quality Control, Quality Assurance, Regulatory Affairs, and Project Management groups.

Step 3: Review Development History
Review the design basis and development history for the existing unit operation to understand the designed purpose of the proposed reprocessing steps, especially the control of CQAs. Leverage knowledge obtained during the original development of the unit operation to ensure that reprocessing will address the designed purpose of that step to consistently control specific product quality aspects (CQAs) (8, 9). Perform a systematic assessment to identify which development studies (e.g., viral/impurity clearance, process characterization, resin/membrane cleaning) should be repeated or augmented to support reprocessing. Evaluate the assessment for any potential impact of reprocessing to subsequent processing steps.

Step 4: Identify Reprocessing Triggers
Identify the in-process testing or monitoring triggers that would indicate a need for reprocessing at a given unit operation. It may be necessary to introduce a new in-process test(s) to detect failure modes identified in the initial reprocessing risk assessment. The detection method for a failure mode that leads to reprocessing is critical to the reliability of the overall process and to the success of a reprocessing step. The method must be rapid enough to allow for timely decision-making before the opportunity to reprocess has passed. For example, integrity testing of a 0.2 µm filter would be a better detection method to support reprocessing because it can be completed in a few minutes or hours compared to microbial testing, which can take several days to complete.

Step 5: Design Reprocessing Procedure
Begin the initial design with the proposed manufacturing-scale reprocessing procedure including all equipment, process parameters, linkages to adjacent unit operations, pre-use/post-use procedures (e.g., equilibration/cleaning), and process controls. Give special attention to any procedure required to return the process intermediate and equipment to an appropriate starting condition so that the reprocessing step can be performed. This restoration step is designed to replicate the intermediate input feed-stream conditions (e.g., concentration, volume, pH, conductivity) and is not considered to be a rework step.

Step 6: Design Assessment
Consider the broader effect of the reprocessing design outside the scope of its specific unit operation; for example, facility design, pre-/post-viral segregation, linkages to adjacent unit operations, draw on utilities, and scheduling.

Step 7: Perform Quality Attribute Impact Assessment
Perform a risk assessment (6), to identify all quality attributes that may be affected by the proposed reprocessing step. In particular, consider such issues as modes of degradation, CPPs, exposure to product-contact surfaces, leachables from filters/single-use tubing, and adsorptive losses that may be impacted by an additional processing step, intermediate hold point, or freeze-thaw cycle. It is not expected that any new CQAs will be identified, but CQAs may take on a higher or lower overall risk following reprocessing (8, 9, 20, 21). Consider whether the stability of the reprocessed batch should be formally evaluated, especially upon the first instance of reprocessing.

Step 8: Reassess Criticality
Review existing criticality assessments conducted for process parameters, raw materials, equipment, in-process controls, and other factors based on the standard process to determine whether any changes or additions are required. The criticality may be influenced by the addition of a reprocessing step.

Step 9: Identify or Develop Analytical Methods
Identify or develop the analytical methods to monitor all relevant CQAs in intermediate feed-streams and/or drug substance/product. Even though no new CQAs are likely to be identified, additional analytical methods capable of monitoring all potentially impacted CQAs in the relevant matrix may be needed. Additional development and qualification may be needed to adapt methods to the reprocessed product. The risk assessment (Step 7) should determine whether additional characterization methods are required to establish comparability and acceptable product quality.

Step 10: Establish Comparability Criteria
Establish appropriate comparability criteria for all CQAs. Once CQAs and their corresponding analytical methods are identified, it may be possible to develop the key comparability criteria to assess product quality during the development of the reprocessing unit operation. All of the original drug substance or drug product specifications must continue to be met following reprocessing. Verify that product quality at intermediate points in the process meets original expectations or consider new criteria as appropriate.

Step 11: Design Scaled-Down Model
Design and qualify a scaled-down model, if applicable. To generate additional data, a scaled-down unit operation model can be adapted from the previously qualified model of the standard process. The requirements for a laboratory-scale model of a reprocessing step are the same as those used in the development of a standard manufacturing process (i.e., design a scaled-down model that is representative of the manufacturing-scale reprocessing unit operation).

Step 12: Obtain Representative Materials
Obtain representative materials for performing reprocessing at the reduced scale. If representative intermediate feed-streams from manufacturing cannot be obtained, then it may be necessary to generate the materials using a qualified scaled-down model of all, or a section of, the manufacturing-scale process. It should also be possible to recreate the failure mode in the feed-stream in a representative manner (e.g., spiking with impurities). If the failure mode is based on operational checks, such as integrity tests, rather than a product-quality excursion, then use of a standard intermediate feed-stream may be justified.

Step 13: Execute Developmental Studies
Plan and execute reduced-scale development studies, if required once the process design elements described above in Steps 4–12 are completed. The design of reprocessing development studies (e.g., using a design-of-experiments – methodology or worst-case conditions) should result in an understanding of robustness across appropriate critical and noncritical process parameter ranges. Evaluate any effects of the reprocessing step on quality attributes using the selected analytical methods. The design and scope of these studies should take into consideration all elements of the original development studies related to the unit operation, including intermediate and drug substance/product stability. Determine which prior development studies should be repeated and what additional studies (e.g., testing for new degradants) are needed.

Step 14: Assess Development Data
Upon completion of the development studies, review the generated data to evaluate whether the scaled-down process studies indicate the following:

  • All predetermined comparability criteria are met by the reprocessing procedure.
  • Process parameter operating ranges are confirmed.
  • Process intermediate, drug substance, and product stability are potentially impacted.

Step 15: Finalize Designs
Adapt GMP manufacturing-scale reprocessing procedures and equipment based upon the results of scaled-down studies. For example, if the development studies described in Step 13 indicate that process parameter ranges must be narrowed or shifted to ensure that all quality and comparability criteria are met, then it may be necessary to modify the manufacturing-scale process design and control strategy to reflect this new process understanding.

Step 16: Transfer Technology to Manufacturing
Execute technology transfer according to company policies and procedures. Transfer of the reprocessing step to manufacturing should consider scale-up issues and implementation of associated GMP procedures. For more complex reprocessing activities, an intermediate pilot-scale process or non-GMP "engineering run" may also be beneficial to ensure a successful transfer into the GMP manufacturing environment (see Section 3.8 for more information on contamination control strategies).

Step 17: Validation, Comparability and Stability Protocols
Write formal prospective process validation, comparability, and stability protocols for manufacturing-scale reprocessing. Validation considerations include the number of lots of resin or other raw materials required, the number of runs needed, and the additional nonroutine sampling and testing, beginning with consideration of the original validation of the standard process. Additional validation and comparability run(s) at manufacturing scale may be supported by the reduced-scale data generated during development. Real-time stability studies are considered to be a holistic test of recombinant proteins and, therefore, are often used to document that no changes to the protein have occurred. After validation of the reprocessed unit operation and subsequent license approval, it may not be necessary for reprocessed lots to undergo further stability testing.

Step 18: Regulatory Submission
For approval of the reprocessing step, include the supportive scaled-down development data and prospective validation/comparability/stability protocols in the submission (3, 8, 9). If using the proactive approach, file these elements in the initial marketing application:

  • Output of the quality attribute impact assessment (Step 7), criticality reassessment (Step 8), and results of development studies (Step 13) in Module 3.2.S.2.6
  • Manufacturing-scale reprocessing protocols in Module 3.2.S.2.5, including triggers, tests/acceptance criteria, and stability commitments
  • Any other agency commitments or strategies for implementation
  • Description of the validated reprocessing step in Module 3.2.S.2.2

Step 19: Monitor and Investigate Triggering Event
Monitor failure modes for signs that the manufacturing process might need reprocessing or, alternatively, decide to proactively carry out reprocessing to collect data for a submission. Regardless of whether the reprocessing is prospectively designed, it is essential to thoroughly investigate the root cause of any triggering event and its impact on product quality following GMP procedures and the principles of ICH Q9(R1): Quality Risk Management (6). The investigation outcome, along with CAPAs, should be considered in relation to process validation, regulatory commitments (Step 20), and reprocessing design (Steps 5 and 6) (23).

Step 20: Validate and Release
For each reprocessing batch, follow established GMP procedures for executing validation, comparability, and stability protocols, and ensuring all acceptance criteria, including release-testing, are met. Document validation and comparability results in a final report, completing change control and regulatory commitments before releasing the reprocessed batch.

3.8 Impact of Contamination Control Strategy during Reprocessing

Original Text

As discussed above, it is critical to ensure microbial controls are considered and protected during reprocessing events. The reprocessed biological drug substances and sterile drug products should adhere to all CQAs associated with bioburden and endotoxins.

During the reprocessing of a sterile biological drug substance and drug product, European Union (EU) Annex 1 (20), provides general guidance that should be used in the design and control of facilities, equipment, systems, and procedures used for the manufacture of all sterile products. The principles of quality risk management (QRM) should be applied to ensure that microbial, particulate, and endotoxin/pyrogen contamination is prevented in the final drug substance and drug products. QRM is also applicable to low-bioburden biological intermediates and drug substances (20).

Critical aspects considered in QRM include, but are not limited to:

  • Process step where reprocessing occurs
  • Time of product exposure (open operations)
  • Air classification of the reprocessing work zone (where the product is exposed in absence of closed system)
  • Particulate counts and bioburden trends
  • Training records of personnel carrying out reprocessing
  • Sanitization and disinfection procedures
  • Material movement and area segregation controls (e.g. CCS impact, pre/post viral areas)
  • Hold times during reprocessing
  • Line clearance before and after reprocessing
  • Cleaning and/or change over limits, in cases of multiproduct facilities
  • Filter validation studies (critical on operating ranges while handling concentrated protein solutions)
  • Sampling techniques and number of samples taken for studies

The Quality Control Unit should establish a more comprehensive monitoring program for operators involved in reprocessing operations, which are especially labor-intensive and require complex manipulations and time criticality.

Processes, equipment, facilities, and manufacturing activities should be managed in accordance with QRM principles to provide a proactive means of identifying, scientifically evaluating, and controlling potential risks to quality. Where alternative approaches are used, these should be supported by appropriate rationale, risk assessment, and mitigation and should adhere to principles of CCS. A combination of QRM and a contamination control strategy (CCS) should be established for a robust assurance of contamination prevention during reprocessing. The CCS should be actively reviewed and, where appropriate, updated, and it should drive continual improvement of the manufacturing and control methods. (See PDA Technical Report No. 90: Contamination Control Strategy Development in Pharmaceutical Manufacturing for further details (21, 24), Contamination Control Strategies (CCS) in Low Bioburden Biologic Drug Substance Manufacturing (25), and Contamination Control Strategy Guidance (26).)

4.3 Specific Guidance from the United States (FDA) FDA

Original Text

In addition to implementation of the ICH Guidelines, the FDA provides general guidance for biologicals during the clinical investigation period and specific guidance for biologicals during the post-market approval period.

4.3.1 During Clinical Investigation Period

In 2008, FDA published Guidance for Industry: CGMP for Phase 1 Investigational Drugs that stressed the importance of documenting how the manufacturing process was carried out during the clinical investigational period:

  • A record of manufacturing data that details the materials, equipment, procedures used, and any problems encountered during manufacturing. Manufacturers should retain records sufficient to replicate the manufacturing process.
  • A record of changes in procedures and processes used for subsequent batches along with the rationale for any changes.

Since reprocessing and/or reworking are significant changes, they are required to be reported to the FDA under an information amendment to the Investigational New Drug (IND) Application on file to carry out the human clinical trials (21 CFR 312.31). Although there is no required time period of delay of implementation for these information amendments, manufacturers typically wait for a period of time to allow the FDA reviewers opportunity to review the amendment.

Note: "Consider the impact of changes to be discussed with regulatory authorities in order to determine the need for additional toxicology studies."

FDA in 2020 published Guidance for Industry: CMC Information for Human Gene Therapy Investigational New Drug Applications (INDs). While there is no mention of reworking, the following guidance for reprocessing applies:

  • Drug Substance: For early stage INDs, there may be differences between manufacturing of toxicology lots and clinical study material. For later stage INDs, process changes or reprocessing step(s) may exist. Describe how manufacturing differences are expected to impact product safety and activity; provide batch analysis in CTD Section 3.2.S.4.4.
  • Drug Product: Under certain conditions, repeating sterile filtration (if applicable) may be critical. Clearly describe the conditions and justifications for each reprocessing procedure and demonstrate product consistency between reprocessed lots and normal production lots.

4.3.2 Post-Market Approval Period

Under 21 CFR 601.12, each post-approval change in the production process established in the approved BLA must be reported using the submission type associated with one of the three tier-based reporting categories. This depends on the potential (i.e., risk) of the change to have an adverse effect on the identity, strength, quality, purity, or potency of the product. The submission types are:

  • Prior Approval Supplement (PAS): Change that has a substantial potential to have an adverse effect on product quality (major changes). A PAS must be approved by FDA prior to distribution of the product manufactured using the change (21 CFR 601.12(b)).
  • Changes Being Effected in 30 Days / CBE (CBE-30/CBE): Changes that have a moderate potential to have an adverse effect on product quality. Report at least 30 days prior to distribution (21 CFR 601.12(c)).
  • Annual Report (AR): Changes that have a minimal potential to have an adverse effect. Documented by the applicant in an annual report (21 CFR 601.12(d)).

In 1997, FDA published Guidance for Industry: Changes to an Approved Application for Specified Biotechnology and Specified Synthetic Biological Products. Key provision: reprocessing without a previously approved reprocessing protocol is considered a change with substantial potential to adversely affect product quality, safety, or effectiveness — requiring a PAS.

In 2021, FDA published Guidance for Industry: Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls Changes to an Approved Application: Certain Biological Products (scope includes vaccines, plasma-derived protein products, cellular and gene therapy products). 5.0 Introduction to Reworking

Original Text

It is the primary responsibility of the Manufacturing Authorization Holder (MAH) of a biological drug substance and/or drug product to consistently meet all quality, safety, purity, and identity requirements. This authoring team has strict recommendations on how and when such a reworking action should be considered by the MAH. The ultimate approval of a reworked drug substance and/or drug product for human use is solely at the discretion of the approving regulatory agency.

ICH and EU define reworking as:

"Subjecting an intermediate or API that does not conform to standards or specifications to one or more processing steps that are different from the established manufacturing process to obtain acceptable quality intermediate or API." (ICH Q7; EU GMP)

During reworking, the nonconforming material is subjected to a different manufacturing process to get a product that meets all the predefined acceptance criteria and specifications for quality, safety, purity, and identity (QSIP).

The scope of reworking for biologicals includes a diverse array of therapeutic proteins, each with unique challenges and considerations for manufacturing and quality control including, among others:

  • Interferons
  • Cytokines
  • Growth factors
  • Interleukins
  • Coagulation factors
  • Blood factors
  • Monoclonal antibodies (IgM, IgD, IgG, IgE, IgA)
  • Bispecific antibodies (IgG-based bsAbs, fragment-based bsAbs)
  • Antibody drug conjugates targeting cancer therapy
  • Fusion proteins
  • Glycoprotein hormones (e.g., rhCG, rFSH)
  • Insulins
  • Vaccines

Each of these therapeutic categories requires specific attention to factors like protein stability, aggregation prevention, and purification processes to ensure product purity, quality, efficacy, and safety.

Due to the intricate sourcing, including the nature of their manufacturing processes and stability concerns, cell and gene therapies are excluded from the scope for reworking. The complexity of these treatments often involves different considerations that require specialized processes and controls distinct from those applicable to the protein-based biologicals listed above.

5.1 Prioritization Tool for Reworking

Original Text

Using the MoSCoW (Must-have, Should-have, Could-have, and Won't-have, or will not have right now) Prioritization Tool to evaluate reworking of a biological drug substance involves systematically categorizing tasks or issues based on their criticality and impact.

This step-by-step guide outlines how to apply the MoSCoW method in this context, and Figure 5.1-1 illustrates the Patient-Centric Reworking Prioritization tool.

1. Gather Relevant Data and Stakeholders

  • Collect all information related to the reworking process, including technical assessments, quality data, regulatory considerations, and risk analyses.
  • Assemble a cross-functional team comprising manufacturing, quality assurance, regulatory affairs, and process development experts.

2. Define the Scope of Reworking

  • Clearly specify which aspects of the biological drug substance are being considered for rework (e.g., purification, formulation, in-process controls, final product adjustments).

3. Identify Rework Tasks or Issues

  • List all potential rework activities, corrections, or modifications needed to meet quality and regulatory standards.

4. Apply the MoSCoW Prioritization Criteria
Categorize each task or issue into one of the following groups:

  • Must have (M): Critical for human survival, compliance, safety, efficacy, or regulatory approval. These are nonnegotiable and should be completed before proceeding.
  • Should have (S): Immediate medical supply need; can be scheduled after Must-Have items.
  • Could have (C): Desirable to support patient needs, especially those suffering with genetic disorders, cancer, glaucoma, blood issues, and wherever critical to support health condition of work force.
  • Won't have (W): Tasks that are not necessary in the current scope or can be deferred.

5. Prioritize Reworking Activities

  • Focus first on Must-Have tasks to ensure the reworked product meets all quality and regulatory standards.
  • Schedule Should-Have activities shortly afterwards, resources permitting.
  • Consider Could-Have tasks, if time and resources allow, to enhance overall process robustness.
  • Leave Won't-Have tasks for future considerations or continuous improvement.

6. Document and Communicate

  • Record the prioritization outcomes clearly, including rationales for each category.
  • Communicate the priorities to all relevant teams and stakeholders to ensure aligned execution.

7. Implement and Monitor

  • Proceed with rework activities according to the established priorities.
  • Monitor progress and adjust priorities if new information or issues arise.

8. Additional Measures

  • Ensure compliance with GMP and regulatory requirements throughout the rework process.
  • Use risk assessments to support the categorization, especially for CQAs.

5.3 Background to Manufacturing Changes

Original Text

Reworking is an unexpected occurrence and is not preapproved as part of the marketing authorization. The MAH's Quality Unit should oversee investigations into a nonconforming (OOS) in-process drug, a bulk process intermediate (final biological bulk intermediate), a drug substance of a single batch or lot to an alternate manufacturing process during reworking decisions, prior to release to ensure that the details of rework in question has been submitted to the regulatory agency and formal approval has been obtained.

Changes that have a substantial potential to have an adverse effect on product quality (i.e., major variations) require the manufacturer (applicant) to report the change to the FDA in a supplement to the approved BLA. A PAS (Prior Approval Supplement) must be approved by the FDA prior to distribution of the product manufactured using the change to protect the public from products whose identity, strength, quality, purity, potency, safety, or effectiveness may be compromised (10, 33, 46).

If biological drug products are reworked, they must be reported in a supplement to FDA prior to distribution, unless the reworking was done according to a procedure previously approved by the Agency. The type of notification is based on the potential risk of the change having an adverse effect on the identity, strength, quality, purity, or potency of the product as it may relate to the safety or effectiveness of the product (24).

The Variation Regulation is supplemented by the European Commission's Guidelines on the Details of the Various Categories of Variations, on the Operation of the Procedures Laid Down in Chapters II, IIa, III and IV of Commission Regulation No 1234/2008 of 24 November 2008. The EU guidelines define a major variation of Type II as one that is not an extension of the Marketing Authorization and that may have a significant impact on the quality, safety, or efficacy of a medicinal product (37).

5.5 Application of Knowledge and Risk Assessments

Original Text

ICH Quality Guideline Q10: Pharmaceutical Quality System was the first official global guideline known to firmly position QRM (Quality Risk Management) and knowledge management (KM) as important enablers of an effective pharmaceutical quality system (PQS). Specifically, QRM and KM jointly enable (i) achieving product realization, (ii) establishing and maintaining a state of control, (iii) facilitating continual improvement, and (iv) critical decision-making during the reworking strategy (57).

ICH Q9(R1) emphasizes the relationship between risk and knowledge, and it highlights the importance of KM in managing risks. It places knowledge and KM repeatedly and explicitly at the core of all QRM activities, further reinforcing the connection of scientific knowledge and risk in several other regulatory guidance documents. Specifically, the definition of risk-based decision-making reads:

"An approach to, or a process of, making decisions that considers knowledge about risks relevant to the decision and whether risks are at an acceptable level." (ICH Q9(R1))

The Risk Knowledge Infinity Cycle (RKI Cycle) establishes and promotes the following concepts:

  • Knowledge is both an input to and an output from QRM
  • Knowledge has an inverse relationship with risk
  • Knowledge should flow effortlessly and on demand to inform risk and risk-based decisions
  • The Cycle is continuous and perpetual

The RKI Cycle can be applied to support the goals of the PQS as defined in ICH Q10. A set of six principles corresponding with six "nodes" along the Cycle has been developed to better guide the tangible connection between QRM and KM practices as illustrated in Figure 5.5-1 (58).

During the reworking of biologicals, rigorous risk management ensures that any changes or corrective actions do not compromise critical quality attributes, safety, or regulatory compliance. Structured assessments (e.g., FMEA within a quality risk management framework) prioritize controls and justify decisions, while robust change control, deviation tracking, and CAPA link risks to measurable outcomes. This enables cross-functional review and consistent, evidence-based decisions that reduce variability and prevent repeat issues.

Knowledge

  1.   2. External knowledge comes from public databases, literature surveys, journals, conferences, peer-reviewed publications and monographs.
  3. Knowledge application of scientific principles from biophysical sciences, pharmaceutical sciences, biochemical engineering, material science, and application of principles of engineering for evaluation of the structure-activity relationship of the molecule under investigation.

ICH Q9(R1) defines two primary principles of QRM:

  1. The evaluation of the risk to quality should be based on scientific knowledge and ultimately linked to the protection of the patient.
  2. The level of effort, formality, and documentation of the QRM process should be commensurate with the level of risk.

5.7 Established Conditions

Original Text

The implementation of ICH Quality Guideline Q12: Technical and Regulatory Considerations for Pharmaceutical Product Lifecycle Management by different regulatory agencies will also enable the recognition of Established Conditions (ECs) that require regulatory notification as well as those non-ECs that have been recognized as not necessary to ensure product quality and, therefore, can be managed prior to implementation notification. The amount of flexibility in a control strategy is based on the totality of product and process understanding (e.g., prior knowledge, development studies) in the context of QRM principles described in ICH Q9(R1).

A control strategy is designed to ensure that a product of required quality will be produced consistently. This planned set of controls, derived from current product and process understanding, assures process performance and product quality. The controls can include parameters and attributes related to drug substance and drug product materials and components, capability of the verified mathematical models, facility and equipment operating conditions, in-process controls, finished product specifications, and the associated methods and frequency of monitoring and control. The control strategy is based on the development of the CQAs which, in turn, have been based on thorough scientific work.

Pharmaceutical development activities result in an appropriate control strategy of the ECs, which are critical elements identified and proposed by the MAH to assure product quality and, therefore, would require a regulatory submission if changes impacted any EC. ECs can include CPPs, critical material attributes (CMAs), and CQAs as defined in ICH Q8(R2).

In the event of reworking of a drug substance or drug product, CMC changes can occur. Thus, the ECs for the manufacturing process should be defined based on enhanced product and process understanding, considering all the relevant elements of the control strategy. To identify the ECs, an enhanced approach is recommended with an increased understanding of interaction between the changed key and critical input parameters along with the outputs, and the impact on quality attributes together with a corresponding control strategy. Once ECs are identified for the material to be reworked, a detailed initial risk assessment must be developed from the prior knowledge gathered to identify the potential risk to product quality associated with changing the ECs and considering the overall control strategy.

5.8 Post-Approval Change Management Protocols (PACMP)

Original Text

The PACMP tools described in ICH Q12 enable the planning and implementation of changes to ECs and/or CPPs, and thereby to regulatory filings, in an efficient structured manner. The PACMP (or comparability protocol) approved by the relevant health authority provides an agreement between the MAH and the regulatory agency and describes the changes and level of detail complying to the complexity, major variation, risk assessment, control strategy, and other pertinent elements. Once a PACMP is approved, when the proposed study design or acceptance criteria changes, an update of the protocol should be submitted for implementation as agreed upon in the original protocol. If changes are needed due to unanticipated or unforeseen changes in plans, prior approval of a new or revised PACMP by the regulatory agency may be required.

In the event of a reworking, the proposed comparability protocol (CP) for a modified manufacturing process should sufficiently cover the intended change and provide an increased control when compared to the existing process. A CP can be appropriately designed and/or drafted using a risk-based approach, with an in-depth or a high-level knowledge of process and product understanding. A risk assessment should be conducted on the potential drug substance and/or intermediate CQAs to be affected by parameter changes, including unspecified parameters.

Knowledge from previous developmental studies, small-scale studies, and/or pilot studies can be critical to identify potential risks to quality and provide support in the development of a suitable evaluation plan. The risk of adverse effects as a result of cumulative changes during a reworking should be carefully identified, justified, and included in the supporting information for the CP.

The reworked material should be assessed with suitable structural characterization, analytical and procedures. It should meet the criteria to demonstrate the lack of adverse effect, including immunogenicity on the reworked product quality considering the impact of structural changes in drug substance and/or drug product. Depending on the type and complexity of the drug substance, functional characterization and additional studies should also be included.

5.9 Instabilities and Quality Concerns

Original Text

Protein degradation, aggregation, poses significant challenges in the manufacturing and quality control of biologicals, particularly during reworking processes. Given that, generally, these proteins are only marginally stable in solution, they are susceptible to various forms of instability. Chemical instabilities like deamidation, oxidation, and hydrolysis, alongside such physical instabilities as denaturation and noncovalent aggregation, can compromise the quality and efficacy of the drug substances.

During an event of reworking, proteins will be exposed to stress conditions, such as elevated temperatures, low pH, or hydrophobic surfaces, which might trigger conformational instabilities. Formulation of drug substances at high concentrations can further encourage protein aggregation, impacting long-term stability.

To mitigate these risks, purification steps are essential for removing impurities and aggregates. It is crucial to implement appropriate process controls that manage pH, buffer components, and denaturants to prevent protein unfolding, which can lead to aggregation. These controls are vital to ensuring the drug substance meet specifications, maintains good appearance without visible particulates, retains stability during holding and storage, preserves efficacy, and minimizes immunogenicity concerns.

Protein manufacturing and formulation are complex processes with several factors that can impact the quality and stability of therapeutic proteins, such as:

  • Protein Concentration and Viscosity: During reworking, increased protein concentration can lead to higher viscosity, which is problematic for formulations intended for subcutaneous injections. Techniques such as rheology and dynamic light-scattering can be employed to evaluate viscosity impacts.
  • Thermal Stress: Elevated temperatures, particularly during viral inactivation steps, can lead to protein denaturation and aggregation.
  • High Ionic Strength: The use of high-ionic-strength eluents in ion-exchange and hydrophobic interaction chromatography may promote aggregation.
  • pH Considerations: Eluents with pH values near the protein's isoelectric point, or far from it, can lead to aggregation.
  • Filtration Processes: Membrane contact, presence of air bubbles, and shear stress during filtration can induce aggregation. Ultrafiltration at high protein concentrations requires careful management.
  • Formulation Challenges: Bulk storage, freeze-thaw cycles, and agitation during formulation processes can stress proteins and cause aggregation.
  • Air-Water Interfaces: Exposure to air-water interfaces during formulation can promote aggregate formation by lowering the energy barrier for protein unfolding.
  • Excipients in Formulation: Certain excipients can stress proteins and accelerate aggregation. For instance, magnesium ions (Mg²⁺) have been noted for their potential to increase aggregation rates in some formulations.
  • Visible Particulates: It is critical to effectively monitor visible particulates after any reworking.
  • Leachables and Extractables: Detected in various sterilizing-grade filters (PVDF, PES, mixed cellulose ester). These demonstrate complex patterns of destabilizing and stabilizing effects on the physical and chemical stability of mAbs, including formation of visible and subvisible particles.

5.10 Roles and Communication

Original Text

People drive reworking projects and ensure timely delivery of the biological drug product to the patient. Top management plays a critical role by setting the strategic direction and ensuring that all processes adhere to regulatory standards. Meanwhile, other team members can focus on operational aspects, ensuring each function, whether it is research, development, manufacturing, quality, logistics, or support, is executed efficiently.

5.10.1 Roles

Roles can be defined individually or integrated into teams, which allows for flexibility in management and execution, ultimately contributing to successful outcomes for the project and timely delivery to the patient.

Their responsibilities include:

  • Providing Strategic Direction and Oversight: Approving the rework plan, scope, and objectives; ensuring alignment with organizational goals, regulatory requirements, and quality standards.
  • Ensuring Regulatory Compliance and Risk Management: Supporting adherence to GMP, regulatory guidelines, and internal SOPs; overseeing risk assessments and mitigation strategies.
  • Allocating Resources and Budget: Approving necessary budgets, personnel, facilities, and equipment; ensuring sufficient resources for effective execution.
  • Facilitating Cross-Functional Collaboration: Promoting communication among manufacturing, QA, regulatory, and other departments. Departments should explicitly include contract manufacturers responsible for intermediates and supply chain/demand planning teams, and manufacturing resilience given their impact on production scheduling, inventory, and communication with commercial functions.
  • Monitoring Progress and Performance: Receiving regular updates on status, challenges, and outcomes; making high-level decisions to address issues or deviations promptly.
  • Ensuring Regulatory Submissions and Documentation: Approving documentation for regulatory filings, deviations, or change controls associated with reworking; ensuring thorough documentation for compliance and traceability.
  • Managing Ethical and Safety Considerations: Prioritizing patient safety and product quality; supporting a culture of transparency and accountability.
  • Post-Rework Evaluation and Continuous Improvement: Reviewing rework outcomes including quality, stability, and regulatory feedback; implementing lessons learned to improve future processes.

5.10.2 Communication

Effective communication is essential to ensure that all stakeholders are informed, coordinated, and aligned throughout the reworking process. The best approach involves a structured, transparent, and timely communication strategy. Key principles and steps:

  • Develop a Communication Plan: Define objectives, identify stakeholders (internal: Quality, Manufacturing, R&D, RA, Management; external: regulators, partners, suppliers), establish channels, set frequency.
  • Ensure Transparency and Clarity: Articulate reasons for rework, potential impacts, and actions being taken. Communicate known risks and mitigation strategies upfront.
  • Maintain Documentation and Traceability: Record all communications, decisions, and approvals. Use controlled documents and logs.
  • Facilitate Cross-Functional Collaboration: Hold regular cross-disciplinary meetings. Encourage open dialogue to surface concerns early.
  • Implement Regulatory Communication Protocols: If rework impacts product quality or regulatory filings, prepare necessary documentation for regulators.
  • Designate Clear Roles and Responsibilities: Assign communication responsibilities to specific personnel (e.g., project manager, Quality head, process science/MSAT head).
  • Use Clear, Concise, and Consistent Messaging: Avoid technical jargon when communicating with nonexperts. Ensure messages are consistent across all channels.
  • Prepare for Crisis or Unexpected Events: Develop contingency communication plans for contamination, delays, or regulatory findings.

The best approach to communication management during reworking is a proactive, transparent, and well-structured strategy that involves developing a detailed communication plan, ensuring cross-functional collaboration, maintaining thorough documentation, and engaging regulatory authorities when needed.

5.11 Submission & Approval Timelines

Original Text

Reworking a biological drug substance involves carefully revisiting and potentially modifying production processes to ensure product quality, safety, and efficacy. The timeline for reworking such a drug substance and/or drug product can vary significantly depending on several factors, including the complexity of the product, extent of modifications needed, regulatory requirements, and manufacturing facility's capabilities.

Typical phases and approximate timelines:

  1. Assessment and Planning (2–4 weeks): Evaluation of the current process and product; identifying reasons for rework; developing a detailed rework plan and risk assessment.
  2. Process Development and Optimization (4–12 weeks): Adjusting upstream or downstream processes; small-scale laboratory or pilot studies; analytical method development or validation if needed.
  3. Analytical and Stability Testing (4–8 weeks): Confirming product integrity, potency, and purity post-rework; stability testing to ensure product remains within specifications.
  4. Scale-up and Validation (8–16 weeks): Manufacturing at larger scale; process validation batches to confirm reproducibility.
  5. Regulatory Documentation and Submission (4–12 weeks): Preparing amendments or supplemental dossiers; addressing agency queries and obtaining prior approval.
  6. Manufacturing and Release (2–6 weeks, variable): Final production run; quality release-testing and documentation.
"Total Estimated Timeline: Typically, reworking a drug substance can take approximately 6 to 12 months, but in some cases, especially with complex modifications or regulatory hurdles, it may extend longer." — PDA TR74 §5.11

Factors Influencing Reworking Timeline:

  • Regulatory Pathways: The need for prior approval or additional filings can significantly affect timelines. Approval processes differ by region.
  • Extent of Process Changes: More extensive changes require deeper assessments and validation efforts.
  • Availability of Raw Materials and Manufacturing Capacity: Supply chain considerations and manufacturing capacity can cause delays.
  • Quality and Analytical Testing Turnaround Times: Time for quality and analytical testing can impact timelines, especially if retesting is required.
  • Single-use Systems: If new single-use systems with greater process contact-surface area or new polymers are introduced, a detailed leachable and extractable impact assessment will need to be performed before execution.
  • Regulatory Agency Review Times: Variability in how long agencies take to review submissions and respond can introduce uncertainties.

5.12 Quality Unit Preparedness

Original Text

The Quality Unit requires comprehensive training documents to ensure compliance and efficiency during unexpected events of reworking. Typical documents that might be needed:

  • Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs): SOPs for rework processes with step-by-step instructions; SOPs for identifying and segregating nonconforming materials.
  • Regulatory Guidelines: Relevant regulatory guidelines from FDA, EMA, or other local/national standards related to rework.
  • Quality Control Procedures: Documents detailing quality control checks required before, during, and after rework.
  • Safety Training Documents: Training materials on safety procedures specific to rework operations.
  • Documentation and Recordkeeping Training: Instructions for accurate documentation and recordkeeping to ensure traceability and compliance.
  • Risk Assessment and Management: Guidelines for conducting risk assessments related to reworking processes.
  • Material Handling Procedures: Proper procedures for handling materials safely during the rework process.
  • Equipment Handling and Maintenance: Training on specific equipment used for reworking, including maintenance and calibration.
  • Continuous Improvement Practices: Documents that facilitate identifying areas for improvement in rework processes.

Steps to effectively prepare for a reworking event:

  1. Risk Assessment and Planning: Conduct thorough risk assessment; prepare detailed rework plan with objectives, resources, timelines, and personnel responsibilities.
  2. Documentation and SOPs: Ensure all SOPs related to rework are up to date; document every step clearly and maintain traceability.
  3. Training: Provide training for personnel involved in reworking activities on new procedures, equipment use, and quality requirements.
  4. Material Management: Review and inspect raw materials, components, and consumables; establish segregation system for nonconforming materials.
  5. Monitoring, Review, and Control: Set up monitoring and control mechanisms; implement checks and balances to detect deviations promptly.
  6. Communication: Maintain clear communication channels within the team and with other departments.
  7. Quality Assurance: Conduct QA checks before, during, and after the rework. The quality unit is ultimately responsible for ensuring product quality and providing quality oversight throughout any reworking activity.
  8. Feedback and Improvement: After completing the rework, gather feedback and conduct thorough process review to identify improvement areas; update policies and procedures to reflect lessons learned.

It is crucial for the quality unit, comprising both quality assurance and quality control, to be well-prepared to manage the situations effectively and ensure that product quality and compliance are maintained. Ultimately, preparedness involves a combination of detailed planning, ongoing training, and resource management and ensuring that the team is proactive in adapting to any new challenges that arise during the reworking process.

5.13 Conclusions

Original Text

In urgent situations, such as a global pandemic (e.g., COVID-19), wars, or natural disasters (e.g., Category 5 hurricanes, catastrophic earthquakes, major floods, meteor catastrophe), certain regulatory flexibilities or deviations may be considered by the MAH to address critical supply issues, like immediate drug shortages. However, reworking an OOS drug substance generally involves significant quality and regulatory considerations, such as:

  • Regulatory Frameworks and Guidance: Regulatory agencies such as the U.S. FDA, EMA, and other agencies offer guidance on managing deviations, OOS results, and manufacturing challenges. In urgent situations, these agencies may provide tailored guidance or emergency procedures. However, deviations from approved specifications generally require proper justification and thorough documentation.
  • Reworking of OOS Drug Substance: Reworking involves processing material that initially failed to meet specifications to bring it into compliance. This process must be validated and scientifically justified, demonstrating that reworking does not compromise product quality, safety, stability and efficacy.
  • Regulatory Approval and Documentation: Any decision to rework should be thoroughly documented, including risk assessments and validation data. For licensed products, regulatory authorities often require that deviations or exceptions from established processes be reported. Depending on the jurisdiction and the specific nature and impact of the change, prior approval may be necessary.
  • Risk Management and Justification: In emergencies or shortages, regulators may offer some flexibility, but the company must ensure reworking does not compromise drug quality. A comprehensive risk assessment, incorporating knowledge management, should be conducted to evaluate its impact on safety and efficacy.
  • Good Manufacturing Practice Compliance: Reworking must adhere to GMP standards, and the process should be validated. Proper change control procedures should be followed.
"Reworking an OOS drug substance should never be treated as routine. However, in urgent situations such as a pandemic or critical shortage, biotechnology companies may consider reworking if it is properly justified, validated, and documented. They should proactively engage with regulatory authorities, possibly under emergency use or contingency provisions, to ensure compliance and maintain product integrity." — PDA TR74 §5.13

6.1 Unit Operation Characteristics

Original Text

Chromatography is used to purify a product (e.g., mAb, target proteins, compounds) considering the properties of the target solute, feed stream, required purity level, productivity, costs, process sequence, process parameters, and implementation of the designed purification process. Biomolecules are purified using chromatography techniques that separate them according to the difference in their specific properties as summarized in Table 6.1-1. In ion exchange chromatography (IEX), biomolecules are separated according to the difference in their net surface charges.

See Table 6.1-1 (Chromatography Techniques) and Table 6.1-2 (Quality Attribute Risk Assessment Topics) below.

The overall goal of a chromatographic step is to remove impurities, such as host-cell-derived proteins (HCP), nucleic acids, carbohydrates, and other undesired compounds.

In general, a full-scale chromatography system will consist of the following components:

  • Buffer and Product Reservoirs: Containers (hard-piped or single use disposables) used to supply the buffers, product feed, and for elution collection required during the chromatography operation.
  • Pumps: Peristaltic pumps used to feed the buffers into chromatography columns. Depending on scale, other types of pumps may be utilized considering process requirements and equipment design.
  • Column: The chamber where the chromatographic media is loaded. The size (diameter and height) of the column will be defined based on the process requirements. The material of construction (e.g., glass, polycarbonate, steel) is selected based on process conditions (e.g., pressure and flow rates).
  • Resin: Chromatography media intended for the purification process. The packaging of the columns is commonly conducted by creating a slurry where the particles are suspended, that is, poured into the column.
  • Valves: Sanitary systems used to control the flow of materials during the operation of the chromatography system. These may be manual or pneumatic units.
  • Sensors: Applications intended to monitor process parameters required for the chromatography process. These may include pH, air, temperature, flow, pressure, and UV monitor.
  • Controller/Computer: Used to control and monitor the operation of the system.

The purification strategy involving a chromatography step is based on the characteristics (e.g., properties and stability) of the product under different conditions, final specifications, feed properties (e.g., concentration of product and impurities), nature of the process (e.g., continuous or discontinuous), and nature of the substances (e.g., hazardous). When designing the reprocessing step for the AEX chromatography case study, consideration was given to the quality attributes for mAbs listed in Table 6.1-2 (19).

6.2 Points to Consider

Original Text

The elements in Section 6.2 should be considered as part of reprocessing a chromatography column. These elements are further discussed in PDA Technical Report No. 14 (Revised 2008): Validation of Column-Based Chromatography Processes for the Purification of Proteins.

6.2.1 Column Packing

The resin utilized for chromatography is loaded into the column in a controlled manner to ensure a uniform distribution while preventing the formation of channels that will affect the performance of the unit operation. Commonly, a slurry containing resin in a process buffer is poured into the column. Once the resin is settled, the integrity of the packaged column is confirmed. In the case of reprocessing, the integrity of the column bed should be verified and confirmed prior to its use. Any issue affecting the bed should be solved prior to reprocessing, considering resuspension and repacking of the resin if applicable.

6.2.2 Cleaning and Sanitization

Full cleaning and sanitization of the column must be conducted in preparation for reprocessing. Cleaning and sanitization will follow existing procedures and practices, including the regeneration of the resin. (These elements are discussed further in (68, 69).)

6.2.3 Performance

Performance of the chromatography step must be monitored according to the routine established procedures and must satisfy predefined acceptance criteria. The criteria should include, but not be limited to, the operational conditions (e.g., asymmetry, height equivalent to the theoretical plate, and/or transition analysis) and quality attributes (65).

6.2.4 Regeneration and Lifecycle

The resin must be regenerated consistent with established procedures and must satisfy the acceptance criteria defined and validated. The use of the resin for a reprocessing activity will be within the validated number of reuses if the reprocessing activity is not associated with a malfunction of the chromatography column and resin.

6.2.5 Validation and Filings

Validation of reprocessing will be performed under a prospective protocol. Detailed guidance for validation activities of chromatography processes for the purification of proteins is provided in PDA TR 14 (68). In the event that changes are required to an existing filing, guidance is provided in PDA Technical Report No. 38: Manufacturing Chromatography Systems Post-Approval Changes (ChromPAC).

In this example, a protocol for prospective validation of reprocessing at the manufacturing scale was written and approved; however, the potential of a concurrent validation could be considered if the frequency for reprocessing did not justify a prospective protocol. The protocol required a single GMP run at manufacturing scale, including all routine in-process and release testing as well as additional nonroutine testing, for example, analysis of HCP, acidic variants, aggregate levels, fragmentation, oxidation, and potency in reprocessed material. The protocol also required placing the resulting drug substance in the stability program. The protocol acceptance criteria for nonroutine testing were based on the comparability criteria established in Section 6.4.3 and demonstrated at small-scale. (See Section 6.5.6.)

Yet, a full prospective validation approach may not be feasible or practical for reprocessing in all cases. Instead, a concurrent validation strategy may be more appropriate, allowing the manufacture and testing of products and only reprocessing when and if necessary. In the case of concurrent validation, the acceptance criteria shall be aligned with the approved specifications for the process.

In the EU MAA, a Change Management Protocol was included that also outlined the implementation strategy to seek a reduction in reporting requirements from a Type II Variation to a Type IB Variation upon successful completion of the protocol.

The AEX chromatography step was operated in the flow-through mode, binding impurities such as DNA, HCP, endotoxin, and viruses to the resin while the antibody passed through. In addition, this step was designed to reduce levels of aggregate and acidic variants. Within the operating parameters of the AEX step, in this example, high levels of HCP in the column effluent are possible on rare occasions, and therefore, HCP is the quality attribute most likely to trigger reprocessing.

The risk assessment identified the AEX column (Table 6.3.1-2) as the step with the highest RPN. Development data indicated that the risk of high HCP levels was an inherent process risk and a rare event attributable to multiple, interacting sources of variability in the unit operation. Thus, the conclusion was that the AEX unit operation was a candidate for reprocessing.

The assigned scores are multiplied together (S × O × D = RPN) to determine the risk priority number (RPN). For example, for the "anion exchange chromatography" reprocessing step in Table 6.3.1-2, S × O × D = 5 × 5 × 5 = 125.

"Note: For HCP limits in Table 6.3.1-2: an appropriate IPC limit for DS HCP would typically be set at 70–80% of the action limit (approximately 70–80 ng/mg; a practical choice is 80 ng/mg). This provides a buffer to account for assay variability and process drift, enabling corrective actions before reaching the <100 ng/mg action limit."

6.3.2 Business Case and Project Charter

Three options were considered in assessing the business case for reprocessing at the AEX step:

  1. Discarding the batch upon failure to meet the HCP in-process control requirement of ≤80 ng/mg
  2. Redesigning the AEX step or adding additional steps
  3. Reprocessing during the AEX step

From a business perspective, it was confirmed that all three options could be managed to ensure acceptable product quality at release, but Option 3 was the most cost-effective and could be designed to meet quality and regulatory requirements. A project charter was created, and a cross-functional team was formed to develop a reprocessing step.
