Introduction

Advanced Therapy Medicinal Products (ATMPs) are unique because they are based on cells, genes, or tissues from animals and human beings. ATMPs are produced either in low-batch volumes, such as products produced for an individual patient (autologous), or (relatively) larger batch volumes for products produced for multiple patients (allogeneic).

ATMPs are classified into three main types.

  1. Gene Therapy Medicines: These contain genes that lead to a therapeutic, prophylactic, or diagnostic effect. They work by inserting "recombinant" genes into the body, usually to treat a variety of diseases, including genetic disorders, cancer, or other long-term diseases. A recombinant gene is a segment of DNA that is created in the laboratory, bringing together DNA from different sources.
  2. Somatic-Cell Therapy Medicines: These contain cells or tissues that have been manipulated to change their biological characteristics, or cells or tissues not intended to be used for the same essential functions in the body. They can be used to cure, diagnose, or prevent diseases.
  3. Tissue-Engineered Medicines: These contain cells or tissues that have been modified so they can be used to repair, regenerate, or replace human tissue.

"Combined ATMPs" are ATMPs that combine with one or more medical device(s) as an integral part of the medicine.

In general, ATMPs should adhere to the same manufacturing requirements as sterile parenteral drugs for their upstream, downstream, and fill-and-finish requirements. The minimum expectations for the manufacture of ATMPs are essentially the same as for all biological process considerations, with some specific nuances depending on the starting material, end product, intended administration window, and intended recipient(s). The use of ATMPs for clinical trials must also be manufactured under equivalent quality standards to the manufacturing of ATMPs with a marketing authorization.

Original Text — PDA PtC-14 (p7–p8)

The basic good manufacturing practice (GMP) principles of all processes include design, control, and monitoring. For ATMPs, these are:

Design

  • An appropriate Pharmaceutical Quality System (PQS)
  • A detailed design of the process (open, closed, or hybrid, for autologous and/or allogeneic therapies)
  • Facilities, utilities, and process equipment that support the process design
  • Prevention measures to avoid extraneous contamination, cross-contamination, error, or mix-ups
  • Material, personnel, and waste flows that support the process and prevention measures

Control and Mitigation

  • A formal contamination control strategy (CCS) specific to the process
  • Control of starting materials and their source/donation and critical raw materials that are appropriately sourced and controlled for quality
  • Manufacturing environment controlled in a manner that supports the CCS
  • Laboratory practices and personnel training that support the PQS
  • Process (Safety) equipment controlled in a manner that supports both the PQS and CCS
  • Effective Pharmaceutical Quality System (including a facility risk assessment)
  • Traceability requirements that support batch identity and traceability from manufacture through distribution
  • Warehousing, distribution, and temperature controls that support product quality throughout all phases of production and distribution

Monitoring

  • Process-environment-related attributes in accordance with CCS
  • Cleanliness of facility and equipment to support fulfillment of CCS
  • Testing (including microbial, particulate, airflow, and HVAC) to confirm integrity of environmental controls
  • Release-testing strategy to ensure fulfillment of the quality target product profile (QTPP)

BSL ：

Original Text — PDA PtC-14 (p8)

This document considers the facility, utilities, and process equipment designs necessary to support the manufacture and testing of ATMPs (autologous and allogeneic). Ideally, the design considerations should be weighed upfront to ensure the process meets the respective requirements.

ATMP processes are still an emerging science. As a consequence, there are a variety of groups that are involved in their development and commercialization including academia, research organizations, hospitals, and biopharmaceutical companies. At the time of compilation of this Points to Consider (PtC) document, various manufacturing environment technologies are employed in the manufacture of ATMPs. Products are commonly manipulated in biosafety cabinets (BSCs), isolators, or closed systems with the required cleanroom surroundings. The stage of commercialization does not always dictate the type of facility or type of technology through which the products are produced; rather, the environment or technology is often selected based on the flexibility required for the process.

These technologies all have controls to ensure the environment where product manipulations occur is maintained in a manner that prevents contamination. These cell-, gene-, or tissue-engineering-based products are classified from occupational safety requirements to have a working environment determined by its Biological Safety Level (BSL).

ATMP manufacturing includes some products that are meant to replace, repair, or modify genes in a cell. To achieve this, certain viral vectors (e.g., adeno-associated virus, retrovirus, lentivirus, herpesvirus) are used for that purpose. Viral vector-based products can vary in their BSL depending on the type of virus and its potential to cause disease in humans. These are commonly classified as BSL-1 or BSL-2 and, in rare circumstances, even higher (e.g., BSL-3), depending on the virus load and type of virus. This Points to Consider document will focus on BSL-1 and BSL-2 requirements only, as BSL-3 and BSL-4 facilities are rare for the manufacture of ATMPs.

QRM

Original Text — PDA PtC-14 (p9)

Product and process characterization is performed using quality risk management (QRM) principles to help the sponsor understand the importance of each aspect of the developed process/product. This information includes requirements for environmental conditions, production equipment/systems, product quality, and occupational safety. These elements have been reflected in GMP guidelines, norms, and practices for decades. Additional recommendations on these concepts specific to ATMPs are addressed in this technical document.

Given the novelty of these products and the relative scarcity of comprehensive technical guidance in support of manufacturing, it can become difficult to find resources to help determine appropriate strategies to ensure the successful manufacture of ATMPs. This Points to Consider (PtC) is an initiative of the PDA ATMP Advisory Board and was compiled by a diverse team of PDA subject matter experts to address some of the common questions within the industry regarding the manufacture of these products.

The manufacture of ATMPs should generally comply with the GMP and regulatory guidance for other biologic and aseptic products. Therefore, many of the topics that are included in this document may overlap with the conventional handling of biologics. However, the scarcity of comprehensive technical guidance and common questions from the industry seeking help to understand how various requirements apply to ATMP processes necessitate this guidance exhaustively addressing those design, control, and monitoring requirements, even when those requirements overlap with conventional processing.

As technology advances and regulatory expectations for continuous process improvement continue to grow, pressures may increase within the industry to further minimize the risk of our processes by moving to greater containment, greater sterility assurance, and reduction of personnel involved in the process. This is not always possible with the current state of the art for these novel processes. Hence, this PtC addresses the common technologies employed today and presents the risk-benefit analysis for each so that manufacturers can realistically assess their current process while simultaneously understanding alternate designs and controls that may be available for implementation.

This document is organized into topics and various subtopics that address the questions raised on the subject of ATMP manufacture. Each question first presents an overall recommendation, which may be followed by a rationale for the recommendation and/or appropriate references for further reading.

Q1.1

How can a risk assessment be created for an ATMP facility?

ATMP ？ |  Recommendation + Rationale (p10–p13)

Recommendation

The manufacture of any product, including an ATMP, necessarily entails some degree of risk. The degree of risk often correlates with such factors as the number of manual interventions, the type of environment in which those interventions occur, and the open or closed nature of the process.

For example, autologous therapies produced in small batches (small-scale) often have a higher risk per unit produced when manual manipulations are numerous. The facility design element of a formal facility risk assessment should always be included as part of the Design Review process in the organization, but it is even more important when processes have varying levels of risk.

Based on the process design principles and nature of the work to be performed in the facility, the organization can define the risk profile of the facility and work to mitigate those risks through appropriate design controls associated with the equipment (e.g., isolators, BSCs) and appropriate process improvements (e.g., single-use systems, closed processing, automation, improved intervention design).

As part of the same facility risk assessment, design controls can also be considered to manage risks related to biological hazards.

Interdisciplinary Team Requirement

Facility risk assessment should be undertaken by an interdisciplinary team and should be guided by a risk-assessment facilitator of appropriate experience along with a scribe to record the risk assessment.

The scope of the facility risk assessment should include both facility and equipment design and selection, as well as product, personnel, and environmental risk categories. Including personnel and environmental safety aspects in the risk assessment is recommended as it is likely that safety-related controls could also have an impact on product quality.

Control measures for one risk category should be assessed for impact on other risk categories. For example, the use of a virus in cell therapy manufacturing may pose health risks to personnel while facilities producing the viral vectors have additional safety considerations due to the active replication of viral vectors, albeit in a controlled process and environment.

Where applicable, specialized safety personnel, such as those with biosafety, toxicology, microbiology, and virology expertise, should be involved in the performance of the risk assessment and in the design of the mitigations of the identified risks.

Risk Assessment Elements — 1 & 2

Facility risk assessments should consider, but are not limited to, the following elements:

1. Material

  1. Presence of adventitious agents (e.g., potential presence of replication competent agents).
  2. Material handling and transfer into and out of critical process areas (e.g., appropriate design and classification of pass-through containers, surface decontamination/disinfection during transfer of frozen materials, transfer of sterile parts/consumables).
  3. Controls and material-flow requirements when viruses, cellular materials, or GMOs are present in the facility (including raw material, product, sample, and waste flows).
  4. Incoming control of materials for quality, microorganisms, and adventitious agents (e.g., vendor qualification, vendor audit, vendor certification, in-house sampling and testing, controls at loading dock, warehouse).

2. Facility and Equipment

  1. Equipment, material, and waste flow (e.g., material or equipment moving to a higher-grade area, cross-contamination due to non-unidirectional flow).
  2. Personnel flow (e.g., cross-contamination risk due to personnel working or supporting different batches or different products at the same time).
  3. Cleanability of equipment and facility, including contact materials (e.g., selection of materials of construction that are nonadditive, non-absorptive, or non-reactive, including primary equipment, gaskets, filters, and selection of product-/process-compatible lubricants).
  4. Need for dedicated, segregated, self-contained areas and separation by time (e.g., manufacturing process using infectious viral vectors).
  5. Containment adequacy (e.g., ability to contain potential aerosol release, examination of open processing for possible routes of contamination).
  6. Radiation shielding (e.g., use of x-ray irradiation equipment in process may require additional consideration directed by local regulations).
  7. Aspects of commissioning, qualification, maintenance, and environmental monitoring (EM) (e.g., handling of pass-through containers, process gases).

Rationale — Why Cross-Category Assessment Matters

A facility risk assessment can help determine the most appropriate design of facilities and selection of equipment. In particular, it is important to consider not only quality risks but also environmental and safety risks. Examples of how hazards can affect both safety and quality aspects include, but are not limited to:

  1. Certification and qualification test for a BSC covers product, personnel, and environmental risk but placement and layout of BSC should also be verified according to manufacturer and/or standard requirements (e.g., NSF/ANSI 49). The layout will then likely impact personnel, material, sample, and waste flows and potentially affect the cross-contamination control strategy. If the risk cannot be adequately controlled with the use of a BSC, a closed system or isolators should be used.
  2. Waste treatment is part of the biological risk management in the facility, and the type of waste treatment will impact personnel and material flow and potentially affect the cross-contamination control strategy.
  3. Facility decontamination processes may leave residues that subsequently impact product quality and patient safety but may also represent a risk to operator safety.
  4. Cross-contamination between manufacturing areas of different products (e.g., live vs. inactivated microorganisms) will impact product quality and patient safety, as well as put operators at risk.
  5. Design requirements for BSL containment of GMOs or other highly potent products may conflict with aseptic processing requirements. For example, a negative-pressure cascade may be recommended to contain GMO within the facility, but aseptic processes rely on a positive-pressure cascade to prevent contamination from entering the facility.

Risk Group Classification (WHO / FDA)

A list of biological materials, including GMOs, should be maintained and reviewed regularly against local requirements as part of a risk management program. The list serves as a basis for the organization to determine the risk group classification of each material, and the risk group is determined by the hazard posed by the biological agents and toxins (if any) used in the manufacturing process.

Although risk group classification depends on localities, the definition is generally in line with those from the World Health Organization (WHO) and U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA):

  • Risk Group 1 (no or low individual and community risk): A microorganism that is unlikely to cause human or animal disease.
  • Risk Group 2 (moderate individual risk, low community risk): A pathogen that can cause human or animal disease but is unlikely to be a serious hazard to laboratory personnel, the community, livestock, or the environment. Laboratory exposures may cause serious infection, but effective treatment and preventive measures are available, and the risk of the spread of infection is limited.
  • Risk Group 3 (high individual risk, low community risk): A pathogen that usually causes serious human or animal diseases but does not ordinarily spread from one infected individual to another. Effective treatment and preventive measures are available (not considered in this document).
  • Risk Group 4 (high individual and community risk): A pathogen that usually causes serious human or animal disease and that can be readily transmitted from one individual to another, directly or indirectly. Effective treatment and preventive measures are not usually available (not considered in this document).

Open System

An open system is one where the material is exposed to the manufacturing environment for even a short time. Due to this exposure, the environment is required to be of Grade A cleanliness.

The manufacturing environment is a total sum of events that occur during the process — any particulate, bioburden, pyrogenic, or viral contamination present in the area has the potential to contaminate the product. Open systems are often used for early-phase/proof-of-concept trials when the required manipulations are still being defined and refined.

Closed System

A closed system is hermetically sealed from the manufacturing environment, inside which the product pathway is considered sterile. Examples include single-use systems that have been gamma-irradiated using intrinsic sterile connectors.

Pressure Concepts: Bubble, Sink, and Cascade

Cleanrooms are designed to maintain positive pressure, ensuring filtered air always flows from the cleanest to less-clean spaces — a pressure cascade.

In a multichambered cleanroom, the cleanest room is kept at the highest pressure. Air locks can act as a "bubble" (higher pressure than both adjacent rooms) or as a "sink" (lower pressure than both adjacent rooms).

For potent products or viral vectors, the pressure concept should be adapted to prevent spreading into surrounding areas using negative and positive adjacent areas.

The BSC is an open system; therefore the pressure differential cannot be regulated in the same way as an isolator. Instead, the surrounding environment is controlled with respect to the air locks and corridors.

Liquid Nitrogen (LN₂) Generation and Distribution System

PDA PtC-14, p26–27 | Ultra-low temperature storage, cryogenic burn risk, cell bank backup

Liquid Nitrogen — Key Requirements

a. Installation of a liquid nitrogen (LN₂) generation and distribution system should consider the asphyxiation risk. A room O₂-level monitoring system with visual and audible alarms should be available, and personnel should also be equipped with O₂ personal-monitoring instruments. A buddy system and/or man-down alarms should also be considered. Means to detect and isolate any leak in the system should be available, including PPE for routine use and emergency-rescue purposes.

b. LN₂ is extremely cold and contact to bare skin causes severe frostbite. Signage should be posted throughout the space. PPE-donning stations should be prominently positioned within the floor plan. First-aid stations should also be available for accidental exposures.

c. The materials that require storage in LN₂ (ultra-low temperature storage) are commonly the most critical materials in the process (e.g., cell or viral banks). Therefore, it is common in designs to consider the inclusion of back-up equipment (e.g., LN₂ freezers) in case of equipment failure. The back-up equipment should be qualified for use and subject to routine maintenance so it is ready for deployment.

d. Piping for distributing LN₂ should be adequately insulated both due to the cold-burn risk and to avoid condensation on external surfaces.

e. Pipes should be marked with "LN₂" signs and should indicate the flow direction.

f. Due to the risk of testing LN₂ and its stability, LN₂ quality through the distribution system is typically confirmed by the use of a Certificate of Compliance or Certificate of Analysis provided by LN₂ supplier.

g. Design should include controls to provide continuous supply of LN₂ as required (e.g., source-tank switchover), and such features should be commissioned and/or qualified accordingly.

Q4.1 — Biosafety Cabinet (BSC) Selection for ATMP Manufacturing

PDA PtC-14 p29–p33 | BSC Classes, Airflow, Grade Requirements, Operational Constraints

Recommendation — BSC Overview

A BSC is flexible in its use, although it typically does not have the same levels of integrated control and monitoring systems as an isolator. Instead, it relies on operators to manually decontaminate the Grade A space, maintain it through good aseptic practice, and appropriately monitor the environment.

This increased reliance upon the operator means that operations should be carefully planned with an understanding of the barrier system limitations of the BSC and should incorporate appropriate risk mitigations to safeguard the aseptic operations.

According to EU GMP Annex 1, the BSC is not classified as barrier technology.

The room environment where the BSC is located should support the maintenance of the Grade A environment within the operational zone. This dictates the need for a Grade B environment to surround the BSC.

BSCs are widely used in ATMP manufacturing as they are easy to install and maintain. They generally occupy a modest footprint that permits highly flexible operations. BSCs allow access to the inner working zone via an open sash.

Note: BSC Class III is the sole exception to the open-sash design.

Figure 4.1-1🔍 — Biosafety Cabinet (textual description): A typical Class II BSC shows a rectangular enclosure with a sloped glass sash at the front.   • Type B2 (Total Exhaust): Min. inflow 100 ft/min; 100% exhaust, zero recirculation. For significant toxic chemical quantities.

Airflow Pattern — Figure 4.1-2🔍

Figure 4.1-2🔍 — Airflow Pattern in a Biosafety Cabinet (textual description): The diagram illustrates vertical laminar downflow from a supply HEPA filter in the top plenum. Room air is drawn inward through the front sash opening (inflow velocity arrow pointing inward). Operating Considerations for the BSC

  • Easy-to-perform manual processes through the sash opening
  • Flexibility to adjust how the BSC is used based on process needs
  • Rapid changeover from process to process

Constraint Considerations for the BSC

  • Material transfers can be difficult due to the need for manual sanitizing upon entry to the Grade A zone
  • An assistant is often needed to conduct material transfer so the operator can continuously maintain hands within the Grade A zone
  • Wrist rests should be purchased to avoid the operator covering the exhaust and effectively bringing contaminated air into the Grade A environment

Operational Comparison of Biosafety Cabinets and Isolators

BSCs and isolators are typically equipped with a filter of at least a HEPA classification. The type of HEPA filter installed is expected, at a minimum, to have particulate removal of 99.99% when tested, in accordance with internationally acceptable standards. The filtration capability of HEPA filters in BSCs and isolators should provide a Grade A operating work zone.

Since a BSC is operated via an open sash compared with the closed operating mode of an isolator, there is a greater need to manage particulates and contaminants through good aseptic practice. Products in the work zone of the BSC may be exposed to particulates from gowning worn by personnel since personnel are required to manipulate the products directly via gloved hands.

In contrast, an isolator provides a closed environment where personnel indirectly access the work zone through integrated gloves. The use of gloves attached to the isolator may make product-handling inconvenient (i.e., due to the loss of some physical dexterity) in an isolator compared to a BSC. However, indirect access not only protects the product and process but also protects the operator as there is no risk of transfer of contamination to the operator's gowning.

Production within isolators also reduces the gowning requirement for operators since they may be gowned in accordance with the requirements of the background environment. This means operators working with isolators may be gowned for Grade D (or C) operations, while operators working with BSC must gown for Grade A operations.

Material transfers in a BSC are performed with the open sash, as one operator transfers items to the operator working in the BSC. Transfer technologies such as RTPs can provide lower-risk transfers. Isolators use transfer systems like MALs and/or RTPs to provide a safe and rapid transfer after a vaporized or sprayed decontamination agent cycle.

Decision Framework: When to Choose BSC vs. Isolator

Autologous vs. Allogeneic Considerations

Autologous ATMPs (patient-specific) typically involve:

  • Small batch sizes (one patient = one batch)
  • High product diversity and process variability
  • Frequent rapid changeovers between different patient materials
  • Shorter manufacturing campaigns

Allogeneic ATMPs typically involve:

  • Larger batch sizes from a single donor source
  • More standardized, repetitive processes
  • Longer manufacturing campaigns
  • Commercial-scale throughput potential

Phase-Appropriate Approach

Clinical phase (Phase I/II): BSC may be pragmatic given low batch frequency, limited capital, and need for rapid process adjustments. Regulatory agencies generally accept BSC at early clinical stages if appropriate risk mitigation is in place.

Late clinical / Commercial phase: Regulators increasingly expect isolator-level containment for licensed products. The investment in isolator infrastructure becomes justified by the higher production volumes and regulatory expectation for robust sterility assurance.

Q5.2 — Differences in chamber leak-integrity testing between isolator and BSC?

BSC | pp. 46–47

Recommendation — Isolator Leak Testing

For isolators, the leak test should be performed frequently with the interval established based on the frequency of intended operations in an appropriate risk assessment.

The leak-testing frequency for isolators should not be limited to periodic evaluation, but rather should include the following situations:

  • After changing gloves and sleeves (documented in the glove change management protocol)
  • Following any maintenance or repair that has breached the sealed structure
  • Supporting failure investigations, especially those involving viral and/or bioburden contaminations

PDA Points to Consider for the Aseptic Processing of Sterile Pharmaceutical Products in Isolators (2020) recommends that leak-testing be performed at defined periods, at a minimum, at the beginning and end of each batch or campaign and following any intervention that would affect the integrity of the unit.

The testing procedures are described in Annex E.3 of ISO EN 14644-7. Methods include:

  • Oxygen method (only for negative pressure)
  • Pressure-change method
  • Parjo method (very rare; mainly atomic industries)
  • Constant-pressure method (ISO 10648-2)
  • Tracer gas method (PDA TR No. 34)

Airflow Visualization — Common Principles

The airflow visualization, or smoke study, is a method used to aid in the evaluation of the CGMP-required airflow generated by a BSC, isolator system, or containment enclosure.

Common criteria include:

  • Unidirectional airflow in Grade A areas
  • Airflow around the apertures between Grade A and lower-grade areas

Isolator — Airflow Visualization Objectives

For isolator systems, airflow is designed to ensure that first air from the HEPA filter flows over the sterile product. The objective is to verify that airflows create a "sweeping action over and away from the product" under dynamic conditions (FDA, EU GMP Annex 1, PIC/S Annex 1, WHO TRS 1044 - Annex 2).

The study visualizes flow at:

  • Downflow across the chamber/work zone at the working position
  • Airflow with sweeping action over and away from the product
  • Airflow around large objects that may generate turbulence
  • Airflow during manual operations, interventions, and transfers
  • Airflow during processing of open units — no turbulence enters open units
  • Airflow at apertures — flow from critical area outward without reflux or entrainment

Studies performed at rest AND in operation, including during interventions. Air must not ingress from lower-grade to higher-grade zones.

BSC — Airflow Visualization Requirements

For BSCs, the airflow visualization mandated by NSF/ANSI 49 – 2019 requires the following to be performed at least annually:

  • Airflow along the centerline of the work surface is downward
  • Airflow along the entire perimeter of the sash at the outside of the cabinet is inward into the grill — does not intrude into the work zone
  • Airflow behind the sash, on top of the access opening, does not escape from the BSC
  • Airflow behind the window, at the sides and top, is not leaking out of the BSC

For BSCs as containment enclosures, the primary purpose is to ensure that any hazardous product material liberated in the airflow is transported away from the operator. Visualization focuses on:

  • Airflow around large objects that may generate turbulence
  • Airflow around continuous viable and nonviable sampling equipment
  • Airflow around hand movement and equipment during required operations
  • Airflow during interventions
  • Airflow remaining undisturbed by external airflow from surroundings

Recommendation — Pressure Measurement Methods

Pressure cascades can be measured using two methods:

Method 1 — Direct Differential Measurement

Directly measure the pressure difference between two adjacent areas where common areas exist. It is recommended that individual tubing be used between each high- and low-pressure sample-point location (although a manifold is possible but more complex). Advantage: a direct response to a pressure cascade change of state.

Method 2 — Absolute Pressure with Calculation

Measure the absolute pressure of each space and calculate the differential. Advantage: typically uses fewer sensors and provides a wider view of pressure comparison across the facility. Disadvantage: wider error range as two sensors are required for each differential calculation. In either case, a loss of local pressure would be identified.

Alarm Response and Pressure Control

A suitable measurement interval for pressure readings should provide a balance between:

  • Small variances caused by internal fluctuations within a controlled space
  • Loss of control parameters
  • Enabling the averaging of results accurately over longer periods

The process and use of the cleanrooms (entries, exits, frequency of use, movements) should be used to determine measurement and recording frequency as this may be different throughout each zone.

If a pressure loss is observed:

  • The air-handling unit (AHU) should react immediately to balance the pressure loss
  • Pressure loss should be recorded
  • An alarm should signal
  • The reason for the pressure loss must be investigated
  • Alarm signals should be timed to avoid signaling for routine operational events (entries and exits)

Manual operation in BSCs and isolators, or the opening of a door at a PAL (Personnel Air Lock) or MAL (Material Air Lock), are the most critical factors influencing air stability. Fast movements should be avoided.

Alarm delays should be minimal (less than 3 minutes) and should be justified as part of the CCS (Contamination Control Strategy).

For cleanrooms: air locks with door interlocks on all doors and pressure stabilization are recommended to avoid pressure fluctuations. Disruption of the differential pressure between air locks and adjacent cleanrooms will disrupt the function as:

  • Pressure sink — negative relative to surrounding spaces
  • Air bubble — positive relative to surrounding spaces
  • Pressure cascade — positive on one side and negative on the other

Key Takeaway for ATMP CDMO Operations

PDA PtC-14 establishes that the core tension in ATMP facility design is the simultaneous requirement for operator containment (BSL-driven negative pressure) and product sterility assurance (GMP-driven positive pressure). Every technical decision — BSC vs. isolator selection, mock-up thoroughness, leak test frequency, smoke study scope, and pressure control system design — must be resolved against the same decision hierarchy: operator safety first, patient safety second. The supplementary topics in Topic 5 provide the practical qualification and operational tools that make this dual objective achievable in a licensed ATMP manufacturing environment.
