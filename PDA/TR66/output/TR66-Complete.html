1.0 Introduction 1.0

Original Text

Single-use technology, often described as single-use systems (SUSs) or single-use equipment, has the potential to transform pharmaceutical manufacturing by offering tremendous opportunities to reduce cost, improve flexibility or cycle time, and shorten the time needed to build a manufacturing process for new, lifesaving drugs. This success, however, is very much dependent on how effectively the industry approaches the development and implementation of single-use technology. Ultimately, a new drug can only be successful if it is effective, safe, and available. Traditionally, only a comprehensive understanding of the drug product and manufacturing process can achieve these goals. This remains true as SUS is introduced in place of traditional reusable equipment. Encouraging an open science- and risk-based dialogue during supplier audits and evaluation of SUS supply chains significantly improves an SUS implementation.

This document is intended to provide the reader with critical concepts or points to consider when implementing an SUS strategy in a pharmaceutical manufacturing process. These concepts are intended to be valid both for chemically synthesized small molecules and for bioprocesses that produce large-molecule biopharmaceutical products. However, to be truly effective, many of these critical concepts must start with the design, supply chain, manufacturing, and distribution of SUSs themselves, as many inherent quality attributes can impact either the product molecule or its production process. Pharmaceutical manufacturers and single-use technology suppliers have become partners whose success is dependent on the control strategies implemented.

This document discusses SUSs that are in either direct or indirect contact with the raw materials, intermediates, and pharmaceutical drug substances or drug products. The document does not intend to discuss disposable items related to laboratory activities, final delivery system to the patient, transfusion bags, packaging, or medical devices.

Successful SUS implementation needs a comprehensive approach balancing the product and process goals achieved by using single-use technology. Section 3, Manufacturing Strategy, of this technical report is intended to present an approach that ties together key considerations when evaluating single-use technology. A well-designed manufacturing strategy will address technical, quality, business, and implementation considerations. Each topic has a dedicated section in this technical report, providing a detailed discussion of the associated considerations.

Determining the optimal manufacturing strategy involves concepts from many disciplines. An effective evaluation will have a balanced viewpoint, with input from engineering, regulatory, quality, project management, and accounting. Balancing risks and rewards of an SUS over a multiple-use system (MUS) will help determine the most appropriate manufacturing strategy. Thus, a structured science- and risk-based approach is recommended and should be consistent with principles described in ICH Guidelines Q6, Q7, Q8, Q9, Q10, and Q11. Primary goals, when developing any manufacturing strategy, should focus on controlling impacts to patient safety, product availability, and product and process understanding (1–6).

Only a formal partnership with an SUS supplier can ensure that quality is as good as or better than what is achieved with traditional systems (e.g., a purchase order is not a partnership). SUS suppliers provide equipment that includes the outsourced, value-added activities that the end user no longer performs. These value-added activities are important for the success of both organizations, and a winning control strategy for SUS has elements in both the supplier’s and the end user’s quality systems.

The concepts and recommendations presented in this technical report were developed over several years of discussion within the task force, at PDA workshops, and at other industry meetings. The authors of this technical report recognize that the conversation regarding how best to implement SUSs is just beginning. Ultimately the success of these systems will be determined by the decisions suppliers and end users make during implementation, and the hope is that this report provides a foundation for the industry to build on.

3.0 Points to Consider for Single-Use System Manufacturing Strategy 3.0

Original Text

This section provides an introduction to and overview of the most important points to consider and the necessary steps the end user must accomplish in order to implement a successful single-use (SU) strategy, while a more detailed description of these points and a structured implementation strategy can be found in the subsequent sections of this document. Additionally, this section offers a guideline for a structured approach for an initial feasibility evaluation of a single-use system (SUS) manufacturing strategy.

The key to successful SU technology implementation is the development of an effective manufacturing strategy for the molecule or formulation to be produced. Many factors must be considered when an organization is making strategic decisions, and implementation of SU technology is no different. In this report we suggest developing a high-level manufacturing strategy after a science- and risk-based approach with consideration given to four main areas that influence the success of SUS implementation:

  • Technologies and system integration
  • Qualification of assembled products
  • Business drivers
  • Implementation

The resulting manufacturing strategy will then outline the questions for consideration needed for successful implementation of an SUS. This technical report has a dedicated section for each of these areas of consideration. An overview of each will be presented in this section.

As depicted in Figure 3.0-1🔍, each area plays a crucial role in establishing successful use of SUSs. Questions within these areas of focus must be considered and balanced against an organization's needs and competencies when developing a manufacturing strategy (detailed in Section 7.1). Only a comprehensive approach ensures that the primary customer-based goals of patient safety, product availability, product understanding, and process understanding are met when implementing new technology.

"Figure 3.0-1: Key Decision Areas for an SUS Manufacturing Strategy"

Each area has a situation-dependent role in the decision process and may be weighed differently according to the circumstances of the end user. For example, a contract manufacturer may value the ability to shorten changeover time between different products, whereas a large pharmaceutical company may weigh the risk of adopting new technology against utilization of existing capabilities. A small organization may not have the resources (manpower or capital) to invest in a fixed multiple-use system (MUS). Other important factors to consider are:

  • The current stage of drug development
  • The scale of post-launch manufacturing
  • The likelihood of the facility being used to produce other products in the future
  • The risk associated with depending on a sole SUS supplier

A detailed implementation process for SUS is described in Section 7, and this includes details of the workflow for the implementation process, starting with the preparation of the manufacturing strategy reviewed in this section.

A gated approach is used to facilitate the preparation of the manufacturing strategy and decision process. A "gate," as it is used here, is a decision point where the pros and cons of the strategy are addressed using a science- and risk-based approach. A proposal for the effective evaluation of important factors within the gated approach is described in Figure 3.0-2. Each stage is crucial to the successful implementation of a new technology. Negative results at any stage may halt the implementation of the SUS. An example of such a negative decision will be a case in which there will be an adverse effect on patient safety or where there is no feasible solution to a technical problem. The aim of the gated approach is to fail the process quickly when a major problem cannot be solved. The decision pathway is not necessarily linear—the end user may decide to enter the pathway at any given point. However, each stage must be addressed to have a balanced decision process prior to the implementation of an SUS.

A detailed implementation process for SUSs is described in Section 7, and the implementation workflow is developed based on the decisions made in the manufacturing strategy reviewed in this section. An overview of the different implementation stages is described in Figure 7.0-1 and Table 7.0-1. Key documentary outputs for the whole implementation process are listed in Section 8, Appendix I: SUS Facility Overall User Requirement Specification.

3.1 Single-Use Technologies 3.1

Original Text

SUSs differ in many ways from conventional MUSs. When examining whether a technically feasible single-use solution exists, the end user should evaluate process operating parameters and requirements in relation to traditional systems:

  • Process and product are compatible with materials of construction
  • Process volume, temperature, pH, pressure, and flow rate are within specifications
  • Oxygen, moisture, or light barrier properties of the system are adequate for the process
  • Specific process components can be sterilized
  • Process monitoring and sampling strategy exists for the critical operating parameters
  • Access ports and connection technology are available to perform the operation
  • Mixing technology meets the uniformity needs of the process
  • Weight, size, and physical strength of SUS meet the needs of the process and facility

After an organization determines that a technical solution exists for a single-use solution, other considerations to be included in the final decision to implement an SUS are as follows:

  • Does the implementation of SUS impact existing process equipment that will need modification to optimize use of an SUS?
  • What is the impact on cost and material management of customization, given that custom manufacturing of small quantities is less effective for suppliers? Although there are advantages to using a supplier's standard configurations, in some cases the SUS itself may require customization to accommodate an individual organization's needs.
  • What is the potential impact on facility design and utility requirements?

The end user's approach to the determination of technical feasibility should be recorded in a risk-analysis document that is consistent with the methods outlined in ICH Q9, and it should include process-specific considerations such as:

  • The stage of the SUS application
  • Process performance or capability
  • Process scale
  • Product quality attributes
  • Batch production rates
  • Factors affecting system design (e.g., process compatibility, component reliability, facility design, storage and materials handling, and/or transportation)

3.2 Business Drivers for the Adoption of Single-Use Systems 3.2

Original Text

The decision between a single-use and a multiple-use process solution is often heavily influenced by a desire to reduce costs, increase the flexibility of a facility, and accelerate the drug development process. To that end, a structural comparative model that analyzes the cost of production, capital, quality, and process development, combined with the time constraints of the project, can be developed. The model's level of accuracy and detail should be suitable for the decision under consideration and should incorporate company-specific information wherever possible. A holistic model takes into account many factors that are useful for deciding if a greenfield facility is needed as opposed to a retrofit of existing applications. Often, a simplified model may be sufficient for an initial decision of whether SUS should be considered. The important point to consider is that an economic analysis needs to be done to support any important decision, and this is best done right from the start as part of developing a high-level manufacturing strategy.

The following lists include examples of elements that may be considered when evaluating the business rationale for implementation of an SUS.

Capital Design

  • Capital investment
  • Equipment costs
  • Utilities costs
  • Facility floor space (cleanroom versus lower classification, production area versus warehouse)
  • Validation—costs of validation clean-in-place and steam-in-place systems, facility environment
  • Cost of retrofitting the facility for a new product or purpose
  • Commissioning—costs associated with start-up and validation, testing, and documentation
  • Cost of capital—borrowing costs

Operating Costs: Day-to-Day Variable Costs

  • Labor
  • Utilities and energy
  • Waste management (solid and liquid, disposal versus recycling)
  • Sterilization
  • Cleaning
  • Setup and changeover time and labor
  • Amount lost to sterility failure or integrity losses
  • Operation model make or buy SUS
  • SUS run costs
  • Inventory
  • Media and buffer
  • Preventive maintenance
  • Value-added activities, quality audits of suppliers
  • Multiple-product (MP) or single-product (SP) facility, changeover, testing, and regulatory submissions

Drug Development and Process Assessment

  • Time-to-market and constraints
  • Number of batches per year
  • Number of products per year
  • Product lifecycle—estimates of product demand over time
  • Increased manufacturing time (may not fit existing manufacturing and quality processes)
  • Process complexity
  • Proximity to final drug presentation or container closures
  • Workforce skill level
  • Cost of active ingredients and intermediates
  • Health, safety, and the environment (HSE) (e.g., biosafety, toxicity, occupational health, and industrial hygiene)
  • Multiple-product or single-product facility

Time

  • Process uptime
  • Construction time
  • Time constraints
  • Regulatory milestones
  • Expected market competition milestones
  • Expected product launch date
  • Product demand growth rate
  • Inventory build time line
  • Equipment and component assembly

Green Manufacturing

  • Environmental concerns for manufacturing process
  • SUS waste generation
  • Resources for waste management
  • Design for sustainability

Value-Added Activities

  • Cost of quality and cost of failure
  • Quality, compliance, validation, calibration, operations
  • Outsourced activities, make versus buy SUS
  • Expedited transfer of manufacturing site to new location, reducing the time required to accommodate demand for additional product

Modeling will be highly dependent on specific circumstances and may result in different outcomes, dependent on model design, scale, timing, facility fit, product demand, and process frequency. For these reasons, it is not possible to make specific model design recommendations in this report. Every organization handles economic and process modeling differently. The concepts are presented in this report so the reader is aware that modeling plays an important role in supporting the decision process. Readers should reach out to others in their organization to decide how to best develop models for their organization. The following tools and resources may be useful to aid the decision process:

  • ICH Q9 Risk Assessment Tools, FMEA, fishbone, fault tree, HACCP
  • Comparative matrix
  • Internal cost databases
  • Cost-of-goods modeling
  • Comprehensive cost models

3.3 Qualification and Verification of Suppliers, Materials, Components, and Completed Assemblies 3.3 、、

Original Text

Any adverse effect on product quality and, hence, patient safety must be the main concern when the end user performs a risk assessment of an SUS. An assessment of critical operational parameters must be performed in order to determine the potential impact of an SUS on product quality. In this exercise, the end user must take into account the potential for interaction between process fluid and the materials of construction of the process equipment.

3.3.1 Product Risk

The potential impact of the SUS on the product quality is a key area to be assessed through a risk-based approach considering its proximity to the final product, the processing stage, and product requirements. The following are examples of specific considerations for potential interactions between SUS and product fluids:

  • An SUS is a defined system, which may minimize the risk of both microbial and chemical contamination.
  • SUSs are susceptible to generation of particulates during the fabrication and operation of the system.
  • SUS minimizes risk of cross-contamination when they are used once or in campaign manufacturing of a single drug substance.
  • An SUS will contribute to the leachables present in a process. An assessment of impact can be made by 1) Identifying potential leachables through an extractables study and 2) Conducting further studies should an extractables study identify materials of concern.

Product impact considerations typically include:

  • Sterility
  • Degradation, or binding to the product molecule
  • Adsorption of the product molecule or product pool
  • Adsorption of the functional component of the media or buffer
  • Interactions between leachables or their breakdown products with the product molecule or formulation or the process materials of construction
  • Technical limitations of the SUS, which could lead to suboptimal processing, poor cell growth, low yields, or impact to product-critical quality attribute (e.g., limitation of temperature control may impact MAB glycan profile)
  • Changes in the light, gas, moisture, or other barrier to the surroundings, which could cause oxidation or chemical reaction of the product molecule, product pool, or media components

In addition to product quality considerations, incorporating an SUS may affect key operating parameters at different process steps and potentially the control strategy for the entire process. An assessment for each process step, as well as the entire process, should include the following considerations:

  • Impact on system integrity (may be positive or negative)
  • Estimation of risk of equipment or system failure for both SUS and MUS, including downtime for correcting potential failures and backup equipment required (e.g., maintaining an ISO 5/Grade A-B area for connections if an SU connector fails or designing the connection for backup sterile tube welding if a connector fails)
  • Size limitations of an SUS as compared to an MUS
  • Productivity impacts when using SUS as compared to an MUS approach
  • Verification activities, such as system integrity or leaks, compared to an MUS approach
  • Supplier and vendor supply-chain transparency, change control, communication, product quality, and supply security
  • Proximity of the SUS to the final drug product

3.4 Process Control Strategy Considerations 3.4

Original Text

The monitoring capability of single-use and multiple-use systems may differ. Some points to keep in mind include the following:

  • When deciding between reusable and single-use probes, balance the cost per batch to generate the desired data, control of sensor calibration, cleaning validation requirements, and probe accuracy and reliability.
  • Consider and assess impact from potential differences in automation level; SUSs typically include more manual controls dependent on operator training and execution. Some SUSs may incorporate proprietary support framework and control systems at comparable costs to traditional process skids; however, this significantly limits SUS interchangeability. To avoid this problem, the end user may wish to adopt cross-platform supervisory control and data application (SCADA) systems, which provide greater flexibility and portability.
  • Consider that an SUS may affect how adjustments to the process are made (e.g., adjusting pH, adding solids, foam control); in some cases these adjustments may not be possible with SUS.
  • Note that qualifying and calibrating probes and the measurement system before use may be more complicated or not possible if a single-use probe is built into a presterilized system. Batch documentation practices for recording sensor calibration may be different for SUS.
  • Evaluate dependency on the supplier's and vendor's quality system and qualification activities.
  • Account for the possibility of smaller batch sizes with increased frequency or sub-batch or batch pooling due to smaller container volumes.

3.5 Implementation of a Single-Use System 3.5

Original Text

Implementation includes all of the activities required for the realization of the end user's manufacturing strategy. Since the manufacturing strategy is an assessment of feasibility informed by evaluation of the considerations from the sections on technology, business drivers, qualification, and implementation in this report, it provides a framework for implementation of the specific situation evaluated. However, each evaluation performed will be unique.

Furthermore, since time-to-clinic and time-to-market are becoming increasingly important factors for making implementation decisions, a comparative implementation assessment has become a crucial requirement. A well-thought-out and thorough implementation plan is important for successful implementation of an SUS. The implementation process is organized as a road map with a series of gateways between stages. The workflow should be organized around the following themes.

3.5.1 Stakeholder Management

Successful implementation of an SUS may require an active analysis of the effects of the implementation on the organization and stakeholder management. Broad implementation of SUS across an organization can impose certain organizational strains and stresses and problems may arise which have to be considered and dealt with.

3.5.2 Risk Management

The use of formal risk-assessment methods during SUS implementation provides a means for quantitative comparisons of risk related to MUS versus SUS to assist in decision making and stakeholder management.

3.5.3 Process Validation and Verification (PVV)

The PVV strategy for an SUS differs from that of an MUS. Therefore, the end user must be aware that traditional qualification activities may have limited value for an SUS implementation without the confidence that the supplier's production processes are of a high enough caliber and the end user's process is sufficiently robust to tolerate any variations of the SUS by the supplier.

3.5.4 Scoping

This segment of the implementation plan contains a detailed description of the technical scope of the SUS project; it complements the manufacturing strategy by providing global guidance and policies to be applied for SU applications across the project.

3.5.5 Project Execution Plan (PEP)

A PEP provides a detailed description of the project, the execution strategy, management, and compliance in order to attain project goals in terms of budget, time, and quality. This includes a policy for the identification and selection of suppliers, which will encourage the creation of a transparent relationship and help prevent the selection of equipment and suppliers from becoming an ad hoc process. An example PEP is shown in Section 9, Appendix II: SUS Facility Project Execution Plan Example.

3.5.6 End-User Requirements

This section of the plan provides a description of the end user's requirements for the SUS, including E&L and standardization in preparation for the equipment procurement process.

3.5.7 Testing and Documentation

Following delivery and installation, the user will develop methods and documentation for equipment SOPs; maintenance; qualification and process validation; the HSE plan; and personnel training.

3.5.8 Supplier Management

The effective management of SUS suppliers must include periodic review meetings involving the analysis of the supplier's performance during the previous period for quality and punctuality. Business review meetings should also include collaborative planning and effective forecasting of future demands in order to take effective advantage of the supplier's resources, lower the costs of maintaining capacity, and eliminate spikes in demand that may affect lead times. Appropriate supply and quality agreements must be in place to clearly communicate expectations to critical suppliers.

Drug master files provided by either the polymer compound or film supplier can be referred to, however, they may not exist for all applications and the level of detail of the information may vary by submission. The polymer compound formulations (type and levels of additives), for example, may only list the highest amount of the additives that can be included in the polymer rather than specifying the actual specification range of additives. In any case the information is not available to the end user so it is preferable to receive the information in the form of a regulatory support file.

3.5.9 Logistics Control Requirements

A logistics control strategy is a critical part of the implementation of an SUS because the end user relies on the supplier's production and quality systems to a higher degree than for the implementation of an MUS. The following factors are important for an effective logistics control strategy.

3.5.9.1 Inventory and Supply Chain Management

A thorough analysis of the entire supply chain is necessary in order to safeguard the supply of important materials for undisrupted SUS production. Such an analysis must encompass the supply and inventory strategy of the vendors who supply components and materials to SUS manufacturers in addition to the supply and inventory strategies of the SUS suppliers themselves. SUS suppliers have a responsibility to protect the security and supply of their finished goods by appropriate risk analyses and risk-mitigation strategies for the materials and components they purchase. It is incumbent on the end user to confirm that these strategies are adequate to maintain the continuity of supply. If the supplier's control strategies fall short of expectations, the end user may mitigate the risk of supply interruption by qualifying secondary sources or putting specific inventory management and supply agreements in place.

3.5.9.2 Waste

Operation of an SUS leads to an increased amount of solid waste compared to an MUS. An SUS waste-management strategy must include:

  • Collecting, storing, and inactivating waste in the facility
  • Avoiding cross-contamination
  • Monitoring the waste-management activities of subcontractors

3.5.9.3 Transportation

All aspects of transportation and holding must be understood for their role in maintaining integrity of the material throughout the supply chain. SU Technology requires additional care be taken during the entire supply chain; transportation, receiving, storage and delivery to the point of use. Some considerations are:

  • Maintain integrity of material throughout the supply chain.
  • An SUS must have a control strategy (such as supplier qualification, ID tests, or statistical analysis) of any product contacting materials to assure performance of critical components. Note that testing incoming components will in many cases be impractical beyond confirming the package and label, since SUS are typically not fully unpacked until the point of use.
  • Ensure proper warehousing and inventory management.
  • SU processes place additional demands on a warehouse. Consider shelf space and support functions for a warehouse and ways of improving delivery time, maintaining integrity, and documenting chain of custody from the vendor.

3.5.9.4 Single Suppliers

In cases where an SUS component, film, or integrated assembly may be unique and proprietary, making it difficult to find and qualify a second supplier, the risk (including mitigation efforts and additional equipment needed to ensure uninterrupted supply) must be recorded in a risk-assessment document.

In some cases, sole source situations may exist with intellectual property (IP) protections, which need to be addressed with special contractual provisions in the event that the supplier is no longer able to provide the SUS in the future.

3.5.9.5 Change Notifications

The end user must make sure that a change notification agreement is in place with suppliers (including third-party suppliers) and that such an agreement provides enough time to assess the effects of any changes on SUS functionality. Predictable, controlled transitions and ongoing operations of an SUS depend on this.

4.9 Storage

Original Text

Appropriate storage is necessary in order to maintain the quality of process solutions (e.g., buffers and culture media), intermediates, and bulk drug substance until they are ready for further processing. Long-term storage of process intermediates often requires rapid, controlled freezing or cryogenic preservation. The storage bag must be qualified for the specific use, with a focus on maintaining bag integrity at the lower of the storage or freezing temperatures.

4.9.1 Technological Examples

A storage bag with a polyethylene fluid contact layer is normally used to store aqueous-based liquid intermediates. Polyethylene polymers (particularly low-density grades) have broad chemical compatibility and very low reactivity to most intermediates and process fluids. Other materials like fluoropolymers are used to address specific process compatibility concerns.

Typical plastics used for freezing applications include poly(tetrafluoroethylene) (PTFE) and poly(ethylene vinyl acetate) (EVA). These materials are particularly good at retaining their mechanical strength at very low freezing temperatures. Specific bag systems have been integrated into commercially available controlled freeze-thaw systems. A gas barrier such as ethylene vinyl alcohol (EVOH) is typically used with EVA to create a multilayered composite film that offers mechanical strength and resistance to gas permeation.

For short-term storage, process intermediates in standard bags are normally kept at ambient temperature or in a refrigerator. Long-term storage of process intermediates often requires rapid, controlled freezing or cryogenic preservation (refer to Section 4.10, Freezing). 4.10 Freezing

Original Text

The end user may use freezing techniques to separate process pools or intermediates and hold them for a short time at the process temperature and conditions or for a longer duration before another production step. For long durations, the end user may freeze the intermediate directly in an SUS that is designed for freezing. Uncontrolled freezing is performed in standard laboratory freezers, purpose-designed blast cells, or nitrogen freezing chambers. Controlled freeze-and-thaw equipment (e.g., a plate freezer) is used to regulate the rate of freezing and thawing of the contents of each SU bag. To maintain the integrity of the bag containing the frozen process intermediates or bulk drug substances, careful attention must be paid to the reusable storage container design and logistics of shipping and handling.

4.10.1 Technological Examples

  • Unprotected small-scale bag: It is common practice to freeze an unprotected bag (less than 20 L) that is not specifically designed for the application. Compared to other technologies, the failure rates of frozen unprotected bags tend to be higher (Table 4.10.1-1🔍).
  • Separate bag shell — The purpose of a bag shell is to maintain the structural integrity of a frozen bag by protecting it from bumps and drops during handling at low temperatures. Bag shells are made of either rigid plastics or rigid foam construction.
  • Integrated shell-protected bag — The shell design includes a tubing and fitting compartment for security and storage at the most common point of failure. Off-the-shelf International Safe Transit Association (ISTA) and ASTM-qualified shipping containers can also be used.
  • Controlled freeze-thaw system, bag, frame, and logistics equipment — A controlled freeze-thaw system uses contact freezing technology to allow for the reproducible freezing of the contents of the bag. Contact freezing plates maintain freezing distances by maintaining the filled bag between two freezing contact plates. In addition, the rate of heat transfer from the bags is increased in a controlled freeze-thaw system, and this decreases the time required for freezing. Thus, a controlled freeze-thaw system is part of an integrated refrigeration system for maintaining the integrity of the bag and the quality of its contents during fill, transport, freezing, and thawing.

4.14 Drug Product Final Filling

Original Text

The impact of any failures in final fill operations is high, since further processing cannot reduce quality failures at this stage. Common strategies for reducing the risk of failure include performing filling operations in aseptic environments, use of physical barrier systems to separate operators from the process, and validating the sterility of process components (refer to Section 4.15, Isolators).

SU components are widely used in product final filling operations. For example, flexible plastic tubing is used between the filling pump head and needle. Another example is a peristaltic filling system, which employs disposable peristaltic tubing inside the pump head. Both types of tubing are normally replaced between batches.

4.14.1 Technological Examples

An SU dosing system comprises a product hold bag, supply tubing, and a filling needle. These components are assembled in a cleanroom and sterilized using gamma irradiation. The complete assembly is removed from its outer pouch and connected to the filling machine immediately before use. The filling machine may be as simple as a single pump or may contain multiple pumps to achieve commercial production speeds.

SU filling systems are controlled volumetrically or gravimetrically.

  • Volumetric dosing system — This type of system involves a volumetric system that consists of a peristaltic pump and specialized tubing, within which flows the product to be dispensed. Peristaltic pumps work by mechanically compressing the tubing within the pump head to push a specified volume of fluid through the dosing path. Accurate dosing depends on factors such as the mechanical life of the pump tubing. A positive displacement pump-based system relies on the use of either a rotary-piston or rolling-diaphragm pump design. In both cases, fluid is forced out of the pump by the action of the piston. Accurate dosing depends on the precision of the piston stroke length.
  • Gravimetric dosing system — A gravimetric system is normally used in low-throughput applications. It uses an optical sensor to dose a specified weight of the product based on the calculation of the interior volume of a given length of tubing or glass. The entire product path comprises SU components. The accuracy and precision of the system with aqueous solutions is comparable to that of other dosing systems. The dosing speed and accuracy are directly related to the fluid temperature and viscosity. Dosing time is the speed at which a liquid will flow through the tubing based on gravity; thus, viscosity influences dosing time. Relatively small temperature changes over the course of a filling event can affect product viscosity enough to have a significant effect on the dose volume.

5.0 Qualification and Verification of Suppliers, Materials, Components, and Completed Assemblies

5.1 Introduction

The qualification and verification of suppliers, materials, components, and completed assemblies require attention to several factors. It is important to use a risk-based and science-based approach to qualify both materials and completed assemblies for SUSs in pharmaceutical development and manufacture (18). The assessment of risk must be based on the complexity of the system and its intended use (e.g., product contact versus non-product contact, upstream versus downstream use, short-term contact versus long-term storage). See PDA's Technical Report No. 54 on Quality Risk Management for more information (7). In addition, scientific principles must be applied to 1) identify and monitor extractable materials from films and other components; 2) investigate potential interactions with product CQAs or significant process parameters like cell culture media; and 3) assess the effects of assembly processes such as welding, fusing, mechanical stress, and sterilization (e.g., gamma irradiation) on the materials during manufacturing. In assessing the suitability of the SUS with respect to potential leachables in the final product, extractables may be considered as potential in-process leachables. Finally, the design and functional integrity of the completed SUS must be verified and maintained by appropriately trained end users via suitable processes for construction, packaging, shipping, and deployment.

The supplier of the SUS must provide sufficient data to qualify the materials and components used to assemble the final SUS. In this regard, the end user may use the documents from the supplier to meet most of the verification criteria required to make sure that the SUS is fit for the intended application and purpose. This requires a thorough qualification of the supplier by the end user to ascertain an acceptable supplier quality system, documentation, and an appropriate level of technical capability that extends all the way to the original sources of materials to the supplier. In addition, the suppliers must be able to provide documented evidence of performance claims for off-the-shelf component designs for the SUS. A supplier quality agreement must underpin any custom designs that the end user may need from the supplier.

The first step in a science- and risk-based approach is to identify and assess the risks associated with using an SUS. The end user must identify that the SUS supplier's approach to the design and selection of materials of construction includes components and materials such as:

  • Filters
  • Sensors
  • Polymers
  • Lubricants
  • Slip agents

Supplier quality, manufacturing and analytical capabilities should also be evaluated. The end user must fully qualify and audit a number of potential suppliers before embarking on extensive studies of specific materials because it is preferable to have more than one supplier for the SUS.

It is important for the end user to have access to controlled documentation of the qualification studies conducted on the materials and components of the assembled SUS. Such documentation may include:

  • Batch numbers traceable to the raw materials
  • Product shelf life
  • Certificate of quality
  • Certificate of integrity
  • Endotoxin certification (to required and specific endotoxin level)
  • Animal-free certification
  • Sterilization certification (if sterility is claimed)

A robust change control communication and assessment process between the end user and the supplier (including sub-suppliers) must be established to monitor any changes in processing conditions, equipment, analytical methods, manufacturing site, resins, films, additives, and more. If the supplier does not have an appropriate assessment process in place, the end user may have to perform independent testing. After performing material and component qualification of the assembled products, a functional verification of the SUS should be performed according to the following:

  • Risk assessment — To confirm that the assembled products are fit for purpose
  • Process verification and validation — To confirm that the completed SUS functions within process constraints, including repetitive use or extended storage (if required)
  • Safety — To make sure that the SUS conforms to safety requirements as a pressure vessel (especially for larger volumes) or for containment in the event of rupture or leak

If the SUS assembly includes SU sensors, calibration and additional verification documentation must be supplied.

5.4 Supplier Qualification of Single-Use Systems 5.4

Original Text

Since a typical SUS installation requires the acquisition of several components from various external suppliers, it is important for the end user to qualify suppliers to ensure that they can deliver SU components that meet or exceed regulatory and customer expectations.

5.4.1 Supplier Audits and Technical Diligence

It is important to audit the supplier's quality systems as part of the supplier qualification process to ensure delivery of consistent SU components to the end user. An understanding of the supplier's capabilities may lead to a better understanding of end-user expectations and can help identify any gaps in the capabilities of the supplier. Therefore, the auditing step must encompass manufacturing and technical aspects, and both parties must agree on corrective actions as appropriate. Microbiological control measures for SUSs must be evaluated as part of a supplier audit.

The auditor should review the procedure and records for quarterly dose audits where the SUS is subjected to gamma-irradiation sterilization.

Standard auditing practices, extending to a review of quality systems and complaint handling, should be followed. Unique to SUSs for inclusion in audits would be the supplier's definition of change and how the change-control process functions, including customer notification. The specific particulate control and reduction schemes in place at the supplier should also be evaluated.

Many SUS suppliers may either be ISO 9001 or medical device manufacturers under FDA and ISO registration. ISO 9001 registration means a company operates in a quality management framework that has been audited by a registrar and confirmed as adhering to ISO 9001 guidelines (20). The manufacturing facilities of registered medical devices are subject to inspection and follow 21 CFR Part 820 (cGMPs for Medical Devices) and ISO 13485 (21,22). SUSs, including bulk drug containers, are considered process equipment rather than medical devices; therefore, the manufacturing facilities of SUS suppliers are not subject to regulatory registration or inspection. Although SUS manufacturers tend to design their quality systems along GMP guidelines, the auditor should not expect these systems to adhere strictly to cGMPs for drug or medical device manufacturing (21,23). ISO 9001 is considered a minimum requirement.

Pertinent factors for a technical diligence assessment and an interpretation of its implementation over the clinical development lifecycle are reviewed in greater detail in Section 7.7.3.

5.4.2 Supplier Quality Agreements and Responsibilities

The purpose of a supplier quality agreement is to set quality expectations, identify and develop a mutual understanding of pertinent regulations and guidance documents, and provide advice on managing quality concerns such as audits, complaints, and change control. Change control can be a complex process for an SUS because of the diverse number of suppliers (e.g., compounders, film producers, and bag assemblers) and materials (e.g., resins and additives) involved in the supply chain. The supplier must provide full transparency on the full supply chain of the SUS and guarantee quality oversight of the supply chain. The supplier must have a robust supply chain, which may require improvements, which can be developed with the support of the customer in a partnership mind-set. The extent to which the primary supplier manages the overall supply chain should be assessed, and if necessary, audit and technical diligence activities should be extended to the original supplier of materials and components. It is important to establish a cordial working relationship between the end user and supplier because an SUS supplier will inevitably become an extended part of the end user's manufacturing and quality systems.

5.5 Qualification of Alternative Suppliers 5.5

Original Text

The use of SUS permits considerable flexibility in the choice of materials, designs, and suppliers. This flexibility can be used to minimize risk by ensuring that multiple configurations are viable options for any given requirement. There are multiple combinations of films, bag designs, tubing, filters, connectors, and so on, that are capable of delivering or receiving a specified volume of process fluids to the required standard. Therefore, it is advisable for the end user to qualify an alternative supplier as part of the SUS risk management system (7). Risk- and science-based assessment must be used to determine the level of testing and validation required. In a case involving minimal risk, it may be sufficient to treat an SUS from a secondary supplier as representative of the same class of system or material from the primary supplier. The data generated for the original SUS can be used and compared to that from the secondary supplier. With higher-risk cases (e.g., bioreactor bags or product storage), the secondary supplier's component must be subjected to the same comprehensive qualification process as that of the primary supplier. Note that different suppliers may adopt different approaches to tackle a particular issue and still remain within the bounds of an acceptable supplier quality system. For instance, supplier A may test systems for bioburden from every lot whereas supplier B may test representative samples from different lots as proof of an overall bioburden management system. Both approaches may be acceptable and should be reflected in the user specifications (Table 5.5.2-1🔍). An industry effort to standardize SUS testing methods would improve end users' ability to compare alternative SUS suppliers and adequately assess testing differences between suppliers.

5.5.1 Qualification of Alternative Sources

End users typically have two options when it comes to alternative sources: 1) the same SUS supplier with alternative sources of raw materials, or 2) an alternative SUS supplier. Regardless of the choice, the qualification of an alternative source should be part of the drug substance manufacturer's risk-management system. The user should qualify the alternative supplier or raw material source in the same thorough way as the primary supplier or source.

With each option, the risk- and science-based assessment will continue to determine the level of testing and validation required.

The issues under consideration differ somewhat for each option. Suppliers may qualify alternate raw-material sources as part of their risk mitigation strategy at the level of the resins, additives, or the film itself (if sourced separately). For applications with low potential for process or product impact, it might be sufficient to review the data from the supplier's qualification. For applications with high potential for impact, the user may need to consider additional testing (e.g. cytotoxicity, stability). For films sourced separately, differences in formulations and manufacturing processes may occur, and the physiochemical properties and extractable profile of the alternate film can be quite different from the original. Such differences, however, should not be the deciding factor for the suitability of this alternative material for the SUS. The suitability of the alternative material should be determined by the end user depending on the intended use of the SUS.

For an alternative supplier, data generated for the original material can be used and compared to the data of the alternative supplier source. In high-risk cases (e.g., bioreactor bags or storage of bulk drug substance), the second supplier's component has to be treated as a specific instance, and full-scale qualification is required.

5.5.2 Interchangeability

It is important for end users to qualify alternative sources of films to safeguard against supply disruptions and possible shortages. In some situations this may be a minimal risk (e.g., sampling bag used for monitoring cell growth or nutrient conditions), so a comparison of extractables and leachables data from different suppliers may be acceptable; other situations may be more demanding (e.g., storage of bulk drug substance). SUS interchangeability can be considered if functionally equivalent substitution provides a contingency or process alternative while ensuring process performance and product quality. When considering SUS interchangeability, it is important to assess the component's intended function, anticipated processing conditions, and fitness for use (Table 5.5.2-1🔍). In every case, the approach taken should be supported by a sound risk- and science-based assessment with appropriate evaluation and performance verification for the type of substitution desired.

For example, a bag or assembly used simply as a container for process fluids with no product contact has limited critical attributes—the bag should not leak and there should be an acceptable leachables profile. If the assembly has the additional function of acting as a mixer, the design would need to be assessed for functional performance, in this case minimum mixing conditions (time, speed, and so forth, depending on the mechanism) to achieve homogeneity. Once the design is qualified, this would not need to be established for every lot. For an in-process product container potential interaction with the product would need to be examined and deemed appropriate for the conditions (e.g., liquid, frozen, storage time). The presence of particulates may also need to be assessed, depending on where the alternative component is used in the process. For a complex assembly such as a bioreactor, a full-scale assessment should be conducted on the design for performance parameters and attributes (mixing, pH control, sparging, temperature control, cell growth, viability, titer/yield, etc.). The potential impact of differences in growth conditions on product quality should be included.

*Risk Assessment Focus Descriptions:

  1. Design, dimension, performance, and materials claims of the proposed substitute (form, fit, and function)
  2. Materials of construction and process contact fluid compatibility, contact area, and time
  3. Availability and extent of supplier biosafety (USP <87> and/or <88>, as appropriate for the application) and extractables data (24,25)
  4. Suitability of supplier extractables data to bracket process fluid and conditions
  5. Application and potential for subsequent leachable removal or dilution
  6. Product impact assessment

When considering interchangeability, in most cases other than like-for-like, the end user will need to do some level of operational or extractable/leachable testing to verify fitness for use. Different dimensions or even top or bottom mixing can have product impact. As discussed in Section 5, Business Drivers for the Adoption of Single-use Systems, the cost of quality can be defined as "any cost that would not have been expended if quality were perfect." The additional end-user operational testing of SUS and related quality control activities to ensure process comparability for SU interchangeability could be included in the cost of quality and should then be considered in the overall SUS implementation costs. After all, if a supplier had perfect interchangeability, this end-user cost would be zero.

5.5.3 Using Supplier Quality Documentation

The end user may employ the supplier documentation as part of the SUS document verification process so long as the end user has audited the supplier and collected the requisite evidence of an acceptable supplier quality system, technical capability, and application of Good Engineering Practices (GEP) guidelines (Table 5.5.3-1🔍) (18). If there is not strict adherence to GEP, then there should be assurance that documentation is accurate and suitable to meet the purpose of verification.

5.6 Extractables and Leachables (E&L) 5.6 （E&L）

Original Text

Extractables may be defined as chemical entities that can be extracted from the components of a material by exertion of an exaggerated force (e.g., organic solvent, extreme temperature, ionic strength, pH, contact time). Leachables may be defined as chemical entities that can migrate from product-contact or non-product-contact surfaces into a process stream. Leachables may also include chemical entities that can migrate from contact surfaces into the bulk drug substance or final product under specified conditions of production, storage, and use. While leachables are considered a subset of extractables, they can also be derived from product-contact or non-product-contact materials, under typical process conditions and detected in final dosage. The evaluation of extractables is an essential step in the accurate prediction of leachables and in the selection of a suitable SUS dedicated to the manufacture of a given product. When chemical substances are identified that could be cleared or sufficiently diluted by downstream processes so as to be undetected, these can be termed as in-process leachables for the final dosage and may need to be accounted for during risk assessment. Refer to Table 4.8-1🔍 for a summary of common materials of construction and comments regarding their respective E&L properties.

Drug manufacturers are encouraged to perform a risk-assessment analysis as part of an E&L evaluation within the overall context of enhancing process performance and product safety and efficacy. The accumulation of extractables and leachables in a process fluid is governed by thermodynamic (i.e., the extent to which the materials would migrate) and kinetic (i.e., the rate at which materials would migrate) factors, as well as the amount of time during which such migration would occur. Higher temperatures increase the migration rate of leachables from the bulk of the plastic to the surface in contact with the process stream or formulation. The user can determine the worst-case extent of the extractables by determining the highest temperature at which extractions cease and the entire additive has been removed from the plastic. E&L risk factors can be generally categorized as risk to the patient and risk to the process. In evaluating risks, an extractable or leachable and the level at which it appears will need to be evaluated for impact on process and product quality and for patient safety. The following risk factors may be considered:

  • Proximity of the process steps undergoing a change to the final product
  • Storage and processing conditions (e.g., duration of exposure, temperature, pressure, pH extremes, buffer extraction propensity)
  • The type of process fluid (e.g., purification buffer versus formulated drug substance, presence of solubilizing agents)
  • Construction materials (e.g., PVC containers, bags, and tubing are prone to leaching diethylhexyl phthalate, which has adverse effects on the liver, testis, mammary, nerve, immune system, blood, and fatty tissue) (26,27).
  • Potential adverse effects, including synergistic and additive affects
  • Drug dose, mode, and frequency of administration
  • Therapeutic necessity of the drug (included in drug dose, mode, and frequency)

The presence of leachables during any stage of the production process or storage may pose a safety risk due to their potential to cause toxicity, carcinogenicity, immunogenicity, and/or endocrine dysregulation (28,29). In addition, these substances may adversely impact the physicochemical characteristics (e.g., via aggregation, oxidation, degradation, and formation of particulates) of the final protein product. It has been demonstrated that leachables have adverse effects on cell growth and viability (30). Lastly, the potential for leachables to impact quality control tests should be considered. For these reasons, proper selection and handling of an SUS that supports the production and processing of safe and efficacious therapeutic biologic products is of paramount importance.

Potential sources of extractables and leachables include SU materials used in manufacturing, packaging, storage, filtration, and transfer systems. These include but are not limited to SU bioreactors, bags for intermediate and long-term storage, containers, filters, transfer tubing, valves, fittings, housings, and so on. Extractables and leachables are chemically diverse compounds that may include but are not limited to phthalates (e.g., plasticizers), organic acids, hydrocarbons, cyclic esters derived from urethane adhesives, antioxidants, residual solvents, antistatic and cleaning agents, as well as trace metals and degradation products from irradiation.

5.6.1 Material and Supplier Qualification

It is important for end users to qualify alternative sources of films to safeguard against supply disruptions and possible shortages. There will be situations where this may be a minimal risk (e.g., bags used for sampling), so a comparison of extractables data from different suppliers may be acceptable; other situations may be more demanding (e.g., storage of bulk drug substance). In every case, the approach taken should be supported by a sound risk- and science-based assessment.

Detection, characterization, identification, and quantitation of leachables may present analytical challenges, since these substances represent diverse chemical classes of organic and/or inorganic compounds that coexist in complex mixtures in trace amounts (31). Therefore, well-designed extractables studies are important to provide an analytical road map and to identify early warning signs regarding leachables released during upstream or downstream operations or in storage. Emerging analytical technologies with appropriate sensitivity and specificity may be considered in addition to the currently available methods during the design of such studies. If an extractable may have an adverse effect on the physicochemical or biological properties of a protein, performing characterization studies in which the extractable is spiked into the product may be of value in assessing the potential risk to product quality.

In order to isolate chemicals from plastic components, the study of extractables begins with the selection of a suitable exaggerated condition that is not stipulated for the normal manufacture, storage, or use of the SUS. It is recommended that the extraction studies be performed with material components using the exaggerated conditions with regards to pH, ionic strength, contact time, temperature, surface area–to–volume ratio, and, if applicable, organic solvents of varying polarity and surfactant properties as the extraction media. If the material component in question is not compatible with organic solvents, aqueous media may be used. If a prerinse step is used in routine manufacturing and use of material component, it should be included in the design of the leachables studies but not in the extractables studies because the latter are intended to represent a worst-case situation. Additional pretreatment of plastics such as gamma irradiation and steam treatment can also impact the E&L profile.

A representative sample of process fluid (e.g., cell culture media and buffer) can be used as the extraction media in addition to others listed above for accurate prediction of extractables. If a representative process fluid cannot be obtained (e.g., due to interference with the analytical method), a similar solution to the original may be an acceptable alternative. The extraction study may be performed using a soaking or a recycling method for a specific contact time, under accelerated temperature conditions and preselected surface area–to–volume ratio. USP chapters <661> and <381> contain recommendations for extraction conditions with regards to surface area–to–volume ratio, temperature, and time of extraction (32,33). The USP <661> monograph also includes general analytical testing for the content of nonvolatile residue, residue on ignition, buffering capacity, and heavy metals. However, because such methods are not equipped to identify specific contaminants, their scope and applicability may not be sufficient in every instance. Several industry organizations, such as BioPhorum Operators Group (BPOG) and the Bio-Process Systems Alliance (BPSA), have developed standardized testing approaches. Please see the latest protocols at their websites as they are periodically updated (33-36).

Testing of E&L substances includes the analysis of nonvolatile, semivolatile, and highly volatile organic compounds as well as analysis of trace light and heavy metals. Less specific tests such as pH, conductivity, chemical identity via Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, and analysis of total organic carbon (TOC) may be performed. TOC analysis cannot be used with extract solvents that contain carbon. Highly selective analytical techniques for the detection, characterization, and quantitation of these chemical entities include high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with mass spectroscopy (HPLC-MS), gas chromatography coupled with mass spectroscopy (GC-MS), inductively coupled plasma with mass spectroscopy (ICP-MS), proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H-NMR), and atomic spectroscopy (e.g., atomic absorption and atomic emission spectroscopy). For instance, nonvolatile compounds may be analyzed with HPLC-MS, whereas highly volatile and semivolatile organic compounds may be resolved with GC-MS. Other methods such as ICP-MS may be used for the detection of residual metals.

The extractables study must be complemented with an appropriately designed leachables study. In addition to monitoring the substances that are leaching under the recommended conditions of use and storage, leachable studies must be designed to identify any interactions and the resulting effect of such interactions on the in-process material and/or on the product. In many cases, drug substance and drug product stability studies may be used to support conclusions concerning the effects of these substances on product quality over time. This is particularly important where there is no downstream purification process to eliminate impurities (e.g., drug product formulation buffer storage container).

In addition to determining the chemical identity, quantity, and composition of E&L, and the impact on process fluid or product quality, E&L must be assessed for cytotoxicity and toxicity in animals in order to preserve patient safety and clinical efficacy (24,25). This information may be obtained from publicly available literature sources such as ICH Q3C (R3) or Product Quality Research Institute Leachables and Extractables Working Group, which stipulates the safety threshold levels for such impurities (37,38). In the absence of specific toxicity data, the threshold of toxicological concern (TTC) approach may be applied in order to judge the risk associated with a particular leachable.

When an SUS is used to grow cell culture, the end user must determine the effect of cell growth on both individual components and the complete SUS. This is important because a recent study showed that the breakdown products of gamma-irradiated antioxidants can have adverse effects on the viability of the cell culture. Spiking studies showed that the phenomenon can occur at levels well below parts per million in a cell line-specific manner (39).

Cell-based assays can be used to identify the particular combinations of cell lines and plastic extractables that may reduce cell culture viability and growth profiles. End users must evaluate the compatibility of the supplied SUS with their specific application, because certain applications (e.g., media storage, bioreactor bags) have been reported to be sensitive to certain combinations of materials and cell lines (30).

The mechanism of interaction of the SUS and contact solution can include leaching from the plastic into the contact solution or the adsorption of the contained solution or its constituents onto the SUS. These interactions can be modeled through science-based assessments of the physicochemical interactions of the solution and SUS. 5.9.4 Bioburden Control 5.9.4

5.10 Sterilization 5.10

5.10.1 Irradiation Sterilization 5.10.1

Original Text

SUSs are especially suitable to sterilization by irradiation because of the relative transparency of plastics to ionizing radiation, which allows complex systems to be sterilized without the risk of spreading the sterilizing agent throughout the fluid path. Irradiation may be used to sterilize plastics or SUS assemblies that may be unsuitable for steam sterilization. The practice of sterilizing via irradiation is well established in the medical device industry with transferable standards that can be applied to SU process equipment (51). Although no specific industry standard has been developed for the sterilization of SUSs, the industry and regulatory communities have adopted the ANSI/AAMI/ISO 11137 standard, Sterilization of Health Care Products—Radiation, and the closely related AAMI Technical Information Report TIR 33:2005, Substantiation of a Selected Sterilization Dose—Method VD max (51,52). Adaptation and implementation of these standards to the implementation of SUS have been discussed in the literature (53). The most common alternative methods mentioned in the standards are the dose substantiation methods (i.e., the verification dose max, or "VD max," methods).

In practice, a typical audit involves the selection of a single-use product (SUP) as representative of the SUS. A supplier quality audit must consider how the product family and the representative product design were derived. In cases where testing the representative product would be impractical (e.g., due to size and complexity), a portion of the sample item may be used with mathematical scaling between the actual product and the sample item. A supplier quality audit must include an understanding of the mathematical scaling technique. Since the standards require that a supplier must perform quarterly dose audits for each product family, the end-user quality audit of the SU supplier must ascertain that there is a procedure in place for the supplier to conduct quarterly dose auditing. Validation normally involves the determination of an average bioburden level in a representative master system, assessment of radiation resistance by sterility testing of the fluid path of the master system after a low "verification" dose (about 8 to 10 kGy), and calculation of a suitable dose to provide a sterility assurance level or a probability of nonsterility of 10-6 for the model product and its variants. The supplier and the end user should review the applicability of validation via master system sterilization to the end user's SUS configuration.

5.10.2 Sterilization with Moist Heat 5.10.2

Original Text

Several methods of sterilization are acceptable for use with SUS. Each is appropriate for different applications and should be evaluated for suitability.

  • Autoclaving — This technique is often used to sterilize an SUS or SU components assembled by end users (54). After that, most end users may find it more convenient to source preassembled SUSs that are sterilized by gamma irradiation.
  • Sterilize-in-place (SIP) — Steam-to-connector configuration is one example of SIP. The physical limitations of plastics limit the ability of an SUS to be sterilized by steam pressure in situ. Some SU connectors are designed to be physically attached to a stainless multiple-use port so that the tank connection can be sterilized with steam. Steam-to or steam-through connectors tend to be preconnected to tubing with an SUS at one end and are presterilized by autoclaving or irradiation. A valve or barrier mechanism is used to isolate the sterile system. The steam-to or steam-through connector is then connected to a tank, and the interface is sterilized with steam (55).
  • Gas sterilization — Although it is not a common practice, an SUS may be sterilized using a gas such as ethylene oxide. Gas sterilization is not as popular as other methods of sterilization for two reasons: 1) ethylene chlorohydrin and ethylene glycol reaction by-products can form; and 2) some ethylene oxide may be retained in the filter matrix, when a filter is present. Some of the factors that affect the use of gas sterilization are preconditioning, concentration, relative humidity, temperature, and time. The monitoring and validation of gas sterilization is conducted by destroying a suitable biological indicator such as the spores of Bacillus subtilis. To achieve successful sterilization using ethylene oxide, the SUS should be dry and wrapped in a way that allows for the influx of water vapor and gas into the fluid path. The appropriate concentration of gas and other parameters must be established during development and validation processes. It is important to rid the SUS of residual gas and other undesirable by-products after gas sterilization.
  • Resterilization — SU components are not amenable to multiple autoclave cycles. Gamma irradiation is a cumulative process and most SU components cannot be re-irradiated without causing significant degradation of physical integrity, creation of extractables, and reduction of shelf life. The end user is responsible for process validation, and the method for validating an SUS must be determined using a risk-based approach, which considers the effective integration of the SUS design into the end user's quality systems (8). 5.11 Integrity 5.11

5.11.1 Testing an SUS for Leaks 5.11.1 SUS

Original Text

The requirements for testing an SUS will depend on its intended use. The retention of structural integrity (i.e., containers do not leak) is the main concern and is a key concern in process-contact applications. For low-risk applications (e.g., sample and buffer bags), an understanding of supplier testing, performance, and training in appropriate handling procedures may be sufficient.

SUSs are increasingly used for the production of vaccines, and the egress of a live virus could have serious consequences; therefore, specific testing of individual systems is recommended.

When an SUS is used for holding media, the potential for microbial ingress that may compromise sterility becomes a significant concern. Attempts have been made to assess the maximum size of a defect that will compromise the effectiveness of a flexible bag (56). This is a complex undertaking because it depends on factors such as the surface tension of the liquid in the bag, time, temperature, and the method of measurement. In addition, the permeability of individual components (e.g., tubing and films) may differ according to the test agent (e.g., gas or dye) used.

The structural integrity of the SUS should be carefully evaluated in a collaborative manner by the end user and the supplier. Post-shipment/transport testing to evaluate the method of packaging the SUS will need to be incorporated. To test for the integrity of the fluid path during qualification—including tubing manifolds and connectors—a suggested method is to internally pressurize tubing with air and hold it under water. Provided that testing will not compromise the sterility or cleanliness of the equipment, manifolds can be inspected by manipulation to check for air bubbles at both tubing connector engagements and along the entire length of tubing. For upstream applications, the user should confirm the supplier's claims during verification and accept materials based on their documentation thereafter. A special case may need to be made for customized SUS. Demands are generally stricter for product containers; therefore, end users may wish to ask the suppliers what testing is feasible for larger containers at their sites versus the end user's site (after installation).

To test a bag container or a SUS for leakage, a suggested method (depending on the size of the SUS) is to fill the plastic bag or SUS with water inside the bin, drum, or tank and check for leaks at maximum volume. If the location of the leak is not apparent, fill the plastic container with water outside of the bin, drum, or tank on a flat, absorbent surface such as cardboard or paper. Locate the leak by identifying wet spots on the absorbent surface corresponding to a position on the container. Using the mapped location of the leak on the bag, examine the bin, drum, or tank for sharp points that may have caused the leak. Examine the leak under magnification to determine whether the leakage was caused by puncture, tear, creasing, pinhole, or another means. Track leak locations to help with root cause analysis of failure modes (e.g. handling, structural/design considerations, and material failure).

If the end user determines that a leak was caused by a manufacturing defect, the failed sample should be returned to the supplier for quality investigation and corrective action. The returned sample should include full information regarding the conditions of use and location of the leak. Suppliers should maintain records of system failures and their root causes for further analysis.

6.2 Lifecycle Approach 6.2

Original Text

The importance of specific business drivers will depend on the needs of each individual company. The scope of the project may be as narrow as using an SUS to boost the capacity of a single process step or to eliminate cleaning steps. Such a tactical comparison depends on technological, process compatibility, and economic factors. Conversely, the project scope could be a corporate-wide initiative to evaluate the feasibility of SUS implementation for an entire production process or part of an overall lifecycle evaluation for a product or facility. A lifecycle approach involves an evaluation of the costs incurred throughout the lifetime of the project in order to provide a complete economic basis for making decisions. Costs for evaluation may include the initial capital investments, production and quality overheads, and decommissioning. The lifecycle approach incorporates time and risk factors as relevant strategies are applied, and this leads to a more comprehensive economic evaluation.

For instance, the forecast for a new product may include an initial launch phase with a relatively uncertain production demand followed by a full-scale production phase with options for capacity expansion or additional products. In such a situation, operational flexibility becomes important to the business case because of the uncertainty in product demand. Such flexibility comes with an increase in capital investment for traditional MUSs, whereas the implementation of an SUS tends to reduce the cost of operational flexibility because it is less reliant on investment for utilities and equipment installation. An SUS is less costly to modify or expand to a new facility when a change in product lifecycle occurs.

6.4 Cost of Quality 6.4

Original Text

The cost of quality can be defined as any cost that would not have been expended if quality were perfect. The inherent advantages of an SUS may lead to reductions in the cost of quality for the end user. In order for an SUS to be used in a manufacturing process, the end user must be able to demonstrate that it is fit for purpose and meets applicable quality and regulatory requirements. It is the responsibility of the end user to ensure that the SUS meets applicable quality standards. Any tests conducted by the end user to ascertain process compatibility due to changes made by the supplier may be included in cost-of-quality estimates.

6.8.1 Process Equipment

The first step in evaluating an SU project is normally an equipment-to-equipment comparison of SUS versus MUS direct investment cost. Reduced demand for cleaning and sterilization allow for simplifications in piping and instrumentation, which leads to a reduction in the investment cost for SUSs. 6.8.2 Utility Equipment

The effect of utility systems on the investment costs should be explored because it may be possible to remove entire system groups from the scope (e.g., clean steam, CIP stations) or achieve enough reduction in capacity to obtain cost reductions (WFI for rinsing, kill systems). 6.8.3 Indirect Equipment Costs

The estimates for engineering and installation costs (e.g., design, commissioning, and qualification and validation) are normally calculated as a percentage of the equipment costs. One of the advantages of an SUS is that reductions in piping and instrumentation may decrease the number of both installed hardware and communication I/O points in the automation system. However, an SUS may incur a higher engineering percentage cost compared to similar MUS designs because of lower initial investment costs and the cost of validating leachables and extractables for the SUS. The installation percentage is lower for an SUS than an MUS. A similar approach can be used to determine the percentage costs for construction and project management (60).

6.8.4 Facility

An SUS uses stackable rectangular containers, which results in a smaller footprint compared to MUS. The absence of a CIP and washing/sterilization function further reduces the space requirements of SUS. However, inefficient movement of containers, installation, and dismantling of bags can easily waste the space saved by an SUS. In addition, proper storage and handling of an SUS is very important, since any damage might result in a lost batch or contamination. The transport route of an SUS may increase compared to that of a hard-piped system, and an SUS requires space to store process waste prior to final disposal. Storage space for SUS is typically greater than that for traditional MUS because of the need to manage SUS inventory and associated reusable storage containers. The main contributions to variable operating costs are summarized in Table 6.8.4-1🔍 (61).

6.8.5 Materials

Often, input materials (e.g., SUS materials prefilled with hydrated or powdered media and buffer components and chromatography gels) may be independent of SUS or MUS technology (62).

6.8.6 Consumables

The best way to determine the component cost of consumables is from the specific SUS process design under consideration because there can be a significant discrepancy between the costs of a supplier's standard configuration and that of the specific assembly required for a particular process step. Whenever possible, using the supplier's standard configurations would be the most cost-effective way to determine the cost of consumables. Designing the SUS with backup systems such as redundant tubing, filters, and connectors will increase the costs per batch of that SUS. However, if these backup systems are necessary, they must be reflected in the final cost analysis of the project.

It is possible to minimize SUS overdesign via a thorough understanding of the real risks determined in a process risk assessment such as a failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA) (7). It is also important to get a feel for the actual cost of the SUS once specific connectors, filters, and tubing sets are added to the design. Since it may be possible to obtain discounts with large-volume orders from prospective suppliers, it is necessary to understand SUS configurations and how they can be customized to meet individual SUS process requirements. It would be advisable to incorporate a sensitivity analysis for the purchase cost of an SUS because final configurations and prices can be difficult to predict. In general, the sensitivity analysis should be extended to any variable with uncertain cost estimates (63).

6.8.7 Utilities

The reductions in cleaning and sterilization associated with an SUS leads to a decrease in the cost of utilities, especially for clean water generation, CIP, and SIP systems. It is important to use carefully verified cost data since large volumes may be involved in these processes. The cost of water for injection is significantly lower for large on-site systems than for commercially supplied WFI. It may be calculated from system depreciation, labor, and energy costs.

6.8.8 Waste

The cost of inactivating solid waste can be significant depending on the degree of containment required. The solid waste may be 1) inactivated on-site, which will lead to increased load on kill autoclaves; or 2) transported off-site for incineration or disposal at a landfill. Both approaches would incur transport and disposal fees. A third approach involves chemical inactivation. The disposal strategy for each SUS must comply with the laws and requirements of the municipal authority in charge (64–66).

7.2.3 Process Validation and Verification (PVV) 7.2.3 （PVV）

Original Text

In pharmaceutical manufacturing, process validation (PV) is the collection and evaluation of data from the process design stage through commercial production that establishes scientific evidence that a process is capable of consistently delivering quality product (8). The U.S FDA and EMA consider PV a requirement in both general and specific terms in Good Manufacturing Practice (74,75). Verification is a systematic approach for ensuring that manufacturing systems (acting either singly or in combination) are suitable for their intended use, have been properly installed, and are operating correctly. This is an umbrella term that encompasses all types of activities to assuring systems are fit for use, such as qualification, commissioning and qualification, verification, system validation, and others (18). Verification (or process verification) is an important concept for SUSs and is a primary control strategy for sourcing from suppliers. This remains active throughout the lifecycle of the project.

PVV is a tool that requires an integrated project management approach that involves all pertinent stakeholders (e.g., a cross-company project team) and full support from management. A science- and risk-based approach to PVV tends to generate a thorough understanding of the manufacturing process, which in turn enhances product quality and patient safety (8,74). ICH guidance documents, such as ICH Q8, Q9, and Q10, provide useful guidelines for development, risk analysis, and product control (3-5). A draft guideline on process validation for the manufacture of biotechnology-derived active substances and a guideline on process validation for finished products has been published by EMA (75,76).

Continuous quality verification can also be used. 7.4.8 Operational-Level Integration Plan 7.4.8

Original Text

This describes the parameters for SUS integration (i.e., assembly, connection, and deployment) in readiness for manufacturing. A risk-based approach should be used because of the structural and functional diversity of SUSs (91). Some of the important parameters to consider during deployment include:

  • Packaging and container integrity — It must be determined whether preuse leak testing of SUS is feasible. Manufacturing, shipping, handling, and operational procedures should be qualified to maintain SUS integrity. This can be achieved by implementing control strategies within the end user's organization and between supplier and end user.
  • SUS testing and release — Testing and release vary with use. The same off-the-shelf system may have different test and release criteria depending on process-, product-, regulatory-, and user-imposed requirements.
  • Reuse of components — In general, reuse of SUSs is not recommended. The policy for this should have been established in the SUS strategy (Section 7.2.1.7). A risk analysis should be performed to determine if the risks are acceptable.
  • Training — Appropriate personnel training is essential because the collapse of a bag may lead to stress cracks that could result in a loss of structural and functional integrity. Training topics might include the use of different sterile connection and disconnection devices or techniques for avoiding bag creases or holes during deployment.
  • Shelf life — A coherent expiration and shelf-life policy should be implemented, especially in situations in which the end user has a large number of different SUSs from multiple suppliers or local, self-made assemblies.
  • Quality assurance — Ensuring quality during deployment should be addressed at an early stage of implementation. Technical diligence may enhance this goal (Section 7.4.10 and 7.7.3).

7.4.10 Supplier Selection and Supply-Chain Review 7.4.10

Original Text

The aim of this review is to determine a policy for the identification and selection of suppliers in order to complete the PEP and project planning.

The selection of appropriate SUS suppliers can often be an arduous task that can be made less cumbersome with a risk-management approach. For a successful SUS implementation, it is essential to have a detailed and transparent relationship between suppliers, vendors, and the end user. This requires good communication and understanding of stakeholder management in order to garner the expectations of both the suppliers and the end users. Being able to share pertinent information with suppliers at the early stages of the project may speed up the design process. Note that in order to obtain full benefits from this approach, the exchange of uni- or bilateral confidential disclosure agreements (CDA) may be required. The implementation and management of an end user–supplier relationship via technical diligence is described in Section 7.7.3. Suppliers provide material data, advice on applying typical designs, and the following documents:

  • Process flow diagrams (PFDs)
  • Piping and Instrumentation Diagrams (P&IDs)
  • Component lists
  • Physical layout proposals
  • Operation manuals
  • Pricing
  • Qualification packages, including guidelines on E&L, for example

For both large companies and SMEs, it is critical to prevent the selection of equipment and suppliers from becoming an ad hoc process because that can lead to unnecessary and costly redundancies. In addition, working with too many suppliers may divert limited resources that could otherwise be gainfully employed in other parts of the SUS implementation process. Since the degree of business integration of a particular SUS supplier may depend on the size, location, and SUS assembly concerned, it is important to adopt a risk-mitigation approach to SUS supplier qualification and validation (Section 7.7.3).

Starting materials such as resins and films should be treated as critical raw materials as part of the verification process due to the complexity of SUS implementation (18). It is important to begin the supplier technical diligence (i.e., obtaining a thorough understanding of the SUS product, supplier, and compatibility of the SUS with the end user's process and facility) at this stage. These principles are based on established procedures for critical raw materials as recommended by the Association for Professionals in Infection Control and Epidemiology (APIC) due diligence process, ASTM E2500-07 vendor technical capability as part of the PVV process (Figure 7.2.7.1-1), and quality by design (QbD)-inspired raw materials qualification processes (18,92–94). Full details on how to implement the technical diligence process are described in Section 7.7.3. Note that this task may require the exchange of uni- or bilateral CDAs or nondisclosure agreements (NDA).

The quality of the entire supply chain should be assessed, qualified, and implemented via a structured risk-management process (72). All parties in the supply chain should make sure that their activities maintain business continuity and support the health of patients. SUS equipment that is purchased occasionally may be done on a contractual basis, but a quality and supply agreement must be used to buy routine SUS equipment (Sections 7.5.7 and 7.7.12).

7.5.1 User Requirements for Implementation 7.5.1

Original Text

User requirements should be prepared at the start of the development process. The principal user and stakeholders must be identified and their requirements obtained. 9.5.4 Commissioning and Qualification

Execution of C&Q is described in the commissioning and qualification plan GMPX-C&Q-07-001 and in the QAP Doc. no. GMPX-QAP-07-001. The execution can be summarized as follows.

9.5.4.1 Factory Acceptance Test (FAT) and Site Acceptance Test (SAT) for Systems Assessed as Indirect Impact and Direct Impact

Suppliers and contractors of indirect- and direct-impact systems must, if applicable according to SQP during FAT/SAT, demonstrate to the SEC that all requirements, including functions' "as-built" documentation, are fulfilled and available and that the deliverables operate as required. In situations where the supplier or contractor is not in a position to perform FAT/SAT as expected, the SEC, in cooperation with the supplier or contractor, must perform additional tests, possibly as commissioning.

FAT/SAT shall be prepared in a manner that ensures that all C&Q requirements are tested and documented and fulfill the acceptance criteria at the completion of SAT.

When the machine or product has been assembled at the site and adequately connected and run-in tested by the supplier or contractor, the SAT is carried out. This takes place in a way similar to the FAT, according to SEC-approved SAT protocols. For indirect-impact and direct-impact systems, SEC QA should review protocols and test plans in the planning of SAT to ensure that approval of the SAT report is obtainable. The SAT is finished with the SEC and SEC QA approval of the SAT report.

9.5.4.2 Commissioning Indirect-Impact Systems and Direct-Impact Systems

For systems assessed as direct or indirect impact, fulfillment of C-requirements shall be demonstrated through commissioning. The work package owner (WPO) has the responsibility to ensure that commissioning activities are well planned and adequately executed, documented, and reported. The Q-coordinator is responsible for overall coordination and follow-up.

Vendor SAT is part of the commissioning.

9.5.4.3 No-Impact Systems

For systems assessed as having no impact, fulfillment of requirements must be demonstrated and documented according to GEP based on the tradition in the discipline.

The WPO is responsible for planning and executing the commissioning activities for no-impact systems and to ensure that the activities are documented in a way that allows the SEC to illustrate that all requirements are met.

9.5.4.4 Qualification

For systems assessed as direct impact, fulfillment of Q-requirements in relation to installations and operations shall be demonstrated through installation qualification and operational qualification (IQ/OQ). Qualification and especially prerequisites before the qualification can start shall be described in the protocols. IQ and OQ must be carried out according to the approved protocols.
