Introduction

Original Text

This document represents the views of the Parenteral Drug Association (PDA). The document is designed to communicate PDA's thoughts and considerations pertaining to the design, operation, and use of Restricted Access Barrier Systems (RABS) for aseptic processing, processes subjected to terminal sterilization and low bioburden, while encouraging further dialog with industry, health authorities, and suppliers of technologies and materials. This document considers the evolution and needs of the modern, global, sterile, healthcare product manufacturing industry. This document does not represent a standard or regulatory guidance.

In 2020, PDA issued Points to Consider for the Aseptic Processing of Sterile Pharmaceutical Products in Isolators, which focus on important regulatory and technology updates surrounding isolator design, validation, and operation for aseptic processing. Several other PDA documents equally address many of the principles for working and operating with RABS, including Points to Consider No. 1: Aseptic Processing (Revised 2023) or Technical Report No. 62 Recommended Practices for Manual Aseptic Processes. Additionally regulatory guidance documents as the European Commission's EU Guidelines for Good Manufacturing Practice for Medicinal Products for Human and Veterinary Use Annex 1: Manufacture of Sterile Medicinal Products (2022), U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Guidance for Industry: Sterile Drug Products Produced by Aseptic Processing-Current Good Manufacturing Practice (2004) and others are addressing the special requirements for RABS. While many of the recommendations and principles from these documents are equally applicable to RABS, until this point, there was no equivalent guideline or Points to Consider (PtC) dedicated to RABS for which technology and operational specific nuances apply. Hence, a taskforce was established to focus on industry best practices, regulatory and technology considerations directly relevant to the application and use of RABS in an aseptic manufacturing setting.

A fundamental principle of sterile product manufacturing is to minimize the risks of contamination (microbiological and particulate) through appropriate facility, equipment, and manufacturing process design, and qualification. The use of barrier systems, including RABS, to increase the protection of the product is not only recommended but should be considered in the context of an overall contamination control and risk-reduction strategy. RABS technology provides for increased product protection compared to non-barrier systems used for Grade A and Grade B aseptic manufacturing operations. RABS is a very different technology to isolators and, consequently, key design, regulatory and operational differences, and considerations apply, most notably background environments, cleaning, disinfection and decontamination methods, aseptic setup, and strictly defined conditions and controls governing open-door RABS interventions.

While the scope of the document relates to considerations of RABS employed for aseptic processing, the principles contained within are equally applicable to RABS for other applications, including products subjected to terminal sterilization and low bioburden processes. Glossary

Original Text

Definitions have been provided to help clarify the concepts discussed in this document. While some definitions used vary among companies, geographic location, etc., the definitions described below are consistently used within this Points to Consider document. Where a definition is based on another published source, the source is cited.

Aseptic Process Simulation (APS)

A simulation of the entire aseptic manufacturing process in order to verify the capability of the process to assure product sterility. Includes all aseptic operations associated with routine manufacturing, e.g. equipment assembly, formulation, filling, lyophilization and sealing processes as necessary (1).

Bio-decontamination

A process that eliminates bioburden via use of a sporicidal chemical agent (1).

Note: In the context of open RABS (oRABS), this is a manual treatment process with sporicidal agents.

Biological Indicator (BI)

Test systems that contain viable microorganisms with a defined concentration and resistance to a specific decontamination process. They help monitor whether the necessary conditions were met to kill a specified number of microorganisms for a given process.

Campaign

Processing of multiple lots or batches of the same product serially in the same manufacturing line.

Contact Time

The minimum amount of time that a sanitizer, disinfectant, or sporicide must be left in complete (wet) contact with the surface to be treated in order to be effective (2).

Note: This is typically referred to as "wet contact time".

Critical Zone

A location within the aseptic processing area in which product and critical surfaces are exposed to the environment (1).

Critical Surfaces

Surfaces that may come directly into contact with, or directly affect, a sterile product or its containers or closures. Critical surfaces are rendered sterile prior to the start of the manufacturing operation, and sterility is maintained throughout processing (1).

Decontamination

The overall process of removal or reduction of any contaminants (chemical, waste, residue, or microorganisms) from an area, object, or person. The method of decontamination used (e.g., cleaning, disinfection, sterilization) should be chosen and validated to achieve a level of cleanliness appropriate to the intended use of the item decontaminated (1). See also Bio-decontamination.

Disinfection

The process by which the reduction of the number of microorganisms is achieved by the irreversible action of a product on their structure or metabolism to a level deemed to be appropriate for a defined purpose (1).

Note: In this document, it is understood that sanitizers and disinfectants are used in the same context.

First Air

Refers to filtered air that has not been interrupted prior to contacting exposed product and product contact surfaces with the potential to add contamination to the air prior to reaching the critical zone (1).

Format Parts

Container specific parts (e.g., based on different container sizes) that allow the proper guidance/transfer of product containers through a manufacturing line.

Grade A Air Supply

Air which is passed through a filter qualified as capable of producing grade A total particle quality air, but where there is no requirement to perform continuous total particle monitoring or meet grade A viable monitoring limits. Specifically used for the protection of fully stoppered vials where the cap has not yet been crimped (1).

Note: For the purposes of this document, the term Grade A is understood to be interchangeable with International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 5.

Hold Time

Set durations between qualified events or process steps.

Aseptic Hold Time

A defined and validated maximum time for 'holding' conditions for aseptic preparation and processing.

Clean Hold Time

Duration between when cleaning is implemented to meet a target requirement before the use of parts or areas. This type of hold time would typically apply before sterilization or bio-decontamination processes.

Sterile Hold Time

Duration sterilized items can be held after sterilization and before use.

Intervention

Activities, manipulations, and tasks performed in Grade A, or other critical processing areas, during aseptic operations.

Corrective Intervention

An intervention that is performed to correct or adjust an aseptic process during its execution. These may not occur at a set frequency in the routine aseptic process. Examples include such activities as clearing component jams, stopping leaks, adjusting sensors, and replacing equipment components.

Inherent Intervention

An intervention that is an integral part of the aseptic process and is required for either set-up, routine operation and/or monitoring (e.g., aseptic assembly, container replenishment, environmental sampling). Inherent interventions are required by procedure or work instruction for the execution of the aseptic process.

Critical Intervention

An intervention (corrective or inherent) into the critical zone (1).

Note: An example of this type of intervention would be an open-door intervention when critical surfaces are exposed within the Grade A RABS. This could be a corrective or inherent intervention.

Glossary (continued) （）

Original Text

Isolator

A contained, decontaminated environment meeting Grade A/ISO 5 conditions used for aseptic process manufacturing that provides an uncompromised, continuous isolation of its interior from the external environment. Once decontaminated by a validated cycle, an isolator prevents the microbiological contamination of sterile products and product contact surfaces of the interior by enclosures and the supply of continuous, controlled overpressure of HEPA-filtered air (3).

Closed Isolator Systems

Exclude external contamination from the isolator's interior by accomplishing material transfer via aseptic connection to auxiliary equipment, rather than using openings to the surrounding environment. Closed systems remain sealed throughout operations (1).

Open Isolator

Systems are designed to allow for the continuous or semi-continuous ingress and/or egress of materials during operations through one or more openings. Openings are engineered (e.g., using continuous overpressure) to exclude the entry of external contamination into the isolator (1).

Product Contact Parts
Direct Product Contact Parts

Are those that the product passes through, such as filling needles or pumps (1).

Indirect Product Contact Parts

Are equipment parts that do not contact the product, but may come into contact with other sterilized surfaces, the sterility of which is critical to the overall product sterility (e.g., sterilized items such as stopper bowls and guides, and sterilized components) (1).

Rapid Transfer Port (RTP)

A System used for the transfer of items into RABS or isolators that minimizes the risk to the critical zone. An example would be a rapid transfer container with an alpha/beta port (1).

Restricted Access Barrier System (RABS)

System that provides an enclosed, but not fully sealed, environment meeting defined air quality conditions (for aseptic processing grade A), and using a rigid-wall enclosure and integrated gloves to separate its interior from the surrounding cleanroom environment. The inner surfaces of the RABS are disinfected and decontaminated with a sporicidal agent. Operators use gloves, half suits, RTPs and other integrated transfer ports to perform manipulations or convey materials to the interior of the RABS. Depending on the design, doors are rarely opened, and only under strictly pre-defined conditions (1).

Closed RABS (cRABS)

A RABS for which the barrier prevents air exchange with the surrounding environment. Closed RABS uses a dedicated unidirectional air flow unit (UDAF) system for air supply. Unlike a closed isolator, a closed RABS typically has mouseholes, and commonly has no automated biodecontamination system.

Open RABS (oRABS)

A RABS for which the barrier permits air exchange with the surrounding environment. Passive Open RABS uses the room HVAC for supply. Active Open RABS uses a dedicated unidirectional air flow unit (UDAF) system for air supply but exchanges the exhaust with the room.

Note: Although Open RABS cannot be automatically biodecontaminated, there are some installations where the cleanroom, and all the equipment it contains including the open RABS, is biodecontaminated, but this is a special case.

6-Log Reduction

The reduction of viable microorganisms with the chance of 1:1,000,000 to have a surviving organism.

Sporicidal Agent

An agent that destroys bacterial and fungal spores when used in sufficient concentration for specified contact time. It is expected to kill all vegetative microorganisms (1).

Unidirectional Airflow (UDAF)

An airflow moving in a single direction, in a robust and uniform manner, and at sufficient speed, to reproducibly sweep particles away from the critical processing or testing area (1).

Unidirectional Airflow (UDAF) Unit

A cabinet supplied with filtered unidirectional airflow (previously referred to as a Laminar Airflow Unit or LAF) (1).

Vapor Phase Hydrogen Peroxide (VPHP)

Agent for the decontamination of the interior of the isolator, including all exposed surfaces, fixed isolator glove exteriors (portion extending into the barrier), transfer systems, equipment, and certain supplies, materials, and parts, using a defined and validated gaseous mixture or micro-atomization of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) over a defined and validated period of time, under defined and validated conditions.

Working Position

Position where high-risk operations occur and where product and/or components are exposed (1).

Note: This is sometimes referred to as "working height".

Q 1.1 What are the different types of restricted access barrier systems (RABS)? Q 1.1 （RABS）？

Original Text

Recommendation

A Restricted Access Barrier System (RABS) is a series of components and processes that enclose and provide a Grade A environment for aseptic processing (e.g., dispensing, filling and closing, lyophilization loading). Due to the protection offered by the barrier system, a RABS can provide a higher level of product and personnel protection than conventional Grade A or Grade B filling operations. See Question 2.3.

All RABS have rigid barriers, glove ports for operator access, a unidirectional airflow (UDAF) unit source providing H14 high efficiency particulate air filter (HEPA) filtered air (i.e., First Air), and a method to transfer materials and supplies in and out of it. Having these features in common, RABS differentiate in three different types of air handling: passive open RABS (oRABS), active oRABS, and closed RABS (cRABS), each offering an increasing level of protection for product and personnel. See Topic 3.

Note: This document does not cover isolator design. (See PDA’s Point to Consider for the Aseptic Processing of Sterile Pharmaceuticals in Isolators for additional information.)

Q 1.2 What are the design criteria considerations for restricted access barrier systems (RABS)? Q 1.2 （RABS）？

Original Text

Recommendation

There are several design criteria attributes that need to be taken into consideration when selecting the type of RABS appropriate for a filling line (1, 5, 6).

Table 1.2-1 summarizes the main design criteria attributes that need to be taken into consideration for each type of RABS.

Table 1.2-1: RABS and Non-Barrier System Design Criteria Attribute Considerations

Note: Biosafety Cabinets (BSCs) are not covered under the scope of this table.

Barrier Considerations

Non-barrier technology is obsolete and is not compliant with regulatory expectations. Non-barrier systems have only individual barriers in place, such as polycarbonate, or curtains and aseptic operations are open to the surrounding space of the room.

All types of RABS have a barrier system in place (e.g., translucent rigid barriers) that surrounds the aseptic operations. In oRABS (i.e., passive and active), the barrier system is open to the room via openings below the product level that allow the unidirectional airflow to vent to the surrounding space. This type of oRABS is not sufficiently sealed during operations, therefore the RABS cannot be subjected to a Leak Rate Test to verify enclosure integrity while in operation.

In cRABS, the barrier system is closed to the room such that the unidirectional airflow is recirculated inside the cRABS. The barrier system is sealed, and the cRABS may be subjected to a Leak Rate Test to verify closure integrity even during operations. However, the lack of inflatable gaskets can lead to leaking into the room, and the Leak Rate Test should not be conducted without additional precautionary measures.

Unidirectional Airflow Source

Non-barrier systems and passive oRABS have unidirectional airflow supplied by the surrounding space/room via a ceiling-mounted HVAC system with HEPA-grade filters. Active oRABS and cRABS have unidirectional airflow supplied by a dedicated RABS HVAC system with HEPA-grade filters.

Glove Ports

Non-barrier systems do not have glove ports because the process is open to the surrounding space/room. All types of RABS have glove port(s) installed on the rigid barrier; number and placement will vary. Glove ports allow for aseptic interventions to occur without the need for the operator to enter the RABS (i.e., aseptic processing area).

Rapid Transfer Ports (RTPs)

Non-barrier systems have no material transfer ports because supplies and samples that are transferred into and out of the processing area are done so directly. (See Topic 6 for additional information.) All types of RABS have material transfer port(s) that are integrated into RABS. These ports allow for the aseptic transfer of materials and samples in and out of the RABS.

Note: Although RABS doors can be opened, in the absence of appropriate controls this would potentially allow the introduction of lower-grade air into the Grade A space, potentially contaminating the Grade A processing area environment with lower-grade air. Open-door interventions should be extremely rare occurrences, and proper risk mitigation precautions should be implemented (1).

Environmental Control

Factors such as temperature, humidity, and differential pressure are controlled by the HVAC unit mounted in the surrounding room/space for non-barrier systems and passive oRABS. These factors are controlled by the dedicated RABS HVAC unit for active oRABS and cRABS.

Typically, no positive pressure is present over the processing area compared to the surrounding space. Minimal to no positive pressure is present in passive oRABS over the processing area compared to the surrounding space. In active oRABS and cRABS, positive pressure is established in the processing area compared to the surrounding space. However, in some designs there is the capability to selectively control the air volume in specific areas to enable a positive air flow.

Pressure Cascade

No pressure cascade is possible in non-barrier systems, or oRABS (i.e., active or passive), but this is possible in cRABS.

Bio-decontamination

Bio-decontamination (by gassing or fogging) is possible for non-barrier systems or oRABS only if the room is also bio-decontaminated. In the case of cRABS, it is possible to perform bio-decontamination without the room, however, if the cRABS does not utilize inflatable gaskets, there could be leakage around the gaskets leading to health safety concerns and adequate precautions are needed to ensure personnel safety.

Product

Only nontoxic products are able to be manufactured in non-barrier systems and in oRABS (i.e., active or passive). In cRABS, it is possible to manufacture nontoxic or toxic drug products.

Additional design features should be considered including, but not limited to the following:

  • Design must ensure that all processes (i.e., with the exception of setup) will be designed to avoid open-door interventions.
  • The barrier walls should be rigid, resistant to chemical cleaning and disinfecting agents, and also resistant to mechanical stresses (e.g., scratches/cracking) from cleaning tools. Typical barrier materials are polycarbonate or tempered/safety glass.
  • The positioning of equipment, components, and sensors within the critical zone (e.g., filling needles, open vials) should be considered to ensure that First Air is not disrupted. For fixed machine components (i.e., transport mechanisms), having an effective disinfection program in place is required.
  • The glove positions inside the RABS barrier wall should be appropriate to allow operator arm entry into RABS glove(s) without disruption of First Air above critical surfaces, for example, open vials at the accumulation turntable, above fill needles, above stopper bowl, or above environmental monitoring (EM) equipment. It is key that these locations be determined early in the design process and tested during the mock-up phase of the project to verify operator ergonomics for all intended operations. Having support from experienced operators during this design process is beneficial and recommended.

Note: Any tools that are stored inside the RABS for operator use must be sterilized.

  • To protect the product from particulate contamination, it is recommended that RABS parts and components in the aseptic processing area be constructed of non-shedding, non-leaching, non-corrosive, chemical-resistant materials. It is recommended that all metallic components be constructed of low-carbon 304 stainless steel or better. In the case of metallic product contact and indirect product-contact components, low-carbon 316 stainless steel or better should be used (5). All nonmetallic materials, including RABS gloves, gasketing, seals, and barrier walls and doors need to withstand exposure to various cleaning and disinfecting agents and be suitable for the process.

The following are some points to consider in design and operations of RABS that impact achieving specified environmental control conditions within the RABS:

  • Appropriate lighting to see the equipment, product, and components without degradation of light sensitive product.
  • Rounded corners and smooth materials to minimize the risk of compromising the glove integrity during changes or line interventions.
  • Safety curtain (i.e., light curtain) to detect operator glove entry into the RABS.
  • Alarms for environmental parameters (Temperature/Humidity/UDAF velocity) and door status (door open or closed).
  • EM system viable and total particles in continuous operation during setup and filling operations.
  • EM system (settle plates and active air-monitoring locations) readily accessible via glove ports.

Rationale

The RABS design attributes as described in Table 1.2-1 are beneficial in assuring that environmental conditions are maintained and minimizing microbial contamination associated with direct human interventions in the critical zones.

Q 1.3 What are the design criteria for closed restricted access barrier systems (cRABS)? Q 1.3 （cRABS）？

Original Text

Recommendation

A cRABS, while not providing the same design constructs as an isolator, does provide some advantages over an oRABS design. The benefits of a cRABS can only be experienced when the RABS remains closed following setup. All qualified interventions should be able to be performed through the glove ports. The need to open the doors should be rare and heavily scrutinized on a case-by-case basis; these types of rare open-door interventions may require a deviation (1, 3, 6).

Differences between cRABS and active oRABS:

  • The rigid barrier completely encloses the Grade A space within the cRABS.
  • Air is recirculated within a cRABS, hence there is no spillover to adjacent space as in an oRABS. (See Figure 1.1-3.)
  • Pressure can be maintained within a cRABS, allowing for better control of a pressure cascade between the RABS and upstream and downstream equipment.

Differences between cRABS and an isolator:

  • If inflatable door seals on a cRABS are not present, a vapor-phase hydrogen peroxide (VPHP) cycle is not recommended without adequate safety precautions.
  • Due to the lack of a positive door seal, the background environment for a cRABS is Grade B to minimize the risk of contamination.
  • In the case of rare, open-door interventions and the potential for not being able to conduct automated biodecontamination for a cRABS, a Grade B background is required to minimize the risk of contamination.

Rationale

The cRABS design attributes are beneficial in providing further assurance of environmental conditions and minimizing microbial contamination associated with direct human interventions in the critical zones.

Q 1.4 What are the design considerations for the placement of the restricted access barrier system (RABS) glove ports? Q 1.4 （RABS）？

Original Text

Recommendation

The correct positioning of the RABS glove ports is one of the key elements that should be considered during the design of a RABS. The position of the glove ports plays a vital role in the operation of the RABS and its ability to remain closed during setup and operation. Efforts to minimize the need to use RABS gloves should be taken to reduce the impact or risk to process performance and product sterility. Examples include reducing, eliminating, or repositioning activities, manipulations, and interventions. Figure 1.4-1 distinguishes which part of the RABS are the glove ports and which are the RABS gloves (1).

Figure 1.4-1: Differentiation of Glove Ports and Gloves

When determining where to place/position the RABS glove ports (typically done during mock-up studies), several factors that may pose a risk to the performance of the aseptic process and sterility of the product should be considered such as:

  • Use of a simulation method to review glove port positioning
  • Ergonomics to address personnel fatigue and safety
  • Diversity of personnel height and reach, from which a determined range may inform optimum port positioning
  • Quantity of glove ports
  • Orientation and location of planned and anticipated activities, manipulations, and interventions (i.e., inherent and corrective interventions)
  • Glove port distance to open containers (e.g., vials, syringes, cartridges, EM components) and equipment*
  • RABS gloves range of motion
  • Weight and orientation of materials and items to be handled via the glove port
  • Ability to install RABS gloves and donning RABS gloves in an aseptic manner
  • Orientation of RABS gloves during decontamination/disinfection, aeration, if applicable, and nonuse
  • Proximity to sharp objects or surfaces that can pierce RABS gloves
  • Position of EM apparatus, instruments, and sampling sites

*Note: Interventions cannot be performed directly using the RABS gloves, but rather by using a sterilized tool and/or sterile material for an additional layer of protection where the First Air concept needs to be maintained.

Rationale

With the use of RABS gloves, it is possible to perform interventions inside the RABS without the need to open it. The correct positioning of glove ports enables the operator to reach machine parts without the introduction of the operator gloves and gown, which are potential particle and microbial contamination sources. For this reason, the design and position of glove ports is an important decision point and should be part of the risk assessment and contamination control strategy (CCS).

Generally, a review of the positioning should be performed using a simulation method. Modeling, rendering, 3-D model, virtual reality, mock-up, or any method that allows for demonstration and testing of glove-related procedures with different operators of diverse heights and arm length could be used.

The glove port needs to be positioned to ensure that the RABS gloves have sufficient distance to the open containers (e.g., vials, syringes, cartridges, EM components, etc.) and to the equipment that comes into direct and indirect contact with the product. This allows for a RABS glove entry by the operator without disruption for First Air above critical surfaces.

The design of glove ports should be sufficiently cleanable (i.e., hygienic design) and resistant to the appropriate cleaning/disinfection agents. The design should also allow for the range of motion necessary with the RABS gloves to avoid excessive operator reaching or stretching of RABS gloves.

Restriction of airflow interrupting or interfering with adequate distribution of decontamination chemicals, vapors, or gases (i.e., typically only seen with cRABS) should be avoided.

If applicable, select position of glove stretchers to allow for automatic bio-decontamination of the whole surface (cRABS only).

Q 1.5 What are the design considerations for restricted access barrier system (RABS) gloves? Q 1.5 （RABS）？

Original Text

Recommendation

Quality risk management (QRM) principles should be used to identify, assess, and address special considerations for the safe and effective use of RABS gloves to mitigate the risks posed by the installation and use of RABS gloves during aseptic operation (1).

Once the glove-port positioning is determined, a number of factors that may pose a risk to the performance of the aseptic process and the sterility of the product should be considered. The following considerations should be given to the composition of RABS gloves (3, 4):

  • Quality and design of RABS gloves should be sufficiently cleanable (i.e., hygienic design) and resistant to the appropriate cleaning/disinfection agents and steam sterilization.
  • Glove size, though usually supplied as standard size ranges, should be considered based on intended user population and demographic to ensure that gloves are sufficiently sized and practical for use.
  • Method of physical and visual inspection for integrity (See Topic 4).
  • Ability to confirm or test the integrity of RABS gloves (See Topic 4).
  • Ability to decontaminate, disinfect and, if applicable, aerate RABS gloves
  • Selection of glove material (e.g., ethylene propylene diene monomer (EPDM), chlorosulfonated polyethylene synthetic rubber (CSM)) to allow for resistance during sanitization/decontamination and use; also, to ensure sufficient dexterity to allow for the accurate execution of interventions
  • Thickness of glove material to address dexterity needed to perform task in RABS gloves
  • Thickness of glove material to resist puncture, cracking, or excessive wear
  • Ability to adequately install RABS gloves in glove ports to maintain aseptic status of the line

Additional operational considerations include, but are not limited to:

  • A sufficient number of spare RABS gloves should be available for each run.
  • Expiration date of RABS gloves
  • Exposure to direct sunlight that can degrade RABS gloves or glove seal material
  • Safe position for intermediate storage of RABS gloves in the RABS glove port after or between operations to avoid both the obstruction to First Air during operation, as well as ensuring that the gloves cannot be damaged by moving parts within the RABS

Rationale

Performing the above recommendations will reduce the possibility of leakages due to the tearing of RABS gloves, which impacts the integrity of the RABS. These recommendations are to promote compliance of good aseptic practices/techniques to reduce contamination risks and promote the prevention of open-door interventions.

With the use of RABS gloves, it is possible to perform interventions inside the RABS and to reach machine parts without the introduction of the operator gloves and gown (a potential particle and microbial contamination source). For this reason, the manner of usage, construction materials, and installation procedures is an important decision point and should, at a minimum, be recorded in the CCS.

Q 1.6 How should the restricted access barrier system (RABS) be designed to minimize the risk posed by interventions? Q 1.6 （RABS）？

Original Text

Recommendation

Human interventions (i.e., open-door and/or inherent interventions) within the RABS are required during the setup and assembly of the aseptic filling machine, including the installation of RABS gloves, and during routine manufacturing operations (e.g., aseptic filling with open- and closed-door inherent and corrective interventions).

Risk-based assessments should be used to evaluate the need for and design of human interventions. Where possible, interventions should be eliminated, reduced, or modified to reduce their relative risk. A process should be created to monitor and control the frequency of interventions. Changes to the process should be made to improve the overall robustness of the manufacturing process and should eliminate the need for interventions, when possible.

It is not recommended to approach the need for manufacturing changes with the goal of reducing intervention. Corrective, open-door interventions should be eliminated or occur only as very rare events. Therefore, the prevention of open-door interventions should be a key factor in the consideration for designing a RABS (1, 6).

The positioning of RABS gloves in proper locations should eliminate the need for open-door interventions during filling operations where good aseptic technique applies. Interventions within the RABS (i.e., inherent and corrective) are to be designed to minimize microbiological ingress, including retention, within the RABS. Such factors are proximity to exposed sterile product, components, indirect product contact surfaces, intervention duration, complexity, and frequency.

The following points for consideration are applicable for an aseptic filling line and also for other aseptic working steps (e.g., dispensing, lyophilizer loading/unloading), where a RABS is used:

  • The RABS and the filling line should be designed to allow for interventions (i.e., open and closed door) in the Grade A RABS with due consideration of adherence to proper aseptic technique, for example, to follow First Air working principles for the operator during open-door interventions and also RABS gloves during closed-door interventions. See Question 1.1 for more information.
  • In the case of an open-door intervention that requires direct entry of part of the operator’s body within the Grade A area, design considerations should extend to operator posture and body positioning and ensure that the door openings and location of the RABS glove ports do not prevent risk of operator contact. Tools should be used in place of an operator entering the RABS when possible. Airflow visualization studies should be performed to confirm adequacy under dynamic conditions. Further precautions and controls are required for open-door interventions (1, 6-8). (See Topic 9.)
  • The number and location of glove ports should be designed based on the need for and ease of performance of interventions, in particular, in locations where First Air principles can be followed.
  • Materials and equipment within the RABS should be positioned to avoid crowding and the need to reach over exposed sterile products, materials, and surfaces.
  • The design and positioning of a RABS filling line should prevent the use of swing conveyors to pass from the front to the back of the filling line.
  • The RABS design should consider, under risk assessment, a zone of protection using a UDAF unit to cover, at a minimum, the open-door radius. The positions must be determined by a risk analysis and should consider typical open-door activities, such as the setup of stopper bowls or feeding lanes.
  • The dynamic use of the RABS, where there are inherent and/or corrective interventions being performed and equipment is operating, needs to be confirmed by airflow visualization studies (AVS) to demonstrate that unidirectional flow of air is occurring, and First Air principles are maintained.
  • The use of automated process steps (e.g., sterilization in place (SIP) after installation of the filling line), and intervention-reducing technology (e.g., rapid transfer port (RTP) filling path or components loading as closures, to install EM samplers for preventing mobile air sampler transfer) should be considered to minimize interventions during setup, operations, and sampling.
  • The use of robotics and advanced automation should be considered during the design of a new RABS line to increase the level of sterility assurance by removing operator interventions for critical processes (e.g., the setup of the filling path). When using robotics in the aseptic environment for aseptic filling, the design and operating principles must be carefully evaluated for operation suitability within a cleanroom environment. Robotic systems, regardless of type, bring a certain risk to the unidirectional airflow due to a tendency to generate turbulence. Therefore, robots should not be used for the sake of using robotics, but rather should bring a benefit for the aseptic filling process. Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) and airflow visualization studies (static and dynamic) should verify the impact of the robot to the unidirectional airflow and its impact on First Air. Besides the robot itself the requirements to meet first air also applies for any tools the robot is carrying and connections made on the robot (e.g., media hoses etc.). In addition to considerations regarding impact to First Air, cleanability, disinfection and accessibility to the robot also require consideration.

Rationale

All interventions into a RABS (i.e., open-door intervention) and within the RABS (i.e., closed-door intervention) in the vicinity of exposed sterile product and critical surfaces can add the risk of microbiological contamination to sterile product. Where possible, the elimination or reduction of these activities through process design, equipment layout, technology, and automation can reduce risk. Where activities cannot be eliminated or reduced, process and equipment design that allows for proper aseptic technique and practices can reduce risk. Where interventions cannot be eliminated or reduced in number, modifying these activities to reduce risk factors can reduce the overall relative risk.

Q 1.7 How can open-door interventions be prevented during filling? Q 1.7 ？

Original Text

Recommendation

RABS are designed with the goal of operating predominately as a closed system. Where open-door interventions may be required, appropriate measures must be taken to mitigate the potential for increased risk to the aseptic process.

Detailed design reviews of the production process undertaken at the onset of design can help avoid or minimize the need for open-door activities during the filling operation (1). These reviews should consider, but not be limited to, the following:

  • Since the open-door barrier manual set-up process presents the highest risk of contamination of the Grade A zone when entering sterilized parts, the design of the set-up operation should be assessed using QRM principles to reduce any unnecessary risk.
  • Following QRM principles, RABS design and operation should avoid the need for open-door interventions so after the last bio-decontamination at set up the barrier doors remain closed until batch end. Primarily inherent and corrective interventions should be via barrier closed and any open-door intervention fully justified and rare.
  • RTPs used for introduction of pre-sterilized process components, consumables, product containers and closures plus EM plates if required to be changed or recovered for incubation during aseptic manufacturing operations.
  • Use of robotic systems and automation that drives intervention free processing including introduction of EM systems that do not rely on growth media plate change (e.g., real time continuous viable monitoring).
  • Design considerations equally extend to consideration of the use and position of glove ports and should be considered in conjunction with the intended operations to define access requirements within the enclosed system and the type of operations for which interventions may be required.
  • Design considerations extend to the use of clean-in-place (CIP)/SIP and single-use systems for fluid or powder pathways.

Periodic reviews of the type, frequency, and duration of interventions for the filling process should be reviewed to inform the next periodic aseptic process simulation (APS) protocol and to otherwise inform a potential engineering or other control solution that may be introduced to eliminate the need for future similar open-door interventions (1, 4, 9).

Notwithstanding these considerations, not all possible events that may be encountered during manufacturing operations can be considered during the design or early stages of RABS introduction for a new product or process. Rather, the intent of a well-designed aseptic program involving the use of barrier technology is to ensure that the RABS protection concept is the primary focus and continuously assessed, with the aim of eliminating or reducing the need to open the barrier during filling activities. When a closed-door intervention is possible to address an issue, an open-door intervention must be prohibited.

Procedural controls should be established to ensure that adequate provisions are included in governing procedures and processes for such unavoidable scenarios and should extend to a detailed review and documentation including, but not limited to:

  • Preassessment of airflow visualization studies (AVS) and positive-pressure differentials to ensure that positive airflow from the enclosure to the external environment can be maintained prior to opening doors. A pre-intervention assessment should also extend to a documented review of what needs to be performed and whether the activity can be considered comparable to a preexisting intervention (1, 4, 9).
  • Consideration of whether the unqualified intervention represents a departure from critical controls established and whether a deviation or formal risk assessment is required in support of finalizing and undertaking the planned intervention. The need for additional EM data, personnel monitoring requirements, sterility sample requirements, particulate-monitoring data and alarm review should be considered. An assessment of whether a media qualification and AVS may be required prior to release of the batch should also be understood and documented.
  • A robust disinfection of the nonproduct-contact surfaces, implicated by the open-door activity using appropriately qualified disinfectants. If necessary, to ensure there is no disinfectant residue, the use of 70% IPA after disinfection should be considered.
  • Consideration of need for batch segregation and/or discarding of any filled product or components that may have been compromised or placed at unnecessary risk during the intervention.
  • Line clearance to eliminate open containers and/or components as applicable that may be in the vicinity of the intervention.
  • Confirmation that Grade A conditions have been reestablished prior to recommencing filling.
"Note: Although not recommended, in rare cases, an unanticipated corrective intervention may be required that necessitates open-door operator intervention (e.g., to mitigate equipment challenges). In such cases, the inherent increased risk to the product requires careful consideration in line with formal QRM principles and should inform future engineering controls or other provisions that may be introduced with the aim of eliminating recurrences or the requirement for the open-door intervention. Before the intervention is executed, it needs to be risk assessed. If the risk-assessment does not support the execution of the intervention, it should not occur."

Rationale

By design, the intent of a RABS or other barrier system is to separate the operator from the critical process zone to reduce the overall risk of contamination. Since interventions within a barrier system pose the highest risk to the aseptic core, the aim of any barrier technology, combined with a system of (engineering) controls, should be to reduce or eliminate any unnecessary interventions. Hence, the use of the barrier technology in conjunction with a system of controls comprising suitable design, robust quality systems, and aseptic oversight is required.

Q 1.8 How can contamination risks of Grade A environments and compromise of sterilized component sterility be minimized during setup? Q 1.8 Grade A ？

Original Text

Recommendation

Setup, if done through closed doors via the glove ports, requires fewer precautions; however, setup most often requires activities through open doors. If that is the case, there are several ways to minimize the risk:

  • Operate with only one door open at a time.
  • As much as possible, equipment should be assembled prior to sterilization to avoid the need for aseptic connections. Sterilized items should be introduced with protective covers or sterile boxes and those covers or boxes should only be removed after the doors have been closed through the glove ports. For legacy systems, where removal of protective covers after the doors are closed is not possible, alternative approaches must be considered and documented as part of the CCS. (See Topic 6.)
  • Utilize a "top down" approach to line build, when possible, so installation is not occurring over an exposed critical surface (e.g., stopper hopper, stopper slide, stopper bowl).
  • Don additional gowning, such as sterile sleeves or longer gloves (1).
  • Perform operator activities with a primary and secondary operator model: In this model, the secondary operator opens the door and hands material to the primary operator who only operates within the Grade A space. (See Topic 9.)
  • The RABS design should consider, under a risk assessment, a zone of protection using an UDAF unit to at least cover the open-door radius. The positions have to be determined by a risk analysis and should consider frequent open-door activities such as the setup of stopper bowls or feeding lanes. Figure 1.8-1 shows an example for UDAF extension (1, 9).
  • Evaluate the need to take additional (continuous) air samples (viable and total particulates) in the Grade B area with UDAF immediately surrounding the open-door area for the duration of the open-door setup activities (1, 9). (See PDA Technical Report No. 62: Recommended Practices for Manual Aseptic Processes for additional guidance for personnel behavior.)
  • AVS qualification should be done to ensure that there is a UDAF flush away and out of the RABS during setup.
  • Evaluate the possibility of performing VPHP decontamination after setup (cRABS or oRABS with cleanroom).
  • After setup, the use of automatic decontamination reduces the risk of ingress of the operator into Grade A.

Rationale

Open-door activities typically necessary for equipment setup are of a higher risk as personnel must ingress into the Grade A space. All activities that minimize the risk of introducing bioburden into the Grade A environment will reduce the risk to the environment and, subsequently, the product. Such activities include, but are not limited to, the following:

  • Minimizing the number of open doors to reduce the potential disruption of the UDAF within the RABS.
  • Maintaining protective covers on equipment, such as bonnets on a stopper bowl, keeps the sterilized surfaces from being exposed until the RABS doors have been closed and ensuring greater protection. It also allows for the door-open time to be minimized, completing the setup activities through the glove ports in a second step (4).
  • The utilization of additional protective measures (sterile sleeves, longer gloves, etc.) needs to be evaluated based upon an approved risk assessment.
  • Similarly, the primary/secondary operator model safeguards the integrity of the gowning of the primary operator, further reducing the risk of introducing contamination. (See PDA TR-62 for additional information.)
  • Having UDAF immediately adjacent to the doors, so that once the door is open, the area remains covered by Grade B surrounding the open door, will reduce the risk of introducing bioburden into the RABS enclosure.

Q 1.9 What construction design considerations are required for materials and maintenance? Q 1.9 ？

Recommendation

RABS surfaces should be manufactured in materials compatible with the environmental classification conditions required for the proposed use.

Construction Materials Considerations:

  • Interior metallic surfaces in direct or indirect contact with the primary packing should be constructed in 316 stainless steel or better and, for the rest of the line, metallic components could be constructed of low-carbon 304 stainless steel or better (1, 6).

The internal RABS working areas should be designed to minimize the risk of contamination in critical zones for troubleshooting and repair activities.

All maintenance activities for a RABS and related equipment should be scheduled in a plan to occur at the same time, when it is possible, to avoid multiple preventive-maintenance work-order days.

The RABS and equipment construction materials should be designed to meet such production requirements as cleanability, sanitization, and practicality of use, with the scope to reduce the contamination risk by particles that could be released from routine use or from eventual damages and abrasion occurred during routine production or maintenance activities.

Q 1.10 What unique design provisions are required for highly potent compounds exposed within closed restricted access barrier systems (cRABS)? Q 1.10 （cRABS）？

Original Text

Recommendation

Environmental Health Safety vs. Good Manufacturing Practices

When designing a RABS for aseptic highly potent compounds, both the good manufacturing practice (GMP) and environmental health and safety (EHS) aspects are very important. While overpressure of the containment is necessary from a GMP perspective, negative pressure is required from the EHS standpoint to ensure operator safety (6).

Therefore, the pressure differential between the inside of the RABS and the outside environment must be positive. To avoid contamination from the most highly potent exposure area (e.g., filling zone), this zone is operated in negative pressure towards adjacent RABS sections. To make this physically and technically possible, a cRABS is needed. To allow for the creation and maintenance of the pressure differences during operation, it needs at least a cRABS, if recirculating air is used, a dedicated air handling unit (AHU) must be present.

Additional Considerations:

  • Ability to safely change out terminal HEPAs by a safe-change filter system or one-pass aeration systems (with bag-in/bag-out exhaust HEPAs) (See Figure 1.10-1.)
  • Interlocked door sensors and other mechanical devices to ensure operator safety
  • Vacuum systems should be avoided where possible; otherwise, the integration of filters that prevent contamination at the base of the machine should be considered
  • Transfer ports should be present to manage the removal of contained material used for spillage clean-up without having to open the RABS.*
  • Pressure differential-monitoring systems to alarm in case of loss of pressure differential or RABS leakage.
  • Double glass doors are to be avoided (See Figure 1.10-1.)
  • Important for the commissioning of such products is the correct protective equipment of the employees and their behavior in case of an accidental breach.

*Note: This consideration does not apply for monitoring equipment using vacuum, such as total-particle counters or active viable monitoring. The monitoring equipment must be located in the most critical positions according to the EM risk analysis. A strategy to prevent contamination of the vacuum lines and the monitoring equipment must be evaluated (e.g., the particulate filter above the machine plate in the aseptic area) immediately after the sampling head for active viable (6). The sampling head must be treated as any other exposed equipment surface within the RABS and needs to be appropriately cleaned before the sterilization cycle. For particulate measurement systems, a different strategy must be considered. Total particle counters should be dedicated to certain areas and changed in situ to reduce the movement of the equipment and to limit exposure. Particulate filter can be placed after the device to ensure contamination prevention of the vacuum lines and pumps.

Cleaning

A highly preferred method for cleaning a cRABS contaminated with toxic substances is the automatic wash-in-place (WIP) process. The WIP process can either be executed with spray guns mounted inside the RABS for manual wash-down or with a spray ball to achieve an automatic wash down. This means that even hard-to-reach areas can be cleaned more easily while the doors remain closed and, therefore, the risk of operator exposure is minimized. The handling of the wastewater must be considered with a special focus on the draining systems (i.e., EU Annex 1: Manufacture of Sterile Medicinal Products excludes drains in Grade A or Grade B environments) and toxic water separation. Depending on toxicity, measuring residuals in wastewater is recommended to ensure the RABS environment is free from potential potent residuals. A risk assessment should be performed to determine the proper neutralization of the wastewater from the toxic process and proper design. Nevertheless, the machine and the containment must, of course, be designed in such a way that all parts can be easily cleaned and equipped in a hygienic design.

Rationale

Potent compounds are hazardous to operators and, while product sterility must be maintained, it is equally important to ensure that operators and the environment they operate in can be protected from exposure. Utilization of cRABS, with negative pressure and WIP systems, allows for the operation and cleaning of the system, without ever exposing the operator or the environment outside of the RABS to the compound.

Original Text

Recommendation

Monitoring should be performed at locations posing the highest risk of contamination of the sterile equipment surfaces, containers, closures, and product. The selection of monitoring locations and the orientation and positioning of sampling devices should be justified in an EM risk analysis and appropriate to obtain reliable data from the critical zones (1, 4, 12).

In-line Viable Monitoring:

  • Viable air samplers located near critical process zones (e.g., accumulation table, filling station, stoppering section).
  • Consider location near glove ports for ease of plate placement, removal, and storage.
  • Consider installing an EM plate holder for staging multiple EM plates as well as holding the lid of the air sampler, if applicable. This stand should be installed near to the air sampler and within reach via glove ports.
  • Verify active air samplers are part of the calibration program (airflow).
  • Consider First Air requirements for critical surfaces (e.g., initial load of agar plates, replacement of settle plates).

In-line Total Particulate Monitoring:

  • Total particle air sampler located near critical process zones within a one foot radius of the working position (e.g., accumulation table, filling, stoppering) (4).
  • If air samplers have covers, verify sampler is near glove ports for ease of cover placement or removal.
  • Verify air sampler is part of the calibration program (airflow, particulates typically 0.5 μm and 5.0 μm).

  • Sampling locations must be representative and close to conventional settle plates to show equivalency of the system for validation and data collection during production.
  • Suitable installation location of the counter to ensure accessibility for retraction of gelatine filter/agar plate for possibility of identifying the autofluorescence unit.
  • Tube length from sampling point to counter needs to be considered and validated by particle loss studies if >1 m.
  • Alarm management for autofluorescence unit hit must be in place and validated.

Temp/RH/DP/Airflow Velocity (6):

  • Sensors should be placed in representative locations—located downstream of UDAF/HEPA filters but above product pathway—so as not to interfere with equipment operation and potential interventions.

Alarm and Notification Systems:

  • Warnings (alerts and alarms) should be in place to alert operators in the event of any deviations from the predefined EM parameters.
  • Where alarm delays are utilized, a duration of delay must be justified.

Data Analysis and Reporting:

  • Consider having the inline EM sensors integral to the RABS control system to facilitate central operator control and integration of EM readings to equipment interlocks (e.g., high particle counts halts filling).
  • Alert levels should be established, and then trended, followed by periodic review.

Rationale

EM should effectively evaluate the state of environmental conditions within the RABS (e.g., total particulates, viables, air quality, airflow and pressure cascade).

Recommendation

Retrofitting a line into a RABS is normally a temporary solution to maintain compliance with GMP. However, retrofitting can be beneficial by increasing sterility assurance while the company works on the longer-term implementation of a full RABS or isolator design.

Typical retrofit scenarios, include:

  • Conventional A/B filling to oRABS
  • oRABS to cRABS

Note: Retrofitting a cRABS could be challenging.

  • Adding transfer interfaces (e.g. RTPs) to existing RABS

Retrofitting a RABS or transferring system interfaces along the filling line is recommended to address the following needs:

  • Create a barrier system for the RABS critical zone and interfaces
  • Compliance with First Air concepts (i.e., modifications to the design of the manufacturing line may be needed)
  • Reduced open-door interventions
  • Improved CCS for the manufacturing line
  • Requirements from relevant health authorities (international and local)

Note: Retrofitting a RABS could be difficult.

Points to consider for a retrofitting approach include, but are not limited to, the following:

First Air Principle Maintained

Check the feasibility of any operation that must be performed with doors closed to ensure that unidirectionality (First Air Principle) can be maintained during the manipulation. Also consider the use of components and materials that can be transferred into the RABS via rapid transfer ports (e.g. RTPs) to avoid the necessity to open the barrier doors. These ports should be located nearest to the use of the materials transferred in. Port location and First Air verification can be accomplished via the use of a 3-D model with computational fluid dynamics (CFD) or a mock-up model prior to the retrofit. Refer to Questions 1.2 and 1.3 for further details on RABS design considerations.

Production Operations

Ensure full filling equipment access via glove ports for aseptic connection and interventions in critical zones, reducing the door opening for intervention during production. It is important to ensure that interventions during all phases of production are evaluated including batch setup. Refer to Question 9.2 for further details on the qualification of interventions.

For the differential pressure requirement associated with, for example, a Load Accumulation Table to Transfer Cart RABS interface (Grade B background), the background environment is the Grade B area with the Grade A Load Unload Automatic Transfer representing the critical zone. Consequently, at the interface, a positive airflow from the critical zones (i.e., RABS) to the supporting background environment (i.e., Grade B area) is required.

Minimize or consider the possibility of eliminating operator intervention during the freeze-dryer loading/unloading, implementing automatic or semi-automatic loading system, that can provide:

  • Creation of a barrier system
  • First Air concept compliance

Retrofitting a RABS for a freeze-dryer loading/unloading system could be realized in a first step by a semi-automatic system before changing to a fully automated system.

Rationale

When retrofitting, the design of the RABS must work around the existing design of the filling equipment. It is important, and challenging, to achieve a balance between ensuring the retrofit allows sufficient access to perform all required interventions while also minimizing the need to perform open-door activities/interventions.

In a RABS retrofit, the design of the unidirectional airflow protection surrounding the RABS barrier can also be a challenge, and it is important to ensure that it can cover the entire area exposed during the opening of doors (i.e., Grade B).

Q 2.1 Room Classification Surrounding RABS Q 2.1 RABS

Original Text

Q 2.1. What should be the classification of the room surrounding the restricted access barrier system (RABS)?

Recommendation

The minimum classification of the room immediately surrounding the RABS must be Grade B. However, a manufacturer may also choose to maintain these areas at more stringent levels of control when assessed for risk, especially in the area immediately surrounding the RABS doors, which may require execution of open-door interventions (i.e., during setup or filling) (1, 4).

Rationale

A RABS provides an enclosed and separated environment designed to reduce the risk of contamination to product, components, and product-contact surfaces when compared to conventional "curtained" aseptic processing operations. However, RABS are not fully sealed from the immediate surrounding environment and, in some designs, allow for open-door interventions by aseptic operators. As such, the adjacent background cleanroom environment must be appropriately classified and maintained to protect the critical processing zone(s) within the RABS; therefore, the cleanroom environment should not be lower than Grade B.

Companies should provide the maximum assurance possible and practicable so that the controlled RABS environment is not impacted by the surrounding environmental conditions since the RABS is not completely isolated from the surroundings, unlike closed isolators, for example.

"Note: The minimum regulatory expectations (e.g., FDA, EMA) for the classification of manufacturing spaces immediately adjacent (i.e., background) to the Grade A critical processing zone is Grade B."

Q 2.2. Which environmental monitoring criteria are required in extended unidirectional airflow surrounding restricted access barrier system (RABS)?

Recommendation

Due to the presence of the UDAF around the open-door portion of the RABS, total particulates and viables should always reflect Grade A limits at the working position, at rest. During times when the doors are closed, this environment can be monitored as a more traditional Grade B environment, whereas during open-door interventions, it should be treated as an enhanced Grade B environment with limits (alert and action) defined based on a risk assessment.

Q 2.3 Airflow Visualization Studies (AVS) Q 2.3 （AVS）

Original Text

Q 2.3. How should airflow pattern visualization/demonstration be performed in a restricted access barrier system (RABS) for airflow visualization studies (AVS)?

Recommendation

The purpose of airflow visualization studies (AVS) (also known as "smoke testing") is the verification of unidirectional airflow maintained during all required equipment functions and manual interventions. This is aligned to the principle of assuring First Air in contact with product and direct and indirect product-contact equipment. The intention is to demonstrate that all permitted interventions are appropriately designed and can be executed according to the approved methodology or procedure.

AVS should verify protective airflow is maintained at RABS entry 'mouseholes' without compromise to the process environment that protective airflow is applied to.

An additional reason to perform an AVS is to have a visual record of process operations conducted per requirements to allow the following:

  • Used as training material to demonstrate correct process execution. An important aspect about AVS is that they should be exactly representatives of operational conditions, so people should have the right gowning, validated loads and packages should be used.
  • Presenting to internal or external inspectors who may require visualization of a process that may not be (fully) running at the point of inspection.
  • Assess EM sampling site locations and design of interventions or intervention discard strategies.
"Note: It is important to know what the air velocity is at the working position during the airflow visualization testing so that a correlation can be established in routine requalification studies to demonstrate the continued applicability of the AVS."

Airflow Pattern Visualization Process Activity Considerations

It must be recognized that the act of performing airflow visualization disturbs the environment that it is designed to visualize due to the purposeful injection of a smoke or fogging spray into the critical zone that would self-evidently not occur during routine operations.

To minimize the impact of this disturbance in the air supplied to the RABS, the following should be considered:

  1. Develop a Qualification Protocol — Work with affected departments (Manufacturing, Quality Assurance, Engineering, Microbiology) to determine and document the setup, operation, and intervention steps necessary for the process. The test objective is to show unidirectional airflow sweeping over critical surfaces.
  2. Select Smoke-Generation Equipment Type (e.g., Fogger, Cartridge, Dry Ice) — There are multiple acceptable equipment options for the generation of "smoke" for airflow pattern visualization testing, including foggers that use dry ice, vaporization of water for injection (WFI) or deionized water, or liquid nitrogen. The use of foggers that generate glycerin-based vapor is not recommended because they can leave an oily residue on the test area, personnel, and/or floor areas.
  3. Select Water Vapor-Generating Equipment — Water vapor-generating equipment has a variety of generation mechanisms and design styles, including cartridge-based (handheld) and continuous supply via external devices. Depending on the specifics of the application and the RABS design, one of these styles may be the better choice as there would be no residue formation.
  4. Amount of Smoke — The amount of smoke generated should be sufficient to visualize the airflow (and possible turbulence), but not too much smoke to obscure the operators' actions or machine operations.
  5. Smoke Speed — The smoke should be generated at slow speed in the opposite direction to the airflow, at approximately 45° against the direction of a vertical airflow and at a speed that minimizes disturbance. It should be noted that if the smoke flow was in the same direction as the normal airflow, that can reinforce the airflow directionality.
  6. Smoke Generator and Smoke Diffuser (i.e., Manifold) — Movement of the smoke generator and smoke diffuser should be reduced to be as slow as possible, as recommended by good aseptic practices. To limit movements, the use of a smoke diffuser of sufficient size is suggested such that it becomes unnecessary to move it during the AVS.
  7. Smoke Generation Location — The smoke is to be generated as high as possible above the working position, according to the design of the equipment, but not too close to the HEPA filter to avoid filter-clogging due to residue accumulation.
  8. Air Movement at Interfaces — To check air movement at interfaces to the Grade B surrounding environment (e.g., when doors are open), the smoke is injected inside and outside the RABS. This is done at different locations to demonstrate the flush of the air from the Grade A RABS and its demonstrated unidirectionality (when injected inside) and the absence of air ingress (when injected from outside) into the RABS zone.
  9. Wall and Vertical Panel Considerations — Injecting smoke near walls or vertical panels should be avoided, as this may create additional disturbances in airflow that would not occur in routine operations. Note: Air will flow slower along the RABS walls, and there will be minor eddies along the walls. This is to be expected by the application of physics.
  10. Airflow Visualization Study Frequency — The frequency of re-executing an AVS is not timebound in the first instance; it should be based primarily on events (equipment or process change control). As periodic validation review is regarded as a qualification study, the study may need to be repeated for other reasons (e.g., improvements in film-capture quality over time).
  11. Open-door Intervention Considerations — In the case of open-door interventions, airflow visualization qualification should be done to ensure that there is a unidirectional airflow flush away and out of the RABS during the intervention.

Selection of Video Recording Equipment and Cinematography

The record that exists after the execution of airflow visualization testing is a GMP record that assures the data integrity, a video record that may need to exist for many years. As such, careful thought must go into both the selection of the recording equipment and the modalities concerning film cinematography to maximize value of what is shown to the reviewer in the video footage. The following points and recommendations should be followed:

  1. Equipment and Physical Setup — The equipment used and the physical setup to video-capture the performance of the airflow visualization testing must be considered to ensure that the record contains sufficient detail to demonstrate that unidirectional flow is maintained in all required areas. A good strategy to ensure this is to set up multiple cameras at different angles with significantly overlapping fields of view. Where applicable, built-in cameras and recording devices can be used to capture video of smoke studies; however, complete and detailed coverage of the study area must be accomplished.
  2. Visibility of Vapor on the Video Recording — The rear glass doors of the RABS may need to be covered by an opaque backing or a contrasting color in order to facilitate visibility in the final video footage.
  3. Cinematography Considerations — Contextualizing the cinematography by filming a timepiece that indicates the time and date before panning the camera to visualize the smoke directionality is recommended. Filming a schematic drawing of the RABS system and then the equipment section can also assist the viewer in this regard. Ensure that the differential pressure across the filter and velocity were within standard operating limits during the AVS. Each camera shot should be carefully prepared to ensure that no unnecessary activity is performed in the background. The video should be taken using two to four (2-4) different camera angles to facilitate, as much as possible, a three-dimensional view of the unidirectionality of airflow over the critical zones. Best practice is to synchronize the timing of all filmed scenes, so that direct comparison of the airflow visualization can be made from all filmed angles while viewing in a single pane. When footages are edited to be combined into one stream, the original video file, if considered to contain raw data, is to be retained.

Practical Considerations

Similarly, the logistics of the study execution require careful planning so that the captured film footage is integral and does not allow for any misinterpretation or gaps to the record. The following should be considered for implementation:

  • Simulate a walk-through of interventions (dress rehearsal) to assist with seamless professional execution that minimizes the need for extensive editing. Consider the floor communication method (i.e., radios, hand signals) during execution if participating individuals are on opposite sides of a RABS.
  • Simulate all manipulations strictly following good aseptic behavior, including glove decontamination, and limit the personnel in the space to the smoke-equipment operator, aseptic processing operators, video operator, and reviewer/approver.
  • Filling line operation at maximum speed will require airflow visualization studies because of potential impact on protective airflows, including 'First air'.
  • Consider bringing a cleanroom mobile device (e.g., tablet) to the film location so the video footage can be downloaded and reviewed by approvers on the floor immediately after the test study has been performed (subject to room classification).
  • Verify that all support systems (e.g., room HVAC, unidirectional airflow) are in a controlled and qualified state and operational. Verify all personnel are present and gowned per routine procedure.
  • Verify that the materials needed (i.e., smoke vapor source) are available and in sufficient quantities.
  • Verify any smoke-detection equipment that could trigger an alarm and/or evacuation are switched off for the zone being filmed, in collaboration with the site security personnel. Particle and viable counters should be in operation to mimic the routine, but additional precautions may need to be taken to avoid damage to sensor heads.
  • Verify that the room is in an acceptable state (i.e., normal housekeeping, no work in progress in the background of the video, no materials linked to shutdown activity stored in area being studied).

Rationale

Focus of the video reviewer may be diverted from the smoke study evaluation if simulated operations are not done with the expected level of control for the operation to be simulated (e.g., untidy areas, personnel not wearing required gowning). Additionally, abnormal conditions may not be considered representative of the routine when performing AVS.

The integrity of the airflow visualization film footage requires assurance (e.g., date, time, continuity, accuracy) for both inspectional and training purposes. The firm should assess periodically the need to repeat smoke testing to provide assurance that they reflect current operational characteristics.

This assessment should consider the following:

  • Cumulative effect of process and/or facility changes
  • Quality of the video footage being improved over time
  • Improvements in the methodology of smoke generation and insertion techniques

Q 2.4 Personnel Passage Through Grade A RABS Q 2.4 Grade A RABS

Original Text

Q 2.4. What are the considerations for facility layout design regarding acceptable personnel passage through the Grade A restricted access barrier system (RABS)?

Recommendation

Using a conveyor swing arm to lift the process line and doors that close into both sides to protect the line can allow the movement of operators from one side of the line to the other in cases where it is not possible to have two entrances into the room from the Grade B corridor. This movement should always be performed only after the impacted section of the line has been cleared of vials and is preferable in a section of the line where the units have already been filled and are fully stoppered. After personnel passage through the swing conveyor, a disinfection of the RABS interior/swing conveyor is required.

The environment immediately adjacent to the RABS may be designed to provide unidirectional airflow. This will provide protection to the glove ports and the interior of the doors when opened to performed inherent interventions (i.e., sterile equipment installation) or rare open-door corrective interventions. Computational Fluid Design (CFD) modeling may determine the need to have small rigid polycarbonate sheets extending from the ceiling to the top of the door height to ensure the airflow is unidirectional providing the desired protection.

Rationale

The movement of the swing arm might create turbulence in the surrounding air. Every effort must be made to prevent air external to the RABS from entering the RABS to protect units that are not yet integral from contamination.

Using smaller HEPA curtains that reach down to door height would provide some directionality and flush airflow without introducing risk to operators or materials.

Q 3.1 Operator Glove Disinfection |

Original Text

Q 3.1. Should operator gloves be disinfected prior to entering the restricted access barrier system (RABS) gloves?

Recommendation

Disinfection of operator gloves prior to entry into the RABS gloves is an important component of the CCS and should be undertaken prior to each entry into the RABS gloves.

Gloved hands should be disinfected with an approved disinfectant (e.g., sterile isopropanol) prior to donning the RABS gloves (1, 4). The disinfectant can be applied by spraying. It is considered a basic and vital element of hygiene and cleanroom practices to minimize contamination risk or the transfer of contaminants within the clean room in general. It is also important that the risk of transfer of contamination from one operator to another is also considered and mitigated as part of the overall CCS.

Since RABS gloves are typically shared by more than one operator, the risk of contamination transfer from one operator to another through the shared use of the RABS gloves should be minimized by sanitizing the operator's own gloves before they enter into the glove ports or after exiting from the glove ports. The risk identified above (multiple operators using same gloves) is with reference to mitigating risk of contamination transfer between operators. Of course, the risk of product contamination requires equal consideration.

Rationale

RABS gloves are an integral design element and form part of the physical barrier that enables product protection by separating operators from the process.

Disinfection of gloves worn underneath RABS gloves will reduce the microbial load within the RABS gloves, in contrast to nondisinfected gloves. In the case of a pinhole in a RABS glove, a reduced microbial load on the inside of the RABS glove will reduce the chance of microbial ingress into the RABS Grade A environment (14).

RABS gloves are not infallible and, with continued use, may become worn, torn, or perforated and, hence, may potentially compromise the integrity of the system. While controls, including visual inspection (pre- and post-interventions of use) of gloves, provide for some degree of detection of holes or leaks, the identification of a leak through glove integrity-testing is usually undertaken post-batch or post-campaign manufacturing. This requires a retrospective assessment to determine the impact on the batch in the event of a confirmed glove-integrity breach.

For closed-door interventions that require the use of the RABS gloves, the risk profile associated with a potential breach of the Grade A RABS environment owing to a RABS glove integrity failure is high. This can be further compounded in the absence of operator glove disinfection, since the physical barrier between the operator hand (normally afforded through integral RABS gloves) and the Grade A environment is breached. A vector for microbiological contamination is thereby created, with risk profile reduced by ensuring adequate glove disinfection.

Q 3.2 Personnel Qualification |

Original Text

Q 3.2. How are personnel qualified for executing interventions?

Recommendation

For qualification of personnel for open-door and/or closed-door interventions, an intervention management program should be established. Typically, a phase-wise approach is followed to increase operator proficiency.

Level 1 – Training

A well-rounded personnel qualification training program should include, but not be limited to, the following:

  • Basic elements of microbiology and hygiene with specific focus on cleanroom practices (slow, deliberate movements of body and arms/gloves), principles about contamination control, First Air concept, good aseptic working techniques (i.e., in particular during RABS open-door interventions), protection of sterile products and critical surfaces, and the potential safety implications to the patient if a product is not sterile.
  • Visual airflow studies should be used in training to support the understanding of operator interventions in the aseptic space. Virtual reality-based training modules may be used as a best practice in training to assess operators' skills and mitigate risks by providing immersive, interactive simulations that help them practice real-life situations in a controlled environment.
  • Aseptic gowning and cleanroom behavior training is to be provided. The trainee should perform dry runs of gowning and degowning under the supervision of an experienced trainer, showing that the trainee is able to perform the gowning process appropriately and consistently. Those dry runs can be performed in suitable office spaces or otherwise easy-to-access rooms. Gowning qualification can be initiated in conjunction with a trainer after successful completion of the basic elements of microbiology and cleanroom behavior aspects outlined above.

Level 2 – Gowning Qualification Followed by Demonstration

The aseptic-gowning procedure should be confirmed by a visual and microbial (plating with contact plates) assessment, and the trainee's capability to don the complete sterile gown in an aseptic manner to allow entry in the aseptic area should be evaluated.

Demonstration of Assembly/Machine Setup, Interventions, and Filling Operation

Training is required on site-specific standard procedures, on machine parts setup and interventions, with the help of applicable dynamic airflow visualization studies. Training can be staggered based on intervention complexity, wherein operators first demonstrate competency to perform less complex interventions prior to moving to more complex interventions. Alternatively, the training may be certified as one block, where proficiency in all relevant interventions (irrespective of complexity) must be demonstrated for the operator to be certified.

Personnel should observe the production activities (setup and interventions) during routine commercial batch-manufacturing from the view windows or by viewing remote cameras without entering into the aseptic area. This can be done in parallel with Level 2 training, with supervisory monitoring by experienced and qualified personnel.

Personnel should simulate machine setup and interventions in the aseptic core, with the supervision of qualified personnel, by performing interventions in Grade A when there is no commercial batch-manufacturing (e.g., after completion of the routine commercial batch operations, prior to cleaning of the filling line). Assessment of nonviable and viable particle-monitoring during those simulations may be considered. The number of dry runs to be considered should be based on personnel skill set and prior experience before allowing participation in an APS run.

Level 3 – Practical Assessment in Smaller-Batch and Regular APS

After completing Levels 1 and 2, the personnel should perform setup and interventions with participation in an APS (1, 4, 7, 15). The run can be independent of routine APS, or it can be a routine APS run. The APS run may be smaller or of routine size depending on the size of the operation, the number of personnel to be qualified, and the process complexity oRABS/cRABS and open- or closed-interventions.

A bracketing approach can be applied for setup operations in the case of similarities in manipulations and interventions during the media manipulation run for qualifying more than one operator within the same run, based on the risk-based approach. However, attention should be paid to the number of units filled by an individual operator to be representative of the actual commercial operation.

Choosing a setup process that is more challenging to perform or more complex in execution and grouping interventions to perform with different sets of tools (e.g., using short or long forceps, stopper/seal track adjustments, needle adjustment) is recommended.

For open-door versus closed-door interventions, all open-door interventions and closed-door interventions that are classified as high risk within site-risk assessment documents should be chosen to be performed. When certifying the personnel to perform the aseptic processing activities in commercial batches, the same procedures that were qualified in the APS run should be chosen. Upon the successful result of APS, the data compliance monitoring (continuous viable and total particle), assessment of aseptic techniques, and the behavior of the operator(s) to be qualified for performing aseptic operations, the operator should be qualified.

Within lifecycle management, when any new open-door interventions are added to the system at later stages, quality management tools should ensure that the personnel are qualified to perform the additional interventions and the interventions have been simulated in APS runs, unless it has been otherwise documented in risk assessments with scientific rationale that the new intervention is like the qualified one.

Level 4 – Ongoing Training and Assessment

Upon successful completion of personnel qualification, the operator should be certified to perform aseptic operations, and their aseptic behavior should be monitored on a periodic/continuous basis until a sufficient level of experience is achieved. The assessment should evaluate if supervision of the newly qualified person can be reduced or if further training and additional assessment is required. It is recommended to qualify personnel annually, for both gowning and participation in APS runs.

Disqualification of Aseptic Processing Personnel

In the event when operators are considered disqualified, access to the aseptic area should be removed/prohibited until demonstration of successful requalification. The disqualification criteria may include:

  1. Failure to qualify or meet periodic qualification requirements
  2. Participation in an unsuccessful or failed APS, where their performance was identified as a cause or contribution to the failure
  3. Repeated aseptic processing technique in a manner deemed unacceptable in relation to cleanroom or aseptic-process operations or functions
  4. Failure to maintain gowning qualification
  5. Failure to maintain training requirements
  6. Repeated personnel monitoring excursions
  7. Extended periods of inactivity or absence from the aseptic processing area
  8. Other aspects of performance as identified by a supervisor (e.g., behavioral)

The requalification should be defined on the reason and relative risk associated with the disqualification.

Rationale

The performance of personnel-related activities, manipulations, and interventions in the RABS during aseptic operations can pose a risk to the sterility of the product or adversely affect it if it is not performed in an aseptic manner. Therefore, proper training and qualification of personnel with APS experience is a must.

Q 3.4 Qualified vs Unqualified Interventions |

Original Text

Q 3.4. What are the recommendations for qualified and unqualified interventions?

Recommendation

Best Practices for Qualified Interventions

Interventions should be conducted by trained and qualified personnel. (See Question 3.2 on how to qualify.)

An approved standard operating procedure (SOP) containing all defined interventions with step-by-step instructions, mitigation actions (if any), and images (where applicable) should exist (1, 4, 7, 15, 16). A dynamic AVS should be performed simulating all the listed interventions, and these studies should be repeated with a defined frequency as described in Question 2.3.

Simulation of all qualified interventions in routine APS should be performed on a rotational basis; its maximum frequency of occurrence in routine batch-manufacturing should be covered in an initial qualification run and semiannual qualification runs. A rotation policy can be defined in an internal-site risk management process but should occur at least annually.

Management of Unqualified Interventions

When an unqualified intervention occurs, a real-time risk-based impact assessment shall be performed (See Question 1.7 on how to risk assess). If the assessment determines the unqualified intervention can be performed, only authorized personnel (See Question 3.2 on authorized personnel) may execute the unqualified intervention. If the risk assessment does not support the intervention as safe, then the intervention should not proceed and the appropriate steps to end the batch should be taken. As part of batch release, the impact of the unqualified intervention must be assessed for quality and compliance on the batch.

Unqualified interventions identified to become qualified interventions shall have AVS to demonstrate unidirectional airflow and preservation of First Air are not impacted. The unqualified intervention will then be included in the ongoing APS program and upon successful completion the intervention can be added to the local procedure. Unqualified interventions that are similar to qualified interventions may not require additional AVS execution or inclusion in the APS program, to become a qualified intervention, if proven equivalent with supporting technical and scientific rationale. In certain situations, unqualified interventions may not be recommended to become a qualified intervention, based upon special events, and will not require simulation in AVS and APS programs.

Rationale

Interventions should be designed and performed using proper aseptic techniques to minimize impact to the production environment and the risk of product contamination. An unqualified intervention does not have sufficient data to demonstrate the capability of control, hence, simulating the unqualified intervention in APS testing to demonstrate the process capability is recommended.

Q4.1 How do you ensure the integrity of RABS gloves? Q4.1 RABS ？

Original Text

Recommendation

There are various aspects to designing, maintaining, and verifying the integrity of a RABS. The potential loss of integrity can occur at many locations, including the RABS enclosure seams, baseplate openings, doors, glove ports, gloves, transfer ports, UDAF (in the case of an active RABS), and the lack of First Air.

A risk analysis should be conducted to assist in identifying design decisions and operational actions that have the potential for a loss of integrity. (See PDA Technical Report No. 54: Implementation of Quality Risk Management for Pharmaceutical and Biotechnology Manufacturing Operations for guidance on performing a risk analysis.)

As discussed in Questions 1.1-1.6 of this document, quality-by-design techniques can reduce the risk of incursions to RABS by considering process and user requirements, risk analysis, and designs that limit potential points of contamination. However, active monitoring and verification of RABS integrity is key to protecting the product and operator safety, where applicable in cRABS.

Considerations on monitoring glove integrity throughout a campaign is important as post campaign integrity test failures can be highly impactful. Also, integrity monitoring after significant interventions needs to be considered. Monitoring environmental conditions in real time within the oRABS or cRABS is essential to maintaining Grade A conditions to support RABS integrity. These parameters include:

  • Temperature
  • Humidity
  • Differential pressure
  • UDAF velocity
  • EM

In addition to real-time monitoring of these environmental conditions, glove integrity-testing, both visual assessment and a pressure test are required (1, 17). Firms should establish the procedures for this pressure test based on the specifications of their gloves and testing methods. This process should be incorporated into the CCS. (See Topics 4-6 of this PtC.)

A common pathway to RABS contamination is through the use of glove ports and, in particular, the loss of integrity of the glove material (4). While pre- and post-filling EM sampling may detect contamination via the gloves, it is imperative to verify the integrity of the glove itself. Glove integrity-testing should be performed based on an approved risk assessment. An approved risk assessment should determine the frequency of pressure testing, visual inspection, and glove changeout (6, 8). Additional glove visual assessments should be performed prior to each use during both campaign and batch production (3).

Rationale

It is critical for RABS to maintain integrity in order to support Grade A conditions. The RABS gloves design, use, and maintenance are key elements to ensuring appropriate control and overall integrity. RABS-related procedures and programs should encompass the continuous review of system and process improvements that support product quality.

Q4.2 What are the considerations for a RABS glove-management strategy? Q4.2 RABS ？

Original Text

Recommendation

A RABS glove-management strategy is essential to maintaining the RABS environment (i.e., product quality) and providing operator safety. This strategy includes glove selection, integrity testing, and an established glove changeout frequency (6). Interventions should be designed to in a way to minimize the risk of glove damage and loss of integrity. Within a Glove Management Strategy (part of CCS) the regime for glove integrity testing should be specified based on risk and impact of glove integrity breaches. Visual inspection, and where appropriate physical methods of glove integrity testing, should be applied as combined assurance that glove integrity has been maintained through critical process steps of set-up and during routine operations where inherent and corrective interventions apply.

Based on the glove selected and the established integrity-test requirements, the risk assessment should evaluate a preventive maintenance (PM) program to establish the duration of glove changeout and replacement frequency (6). For example, if certain gloves are experiencing higher wear due to machine setup and adjustment, operator interventions, and/or cleaning and disinfecting agents, the PM frequency may be increased.

Glove rotation can be considered to minimize the damage or failure rate for reusable-type gloves. Consideration should be given to the location(s) where the glove is subjected for low/high utilization during processing for rotation from batch or campaign operations (e.g., from a high-interventions zone to a low-interventions zone). The rotation policy and its recording procedure is to be defined by QRM for its traceability.

Rationale

The selection of the proper gloves, glove testing, and management of gloves is necessary to ensure operator safety and to maintain the RABS environmental conditions. The firm should evaluate their process and perform a risk assessment to determine an appropriate integrity test that reliably detects glove defects.

Q4.4 What is the response to a glove-integrity failure? Q4.4 ？

Original Text

Recommendation

In response to a glove-integrity failure, the actions to be taken will vary depending on when and where the failure is identified. The impact on previous batches must be always considered unless there is an approved end of batch glove integrity test available. A risk-based site policy and/or procedure should detail the actions required when a glove failure is discovered (1, 6). Typical responses to a glove failure include, but are not limited to the following:

  • If discovered during line setup and prior to RABS cleaning, disinfecting, and/or bio-decontamination, the nonintegral or damaged glove should be replaced, and the replacement should be documented per the site's procedures.
  • If discovered after RABS cleaning, disinfecting, and/or bio-decontamination but prior to product exposure inside the RABS, the glove should be replaced, disinfected or sterilized, and the action documented per the site's procedures. An investigation, including a root cause analysis of procedures, process, and equipment, is recommended.
  • If discovered during or after a production run, an investigation, including a root cause analysis of procedures, process, and equipment as well as the impact on the filled product, is recommended.

During the investigation process, the evaluation of the following elements should be considered:

  • Time and process step when the glove failure was identified
  • Who identified the glove failure and the associated interview
  • RABS location of the failed glove
  • Failure location on the glove (e.g., fingertip, palm, glove port interface)
  • Activities performed by those wearing the failed glove
  • Review of the gloves and RABS preventive maintenance records, including:
    • Recent glove integrity test results
    • Last glove changeout
  • Review of the batch records, including:
    • RABS gloves Incoming Quality Assurance documentation (e.g., material specifications)
    • RABS setup/glove installation
    • RABS cleaning/disinfecting
    • Filling interventions, both qualified and unqualified
    • Any batch-related deviations
  • Review of the operator's qualification and training records
  • Review of the glove integrity-test system qualification studies
  • Review of the current unidirectional air visualization studies
  • Review of batch EM data, both viable and total particles (See Topic 5.)
  • Review of batch bioburden samples data

The evaluation of these materials and records should be conducted according to the site's QRM system. This glove-integrity investigation should be conducted such that the impact on the product is clearly understood and suitable corrective and preventive actions are put in place (4).

Rationale

When nonintegral gloves or gloves with tears or pinhole leaks are detected, proper QRM must be used to determine the potential for contamination of the Grade A environment. While cleaning and disinfecting prior to the run, as well as continuous First Air, provides a level of protection, all integrity-test failures should be investigated. The investigation should determine the potential impact on process and product quality and whether any other processes or batches are potentially impacted. The reason for including or excluding a product or batch from the scope of the investigation should be clearly justified and documented.

Recommendation

To keep the gloves in good condition during the processing of a batch or maintenance intervention, any operation that could compromise glove integrity should be avoided. Q 5.1 — Environmental Monitoring Frequency (EM )

Original Text

Q 5.1. What should the environmental monitoring (EM) frequency be in a closed or open restricted access barrier system (cRABS or oRABS)?

Recommendation

The frequency of EM for oRABS and cRABS should be the same; continuous viable and total particle monitoring is required for the full duration of critical activities, including setup (1). (See Question 5.5.)

Critical activities start at the point when a product-contact part and/or a primary container or closure are transferred and are exposed to the inside of the RABS. Where the RABS is subdivided into discrete zones, the point where critical activities start and end may well be different within the different zones; hence, the continuous EM may start at different times in each zone (4).

Continuous viable (i.e., a combination of active and passive air monitoring) and total particle monitoring should begin at the start of the aseptic setup and should continue up until the end of filling. Typically, this is the end of the batch or campaign.

The RABS should be well designed to minimize the need for interventions involving opening the doors. By exception and where required, the doors should only be opened under strictly defined conditions, which should be procedurally defined and pre-assessed as part of the QRM. The conditions for the opening of the doors and controls in place to mitigate risks should be considered and documented as part of the overall CCS.

Appropriate controls should be in place to minimize the risk posed by the intervention through the opening of the doors and should extend to consideration of further disinfection steps. Additionally, controls should extend to the need to undertake a partial line clearance, for example, to discard exposed primary packaging containers or other direct product-contact parts located close to the intervention (e.g., vials, syringes, stoppers located on the stopper track). While sterility testing cannot be used to rationalize poor practices or otherwise support the undertaking of a high-risk intervention, based on risk, additional sterility samples should be considered as part of an additional control measure for critical interventions (4).

The same EM program applies to both product manufacture and APS.

Rationale

There is no difference in expectations around the frequency of EM inside oRABS and cRABS.

Once a critical surface is exposed inside the RABS, that surface is potentially at risk of contamination, so continuous EM is required.

The requirement for continuous EM ceases once the last unit is closed for the batch or campaign provided any used product-contact parts are reprocessed, and any sterile components are cleared prior to reuse of the line.

In the rare event that the RABS door is opened after line setup is completed, the critical zone is put at an elevated risk of contamination from the person(s) intervening and from the surrounding cleanroom. Additional controls should be considered to reduce risks of such interventions and should extend to consideration of additional disinfection steps and partial line clearances.

Monitoring should not interfere with production processes in a way that raises the level of risk by introducing an activity in the critical zone, which defeats the purpose of the EM.

Q 5.2 — Surface Monitoring in RABS (RABS )

Original Text

Q 5.2. Which surfaces should be monitored in a restricted access barrier system (RABS) (open or closed)?

Recommendation

Firms should conduct a risk assessment to define which surfaces of a RABS to monitor after use. Swabs should be used for complex geometric surfaces; contact plates should be used for flat surfaces. Sampling is only representative; not all surfaces can be sampled (e.g., not all filling needles and not all other surfaces above the critical surfaces).

Typically, based on risk, firms will sample:

  • Direct- and indirect-contact parts
  • Sterile tools that contact or breach First Air over contact parts or sterile primary packaging during the batch/campaign
  • RABS intervention gloves used during the batch/campaign for critical interventions*
  • RTP doors, if used, where the door breaches First Air over a critical surface
  • Parts above the critical surfaces that cannot be sterilized

*Note: When RABS intervention gloves are used ambidextrously, considerations should be given to EM sampling on multiple sides of gloves. This should be assessed using QRM principles.

Other locations linked to the risk assessment may also be sampled (e.g., occluded/hard to reach surfaces).

Surface samples are taken at the end of use (i.e., the batch or campaign). The surfaces sampled should be returned to a state suitable for aseptic manufacturing before the parts are reused. Typically, sterilized surfaces will be removed, subjected to a qualified cleaning process, and then resterilized. Other surfaces, including barrier gloves, should be cleaned and returned to a state suitable for aseptic manufacture.

Rationale

Surface monitoring is typically aimed at surfaces that have the greatest potential impact on product sterility because they directly or indirectly contact the product, or they are permitted to breach First Air (e.g., forceps).

A risk assessment is required to select and scientifically justify both the surfaces to be sampled and locations on those surfaces that are most representative of the risk. Sampling the entire product-contact surface is not necessary; instead, risk-based sampling should occur on the parts most likely to have been put at risk during installation or use.

RABS gloves are used for interventions; therefore, they are required to be sterile before installation and bio-decontaminated in situ thereafter. During use, RABS gloves are likely to contact surfaces inside the RABS that have been disinfected, not sterilized. Where open-door interventions are permitted, a barrier glove may well reside in a lower grade for the duration of the intervention and then be disinfected as the door is shut. Also, RABS gloves are made of flexible materials prone to puncture. RABS gloves that may have come into proximity with or breach First Air over sterile primary packaging, open product, or product-contact parts potentially puts the product at risk and should be sampled; such events should be avoided whenever possible.

Tools used for critical interventions should be sampled. Consideration should be given to the use of dedicated sterile tools that are used exclusively for conducting certain types of interventions (e.g., involving direct or indirect product-contact parts). Sterile tools should be kept in Grade A condition and maintained in a manner that prevents contamination (i.e., proper aseptic technique).

RTP doors are not sterile; they are bio-decontaminated inside and out in situ.

Surface samples are taken at the end of use because the surfaces being sampled need to be maintained both sterile and clean until the end of use; the samples allow this to be assessed as part of batch release. Care must be taken to ensure that the act of sampling does not put the surfaces being sampled at risk of contamination due to either improper sampling techniques, or the use of wrong or incorrectly prepared sampling equipment or consumables.

Q 5.3 — Transferring EM Plates Into/Out of RABS (EM )

Original Text

Q 5.3. What factors should be taken into consideration when transferring environmental monitoring (EM) plates into and out of a restricted access barrier system (RABS)?

Recommendation

Plates should be brought into the surrounding cleanroom sterile and suitably packaged for transfer into the RABS.

Plates should be transferred into and out of the RABS following QRM principles and good aseptic working practices. Suitable methods include, but are not limited to:

  • Plates sterilized in beta bags or containers that are transferred via alpha RTP ports in the RABS.
  • Plates sterilized in impermeable bags that enter and exit the RABS via an integral bio-decontamination (e.g., VPHP), rapid transfer airlock, or transfer chamber.
  • Transfer into the RABS occurs prior to line setup, with the outer bag being removed or disinfected at the Grade transition point. Exit occurs after the batch is complete. Transport to the testing laboratory should be done in a manner that prevents contamination.
  • Using plate sliders, with the outer surfaces of the plate being disinfected at the Grade transition point. Care must be taken to ensure that no disinfectant goes inside the plate. Design should ensure that no airflow enters the RABS from the cleanroom. An example of a plate slider can be seen in Figure 5.3-1.

It is not recommended to open RABS doors to transfer EM plates in or out of the RABS after line setup is complete or when critical surfaces are exposed.

Rationale

To provide reliable results, plates need to be sterile at the point of exposure.

RABS doors should not be opened after line setup; this includes the transfer of EM plates.

RTPs provide a method for safe entry of materials, including EM plates.

Transfer chambers provide a method whereby the risk posed by the entry of material previously exposed to Grade B conditions can be controlled by a combination of outer bag removal and sporicidal disinfection.

Q 5.4 — Media Plate Storage Inside RABS ( RABS )

Original Text

Q 5.4. How should media plates be stored inside a restricted access barrier system (RABS)?

Recommendation

Plate storage locations (i.e., either within the last layer bag or unbagged) should be risk-assessed and well-defined. Considerations include, but are not limited to, the following:

  • Plates should be stored away from product and product-contact parts in a location close to the sampling location.
  • Plates should not be stored in the same flow of First Air that is protecting a critical surface.
  • The operator should not need to reach over or pass close to a critical surface to interact with the EM plates.
  • Plates should not be stored close to air returns within a cRABS.
  • The use of plate stands is recommended (See Figure 5.4-1).

Rationale

Care must be taken to ensure that EM does not add contamination risk to the process. Risk-assessing the storage location and the linked activities (e.g., retrieval of a plate from pre-use storage, labeling, positioning, retrieval of the plate from the sampling location, relocation to post-use storage location) should be undertaken to define the correct storage location.

The storage location must ensure that the handling of the plates does not require the plate or the operator to breach First Air or come into proximity with sterile primary packaging, open product, or product-contact surfaces.

The defined storage location must also be selected with respect to the airflow within the RABS. The plates, and the packaging they arrive in, should not be allowed to block air exits or returns. Storage of the plates should be considered as part of the AVS (Air Visualization Study).

The storage of exposed and unexposed plates needs to be considered to prevent mix-ups and the potential reuse of a previously exposed plate, which may be dried out if used beyond allowed exposure time. Plate stands can aid in this, for example, by stacking plates such that fresh plates are removed from the bottom of the stack and used plates are returned to the top of the stack.

Q 5.5 — Personnel Monitoring During Setup and Open-Door Interventions ()

Original Text

Q 5.5. When and how should environmental and personnel monitoring be performed during setup and open-door interventions?

Recommendation

All open-door interventions need to be monitored from the point where the first critical surface enters the RABS. The minimum level of monitoring inside a RABS during critical activities is continuous viable and total particle monitoring; continuous viable monitoring is achieved by passive-air and active-air monitoring.

For open-door activities, including line set-up, monitoring within the intervention zone is recommended. This monitoring needs to be based on QRM principles and may need to be changed to ensure monitoring covers the performed activities. Sampling locations, as defined in the EM risk assessment, should be linked to qualified and unqualified interventions.

Additional samples might be taken, based on QRM principles, in the UDAF immediately outside the intervention zone, close to the operator and open-door. This can be achieved with either passive or active monitoring; samples should be taken at the working position level.

Each primary operator involved in open-door interventions, including line setup, should be monitored immediately as they exit the RABS Grade A interior, after having completed all the intervention steps in the critical zone, including any disinfection that may be required. The RABS door disinfection and closure should be done by the secondary operator.

Representative samples should be taken as defined in the risk assessment of the EM program. Sample locations must be selected as per the risk assessment. The risk assessment should consider any body part entering the Grade A environment and any location selected through the risk assessment, and these must meet Grade A limits (e.g., taking samples from the operator's RABS gloves, sleeves, head, and chest, as appropriate to the specific intervention).

It is recommended that a second, fully trained operator should support the monitoring to prevent false positive growth. This sampling should be done in an environment that prevents contamination of the sample (e.g., the UDAF zone of protection or another UDAF zone close to the RABS).

To be representative, samples should be taken as close as possible (i.e., physical and temporal) to the end of the operators' activities inside the RABS. Gowns should be removed and replaced following sampling. Care must be taken to ensure that agar is not transferred to the RABS or surrounding cleanroom where the sampling takes place, during the degowning and gown disposal step.

The individual whose clothing was sampled will then need to leave the cleanroom and replace some or all of their aseptic clothing (i.e., RABS gloves and gown). The second person should also open any doors to allow the person sampled to pass without transferring agar onto cleanroom surfaces.

Rationale

Entry of personnel into the RABS reduces the effectiveness of the barrier and potentially puts the product at risk of contamination. Performing personnel monitoring after, and active air sampling during, the open-door activities provides valuable additional data to support batch disposition.

An approved EM risk assessment should be used to determine the representative locations that require sampling meeting Grade A limits (e.g., body parts entering the Grade A environment). Relying only on glove-sampling data may not be adequate, especially where RABS gloves are disinfected or changed immediately prior to the intervention.

When sampling occurs close to the point where the person exits the RABS, the data collected will most closely reflect the risk posed by the operator to the RABS and will be less influenced by the surrounding lower-grade cleanroom and surfaces outside the RABS that are touched up to the point where the samples are taken. Ideally, a second person would sample the person exiting the RABS at the point they exit, this minimizes any external influences on the data (e.g., handling of plates, pens, documentation in Grade B) and ensures that the samples fully represent the risk posed by the first operator to the critical zone and have not been unnecessarily compromised.

Note: Operators should leave the RABS and change gloves to prevent the spread of media residues after finger dabs.

Q 5.6 — Separating EM Data for Setup vs. Filling (EM )

Original Text

Q 5.6. Should environmental monitoring (EM) for line setup and filling be kept separate?

Recommendation

Yes. While not mandated, there are benefits to keeping EM for line setup and filling separate. Keeping the data from open-door line setup separate aids the investigation and process improvement in the event of EM recoveries (1, 7).

Line setup activities usually pose a higher risk to the critical zone because they typically include frequent open-door interventions, material movements into the RABS, and the presence of personnel in the critical zone, whereas filling activities are typically conducted via closed-door interventions. Where EM recoveries occur, identifying the assignable root cause of the microbiological data deviation is aided when the data for the two activities are separate.

Q 5.7 — Fixed EM Air-Monitoring Sampling Point Locations ( EM )

Original Text

Q 5.7. Where should the fixed environmental monitoring (EM) air-monitoring sampling points be located within the restricted access barrier system (RABS)?

Recommendation

Firms should conduct a risk assessment in the design phase to determine the number and locations of EM samples that should be taken. (See PDA Technical Report No. 13 (Revised 2014): Fundamentals of an Environmental Monitoring Program.)

The sampling locations should be positioned as closely as possible to the most critical operations, such as the point of fill, sorting bowl, or rotary-table. They should be positioned vertically at or close to the height of the critical surface they are monitoring and ideally in the same plane.

Note: FDA's 2004 Guidance for Industry: Sterile Drug Products Produced by Aseptic Processing—Current Good Manufacturing Practice states:

"Air in the immediate proximity of exposed sterilized containers/closures and filling/closing operations would be of appropriate particle quality when it has a per-cubic-meter particle count of no more than 3520 in a size range of 0.5 μm and larger when counted at representative locations normally not more than 1 foot away from the work site, within the airflow, and during filling/closing operations. This level of air cleanliness is also known as Class 100 (ISO 5)."

Sampling locations should not breach First Air over critical surfaces. They should be positioned so that they capture the risks from any permitted intervention, for example, between the RABS door or glove and the critical surface being monitored, and between the door and critical surfaces where open-door interventions are permitted.

Viable- and total-particle sampling locations should be co-located wherever possible (if supported by risk-assessment) both to allow a point of comparison and for ergonomic reasons. Sampling locations should not cause an interference on the airflow between the sampling points (i.e., ideally isokinetic sampling for viable monitoring). AVS should demonstrate the noninterference during the sampling (e.g., passages of RABS gloves during the plate changing).

Total-particle sampling positions need to be set to allow a tube no longer than one meter (1 m) to connect the isokinetic sampling-head to the particle counter. Where lengths greater than 1 m must be used,* studies are required to assess the particle loss, in particular, the 5.0 μm particle loss, and determine if improvements are required. Ideally, the connecting tube would run straight. Any necessary bends should have smooth bend radii, where a 90° bend is achieved over no less than 15 cm of tube.

*Note: RABS should be designed/installed to avoid use exceeding 1 m.

Rationale

It is not possible to monitor the entire RABS. A risk assessment is required to position EM samples in locations where they are most likely to detect the risks posed from the main contamination risks, that is, conducting both open- and closed-door interventions.

Each unique particle size will have a different deposition profile in tubing based on, for example, airflow velocity and sample volume, tubing diameter, sampling port arrangement, temperature, bend radius, tube length, and tubing material. Because particulate monitoring attempts to assess a range of sizes (e.g., 0.5 μm and greater in optical diameter), no single set of radii or length conditions can describe best practices for each of these particle sizes. Minimizing the number of tubing bends, maximizing radii, and minimizing overall tubing length should be the goal, and it should be done in accordance with the particle-counter equipment manufacturer's recommended best practices.

The particle-counter equipment manufacturer should provide technical justification for its recommendations. Instrument calibration should address sampling configuration.

Note:

  • EU Annex 1 now states that tube length should typically be no longer than 1 m and any bend radii should be avoided.
  • International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 14644-1:2015 Cleanrooms and Associated Controlled Environments — Part 1: Classification of Air Cleanliness by Particle Concentration also states that tube length should be no longer than 1 m.

Q 6.1 How should indirect product contact surfaces be sterilized or decontaminated? Q 6.1 ？

Original Text

Recommendation

Indirect product-contact parts such as stopper bowls, hoppers, ancillary items (e.g., tweezers, forceps), and guides must be sterilized (1). This is typically done by wrapping the clean items in suitable semi-permeable packaging material and sterilizing them in a moist-heat autoclave, followed by unloading, transportation through the cleanroom environment, and aseptic assembly inside the RABS. Machine items that break First Air (e.g., sensors, holders, brackets at the filling line or rotary table) should be avoided where possible. When machine items do break First Air, they should be sterilized. (See Question 6.5 for more details.)

Based on facility layout and whether Grade A continuity can be applied for the sterilized items (e.g., by using UDAF carts), either single-wrapping or double-wrapping can be applied to ensure transport of the autoclaved, sterilized parts from the Grade B autoclave to the entrance of the RABS. To provide for aseptic removal and transfer of the parts into the RABS, good aseptic technique must be used to ensure that the inner layer or part is only exposed within the Grade A environment. The wrapping materials and removal process should minimize particle generation as much as possible.

When using double wrapping, the outer wrapping is ideally removed at the barrier door, where the contents are still protected by the inner barrier. For large items, such as the stopper bowl, two people may be needed to properly complete the aseptic transfer. That is, the primary and secondary operators are responsible for different steps: typically, one for the transfer activities outside the RABS, and one for the transfer activities inside the RABS. The inner wrapping is then removed toward the end of the assembly process under Grade A conditions, depending on the design of the manufacturing line just before or after the RABS doors are closed. In case parts of the assembly are done as a closed-door intervention, the wrapping could be removed at an earlier stage to enable removal of the wrap from the RABS just before door closure or as a final open-door intervention. Special considerations must be taken in the sequence of removing the wrapping so as to ensure the First Air of already unwrapped parts is not breached during the removal of other wrappers.

Automated surface bio-decontamination processes such as VPHP, are not considered equivalent to sterilization processes, however, they can reduce bioburden risk if applied after the set-up process is complete and the RABS doors are closed.

If items can be directly unloaded into the cRABS (e.g., via RTP) or an aseptic transfer from the autoclave to the RABS is otherwise guaranteed (e.g., via Grade A continuity, typically a process requiring rigorous controls), different means of wrapping and/or dry-heat sterilization of the indirect product-contact items may be applied. The wrapping materials and removal process should minimize particle generation as much as possible.

In addition to open-door installation, an introduction of indirect product-contact parts via RTP or sterile boxes can be considered for the RABS to minimize the risk of potential contamination during installation by open doors. This especially applies for smaller parts such as guiding and stopper guardrails, for example.

Rationale

Indirect product-contact surfaces are critical for the sterility assurance of the product and must be sterilized (1).

Q 6.5 How should equipment, accessories, ancillary items and sterile tools be transferred into a RABS? Q 6.5 、、 RABS ？

Original Text

Recommendation

As a general concept, the transfer of all materials and equipment into the Grade B and, finally, the Grade A RABS should be a unidirectional process, separated from the path for removal of those items. Material and equipment introduced, whenever possible, should be wrapped and sterilized, when necessary.

The principles discussed below are based on the criticality of the item regarding product contact.

Direct Product Contact Parts

Direct product-contact parts (e.g., vials, stoppers, piping, single-use systems (SUSs), connectors or components), which have come into direct contact with the product, must be sterilized (1, 4). This is founded on two basic principles:

  • If the filling equipment has CIP and SIP functionality, then the parts can either remain in the RABS or parts of it can be assembled, followed by a validated SIP process, rendering the equipment and filter (where applicable) sterile. (See PDA Technical Report No. 61: Steam in Place for additional information.)
  • If the filling equipment needs to be manually assembled and has no SIP functionality, either presterilized (e.g., gamma-irradiated) single-use assemblies are used, or alternatively, if the filling equipment is sterilized by wrapping the clean items in suitable semipermeable packaging material and sterilized in a moist-heat autoclave, followed by aseptic assembly inside the RABS. If items can be directly unloaded into the cRABS (e.g., via RTP) or an aseptic transfer from the autoclave to the RABS is guaranteed (e.g., via Grade A continuity, typically a process requiring rigorous controls), different means of wrapping and/or dry-heat sterilization of the indirect product-contact items may be applied. Where presterilized, the equipment should be fully assembled (as far as possible) to minimize the need for aseptic connections.

Indirect Product Contact Parts

Indirect product-contact parts (e.g., stopper bowls, hoppers, tweezers), which have come into indirect contact with the product, are to be sterilized and handled in a similar way to direct product-contact parts (1). (See Question 6.1 for more detail.)

Machine Items that Break First Air

Machine items that break First Air (e.g., sensors, holders, brackets at the filling line or rotary table) should be avoided where possible. This must be part of the initial design considerations for a RABS. Where a First Air interruption is not avoidable, machine items should be moist-heat sterilizable. Where this is not technically possible, sporicidal disinfection and a risk assessment of each individual part should be carried out and included in the CCS (1). These parts should have both the highest-possible level of cleanability (preferably with sporicidal agents) and the lowest-possible air interruption (e.g., fewer gaps/holes, be as small as possible, ergonomically shaped). Inclusion in the EM program (e.g., surface sampling at the end of manufacture) needs to be considered.

Miscellaneous Items that Break First Air

Miscellaneous items that potentially break First Air (e.g., forceps for removing stuck stoppers) must be sterile and remain sterile during their intended use inside the RABS. Those items preferably can be sterilized by moist heat and wrapped in semi-permeable packaging, or by dry heat in stainless-steel boxes (care should be applied on appropriate closure of those boxes). Each individual unit should not contain more items than are reasonably needed for the completion of a batch or campaign. To avoid contamination of the sterile items during their intended usage, suitable holders should be designed inside the Grade A space to avoid reaching over the critical parts of the item with the RABS gloves.

Transfer of Items

Methods to transfer wrapped packages into a RABS depends on whether or not the RABS has automated surface-decontamination functionality (e.g., VPHP). If this is the case, the outside surface of packages could be surface-disinfected by the automated process after a transfer through a cleanroom area. If surface-disinfection is purely manual, either an outer-pouch should be removed if the items were sterilized double-wrapped (preferred) or a manual disinfection of the package/wrapping needs to take place when the items enter the Grade A RABS.

Non-Product Contact Equipment and Parts

Non-product-contact equipment/parts (e.g., format parts such as vial guides) are not always suitable for moist- or dry-heat sterilization. Those are parts formally not required to be sterile but must meet Grade A conditions regarding surface cleanliness when inserted into the RABS. Those parts can either be disinfected manually or with the help of automated surface-disinfection processes (e.g., VPHP or similar). When removing the parts from the RABS (if stored outside the RABS without cleaning (i.e., for product dedicated parts)) and before re-usage, the parts should be protected (e.g., by adding a protective pouch) while still within Grade A. If parts are cleaned, they should be protected during storage and before re-usage.

Removal of Items from RABS

Once RABS doors are closed, the removal process of any items from the RABS (e.g., exposed plates or package/wrapping) must not impact the unidirectional airflow protecting direct and indirect product-contact surfaces. In addition, the removal should follow a unidirectional process where the flow of material into the RABS is separated from the flow of items to be removed. For best practice in cRABS, RTPs placed at different and suitable locations may be used to facilitate the transfer of material. Processing and waste-removal flows should be considered during the equipment design and procurement phase, the placement of access ports should be recorded in the equipment User Requirements Specification, and the rationale should be documented in the Design Qualification and/or CCS.

Rationale

Transfer techniques into and out of the RABS are based on the type of material/accessory/equipment, its cleanability and disposal. Either sterilization or surface decontamination is needed.

Q 7.1 Cleaning and Disinfection Program Planning Q 7.1

Original Text

Q 7.1. What are the considerations for planning, designing, and executing a restricted access barrier system (RABS) cleaning and disinfection program?

Recommendation

Before implementing a cleaning and disinfection program, a RABS cleanability assessment should be performed. This assessment should include the suitability of material surfaces to be cleaned as described in Question 1.2. In addition, cleaning accessibility in terms of ergonomics, the extent of reach required into the RABS, and areas that need cleaning tools to reach should be considered. Hard to clean mechanical spaces and surfaces in processing areas should have been eliminated by design as a basic GMP requirement (4). As disinfection programs must include the use of an approved sporicidal, the materials of construction within the RABS should be assessed to be compatible with such disinfectant agents.

*Note: Sensitive equipment (e.g., environmental monitoring equipment) must be considered during cleaning, disinfection and/or decontamination and may need to be covered at a certain cleaning step. This should be documented in the CCS.

Typically, RABS will include installed process equipment (e.g., filling lines) that are also subjected to cleaning and disinfection together with the RABS barrier. Cleanability assessments will therefore include suitability and access of process equipment surfaces for cleaning and disinfection procedures.

Cleaning is a prerequisite to effective bio-decontamination processes. Initially, at start-up and after shutdowns, a cleaning agent with a surfactant will assist with the removal of bonded layers of soiling (e.g., lubricants, grease, fatty acids from human contact). Cleaning agents are likely to leave residues that may require a follow-up wipe removal step with sterile 70% IPA for example, and such actions should be included in SOP control design (1, 6).

Agents will be validated to demonstrate the appropriate log reduction, including bacteria, spores, and viruses (as required), with the target of achieving Grade A conditions in the RABS. The process in which agents are manually applied must be qualified to ensure appropriate environmental conditions are rendered (6). RABS Grade A conditions must be established and under control before sterilized parts, including fluid path or filling needles and indirect product-contact parts (e.g., stopper bowls, guides, chutes), are introduced (1).

Bio-decontamination processes (e.g., VPHP, dry or wet process technologies) should be automated; an emphasis should be on a validated cleaning process prior to initiating the bio-decontamination process.

More automated bio-decontamination processes (e.g., VPHP, dry or wet process technologies) may be applied to oRABS (in combination with cleanroom) and cRABS. If disinfection cannot be implemented manually (i.e., considered in design), and if enhanced sporicidal efficacy and disinfection assurance is required.

In such applications, cleaning is not replaced by automated disinfection alone and remains an essential pre-step. In first application or following shutdowns cleaning, where more surface-soiling may be present, a neutral detergent with a surfactant may be appropriate, but inevitability it will leave residues, so a following residue-removal step would be required, typically with isopropanol.

A cleanability assessment, selection of cleaning and disinfectant agents, and associated methods of application should be defined in a cleaning risk assessment to cover the risk-based decision-making requirement as part of the QRM. Having a rationale in place, a qualification methodology can be developed ahead of qualification protocols and execution.

Manual Cleaning and Disinfections of RABS

RABS require cleaning and disinfection to establish and maintain Grade A conditions. Manual processes for these steps are described below.

Cleaning is the removal of soiling, residual product, and nonviable contaminants. The process is typically manual, completed with wipes that apply cleaning agents and a physical removal (i.e., collection in a wipe) of contaminants (1, 6).

Disinfection is a process of applying, via a qualified procedure, a laboratory-validated disinfectant that provides an appropriate log reduction of bioburden to specified levels. All disinfection programs for RABS should include a sporicidal agent (1, 6). Appropriate residue removal is required after cleaning using purified/sterile water (e.g., WFI) or sterile isopropanol.

There are two stages of RABS disinfection:

  1. Establishing control of the Grade A processing environment on first installation, after shutdown, and between batches and campaigns. This first step of disinfection should include a sporicidal agent, hence, sporicidal disinfection of the complete internal processing area within the RABS.
  2. Routine disinfection in production, which includes after open-barrier interventions and wiping down the barrier door before closing. The selection of the disinfectant should be based on a risk assessment and assurance that Grade A conditions are not compromised.

It is recommended that primary and secondary operators carry out these disinfection tasks related to interventions. Interventions into Grade A processing zones should be undertaken by a primary operator with the necessary localized internal-zone disinfection followed by personnel monitoring, based on an approved risk assessment, prior to closing the operations. The secondary operator should complete the internal RABS door disinfection before closing the RABS barrier.

Rationale

If RABS are cleaned and disinfected by manual application methods, the prerequisite of a cleanability assessment is paramount.

Different combinations of cleaning and disinfectant agents may be applied based on process steps and levels of soiling.

Q 7.2 Consistent Execution of Cleaning, Bio-Decontamination, and Disinfection Q 7.2 、

Original Text

Q 7.2. What are the current options for ensuring consistent execution of cleaning, bio-decontamination, disinfection, and sporicidal disinfection of a restricted access barrier system (RABS)?

Recommendation

Disinfectant agents will require qualification or validation to provide evidence of efficacy on surfaces for a defined contact time, typically referred to as "wet contact time" (2). Different disinfectant qualification methodologies can be applied based on the best combination of international standards and guidance.

Aseptic-process filling would require Grade A particulate and microbial cleanliness levels inside the RABS, with cleaning and disinfection considered as the surface-contamination control measure (1). The outside of the RABS barrier would require following the cleanroom grading level (in filling Grade B).

Disinfectant agents are validated for efficacy in laboratory studies, including material coupon surface-disinfection studies, with subsequent qualification of the application method and procedure. EM is used to assess the effectiveness of the cleaning and disinfection program (1, 6). In-field efficacy challenge studies are not typically implemented for manual disinfection applied to RABS in contrast to automated VPHP process technologies, bio-decontamination, and alternative sporicidal-agent processes that require biological-indicator (BI) inactivation challenge studies (4).

The bio-decontamination processes of VPHP process technologies have the potential of achieving 6-log reduction when combined with the required qualification of the automated gaseous-disinfection system and method of application with associated control parameters (6). Once the bio-decontamination-equipment integration is qualified, a process of cycle development should be applied, followed by an efficacy-performance qualification (PQ) with a BI reference challenge of Geobacillus stearothermophilus spores on stainless coupons. VPHP BI's are described in PDA Technical Report No. 51 Biological Indicators for Gas and Vapor-Phase Decontamination Processes: Specification, Manufacture, Control and Use.

Enzymatic indicators (EIs) are increasingly playing a role in cycle development to improve efficiency (time savings) and assessment of location-to-location efficacy. However, accepting the initial BI/EI correlation studies is required, and the final cycle qualification currently requires BI challenge studies.

The outcome of manual disinfection, following the application of a qualified agent according to a qualified SOP, is assessed by the EM program. The EM program assesses the effectiveness of the qualified disinfection and bio-decontamination process. RABS cleaning and disinfection qualification should align with ISO 14644-1 requirements for cleanrooms and associated controlled environments and with EU Annex 1 and FDA guidances on manufacturing sterile products by aseptic processing for qualification before conducting APS qualification in readiness for production activities.

Open-door interventions into a RABS barrier during aseptic processing, a rare occurrence, requires additional cleaning and disinfection of the open barrier door, including the inside of the barrier door, before it is closed to reestablish Grade A conditions of the complete barrier. Protective airflow and procedure control should result in the sterilized parts that enter during setup remaining sterile without compromise. Disinfection applied to sterile surfaces could potentially compromise sterility and therefore should be avoided, instead focusing on the procedural control and protective airflow that prevents compromise to the sterile surfaces in the first place.

Rationale

Primarily, RABS are manually cleaned and disinfected using qualified agents and a qualified procedure of application of these qualified agents executed by trained operators. EM, in combination with other microbial challenges (e.g., gowning, material transfers, and environmental control systems), is used to monitor the outcome (effectiveness).

Some of the enhanced attributes in contamination control that isolators provide, are sometimes applied to RABS to enhance the assurance of sterility. In particular, cRABS and oRABS (in combination with cleanroom) with VPHP can align elements of isolators and RABS technology.

The combined cleanroom and oRABS bio-decontamination, using an automated process, can support more assured aseptic hold times and fast changeovers to reestablish the required conditions for aseptic manufacturing. Such processes can be applied, with BI results (negative for growth) defined as the marker, to put the line back in operation at risk, rather than waiting for EM results.

Q 7.3 Qualification and Validation of Cleaning, Disinfection, and Decontamination Q 7.3 、

Original Text

Q 7.3. What considerations are required for qualification of the restricted access barrier system (RABS) cleaning, bio-decontamination, disinfection, and sporicidal disinfection process, and validation of the bio-decontamination and decontamination process?

Recommendation

The qualification of the RABS cleaning and disinfection process is critical to ensuring a Grade A environment within the RABS. The planning of the qualification should begin as early as possible prior to implementation. A key output of the design phase of a RABS installation is a quality risk assessment detailing the RABS physical attributes and usage scenarios that may have an impact on product quality. This risk assessment then feeds into a validation plan of which cleaning and disinfection validation is a major component.

There are different facets to the RABS cleaning and disinfection (bio-decontamination) and decontamination validation process:

  • Establishing Grade A conditions for aseptic manufacturing
  • Applying cleaning validation for drug-product residue removal as a cross-contamination control measure

Decontamination may be applied as a cross-contamination control measure and/or a measure to manage operator exposure to toxic or biologically hazardous products. Where aseptic-containment is required to process toxic or biohazard products, it may be more appropriate to apply isolator barrier technology because of the inherent limitations of containment measures (e.g., air-overspill to the surrounding environment) in RABS.

RABS Cleaning Process

The qualification of the RABS cleaning process should provide confidence of the:

  • Removal of any residue (chemical, including lubricants) or debris that would detrimentally impact the effectiveness of the disinfecting agent to be used. The target should be visibly clean (1).
  • Reduction of bioburden before the disinfection process step, as determined by the end-user.

RABS Disinfection Process

The qualification and validation of the RABS disinfection process should provide confidence that the disinfection procedure demonstrates its effectiveness in reducing any microbiological contamination within the RABS to an acceptable level. The disinfection process should include:

  1. Use of a sporicidal agent, possibly in rotation with other disinfectant agents that together provide broad spectrum efficacy (7). The selection and frequency of use should be based on EM data (quantitative/qualitative), trends, cleanroom materials, and the efficacy of the disinfectant procedures (2).
Note: Rotation of the disinfectants is not considered due to increasing microbial resistance but because different disinfectants have different modes of action, and their combination can increase the spectrum of efficacy. Rotation may also reduce occurrence of material damage.
  1. Use of sterile disinfectant agents for Grade A and Grade B areas.
  2. A target log reduction for bacteria, spores, and viruses (if applicable) (6, 20).
  3. A method of application that assures surface coverage and wet contact time.
  4. A disinfection waste management procedure dealing with wipes, residual agents, and disinfection tools.
  5. Any associated aseptic RABS hold-time qualification.
  6. Environmental control/EMPQ should be qualified and established before implementation of an APS; confidence that the cleaning and disinfection process meets this objective of environmental control is critical to GMP compliance (4).

RABS Decontamination Process

The qualification and validation of a RABS decontamination process applied post-production for product-residue removal as a cross-contamination control measure requires cleaning-validation studies. The following are points to consider:

  • Cleaning methods may be manual (wiping) or more automated CIP systems (e.g., spray balls, spray wands). In both cases, the target is to remove residual products from surfaces.
  • Cleaning validation includes surface-swabbing studies and chemical analysis to confirm removal of product residuals to target levels considered as safe exposure to operators and/or not at risk of cross-contaminating other products or subsequent batches.
  • Where more automated CIP systems apply, control system development will be required with subsequent qualification.

WFI may be used as an agent for product-residual removal for treatment off-line:

  • Chemical agents that denature products in RABS may need a follow-up residue removal step.
  • If CIP systems are applied, sloping surfaces and "zero" dead-leg valves may be required to mitigate collected water that may exacerbate microbial contamination or proliferation.
Note: Disinfectant efficacy studies or validation studies should be conducted as well to demonstrate that the disinfectants used on surfaces in manufacturing areas, laboratories, and other facility areas are effective in the inactivation or removal of such microorganisms as bacteria, fungi (yeast and molds), and bacterial spores. (See PDA Technical Report No. 70: Fundamentals of Cleaning and Disinfection Programs for Aseptic Manufacturing Facilities for further details.)

Rationale

The primary purpose of a RABS is to provide people-operator and process-product-barrier separation so that the risk of contaminating sterile product is mitigated to acceptable levels. As such, it is important to determine the most appropriate bio-decontamination method for the RABS. The qualification and validation of the bio-decontamination methods should consider the cleaning agents, the cleaning application processes, and the associated processes and equipment risks. These requirements and processes should be documented and proceduralized for repeatability and monitored to ensure continuous compliance.

Strong process understanding is the best basis for determining RABS validation requirements. Robust monitoring and aseptic manufacturing processes will add to the confidence that the manufacturing process is under a state of control. Decisions should be made based on the facilities process, control structure, and process understanding. Based on those decisions, validation programs should be initiated and followed. Decisions on the frequency of revalidation should be included in the bio-decontamination validation program.

Q 8.1 — APS in RABS: Special Considerations Q 8.1 — RABS （APS）

Original Text

Q 8.1. What are the special considerations for aseptic process simulation (APS) in a restricted access barrier system (RABS)?

Recommendation

A risk-based analysis and approach taken for APS are recommended for all aseptic processing technologies, including RABS systems. By design, the APS should imitate routine manufacturing conditions and should ensure that all critical steps are challenged. A RABS relies on the surrounding cleanroom environment to maintain Grade A conditions for sterile processing. To maintain Grade A, the RABS routinely includes cleaning and disinfection processes, in addition to sterilization processes for processing accessories and equipment. By design, the RABS should only provide for open-door intervention during setup and, by exception, during operations. The cleanroom qualification, operator training, cleaning, and disinfection programs should all be qualified.

There are several key considerations that need to be established in support of APS, and they should include, but are not limited to:

  • Cleanroom and clean air equipment, such as UDAF units, must be qualified according to the required characteristics of the environment.
  • The RABS must be qualified to meet all requirements for aseptic processing including HEPA-filter integrity-testing, pressure differential across the filters, air velocity testing, and AVS. AVS should confirm unidirectional airflow that sweeps over and away from exposed products during processing, without increased risk of contamination through turbulent airflow. Airflow visualization qualification and monitoring should be applied at open-door interventions as a prerequisite to all APS designs to ensure environmental-control conditions are qualified in readiness for an APS.
    Note: APS cannot be used to justify poor design of equipment and processes.
  • Operator training and qualification extending to Grade A and Grade B aseptic gowning and aseptic behavior training must be complete. Operators undertaking RABS cleaning and disinfection programs should be qualified and operations should be implemented procedurally.
  • A list of authorized inherent and corrective interventions should be designed, defined, and appropriately assessed and documented in the CCS to minimize the risk of contamination. Open-door interventions should be a rare and exceptional occurrence. When required, the need to take representative sterility samples should be considered, and the intervention should be effectively demonstrated in an APS.
  • Qualified methods are in place for the transfer of materials that enter the cleanroom, and subsequently the RABS, that do not compromise the cleanroom-surrounding environment or the RABS Grade A processing zone.
  • Risk assessments support the establishment of a comprehensive EM program, which should be based on detailed knowledge of the process, facility, and equipment. Sampling locations, sampling frequency and type of EM monitoring (e.g., active air, passive, contact plate) should be defined based on risk assessment output.

The use of a "piggyback" APS can be considered as part of the overall APS design. A piggyback APS is where an APS run is undertaken at the end of a routine single or campaign filling process and can be used in support of validating an extended run or campaign length/duration. There are specific limitations associated with a piggyback APS which, by design, does not provide for an appropriate simulation of activities and variables that are only performed for the first lot of a campaign, for example, setup activities. Consideration also needs to be given to potential interferences of residual product with the growth-promoting properties of the media used in the APS. Consequently, a piggyback APS alone will not fulfill the validation requirements to demonstrate an effective APS program. Rather, its use can be considered as part of an overall effective APS program, which also must include an APS strategy that covers setup and other potential worst-case scenarios.

The scheduling of piggyback APS in conjunction with traditional APS should be supported by a formal risk assessment, for which the limitations of the piggyback approach and its use are considered.

Rationale

The planning of open- and closed-door interventions and simulation of worst-case criteria should be based on risk management principles. The same criteria for the design of an APS as stated in PDA Technical Report No. 22 (Revised 2011): Process Simulation for Aseptically Filled Products are also appropriate for all RABS.

After establishing Grade A conditions inside a RABS, the barrier technology provides a physical and aerodynamic barrier to contamination ingress to Grade A when the barrier is closed. The barrier is open at setup where presterilized materials are transferred and are installed. At this time, both Grade A conditions and the sterilized parts are at an increased risk for contamination and, thus, can compromise sterility.

If rare open-door interventions take place during aseptic manufacturing, there is also a risk escalation that needs mitigation by design, qualifications, and procedural controls, and is subject to additional qualification in an APS. Such in-process significant interventions require consideration for sterility samples to be taken and should be included in the APS.

Simulation of the production activities is a necessity for designing the APS studies.

Q 8.2 — APS for Multiple Shifts and Campaign Production Q 8.2 — （APS）

Original Text

Q 8.2. What are the special considerations of the aseptic process simulation (APS) for multiple shifts and multiple-day campaign production in the restricted access barrier system (RABS)?

Recommendation

The duration of the APS should be based on risk assessment and sufficient to adequately challenge the aseptic process. The duration should be long enough to allow simulation of the predetermined interventions and activities and to fill the required units.

Where there are no risk-based time-related effects and where longer duration does not pose a risk to the routine production process, there is no requirement that the APS should be equal to or longer than the maximum production duration.

The following points need to be considered when creating an APS for campaign manufacturing:

  • Setup
  • Number and duration of interventions based on risk management principles to ensure that the APS is representative of the routine manufacturing process and qualified interventions (type, frequency, and duration)
  • Indirect product-contact parts remain installed for the duration for requalification of the campaign and are subject to an aseptic hold time (the duration may need to be shortened based on the risk assessment evaluation)
  • Replacement or leaving in place of direct product-contact parts (i.e., product filling needles), should be representative of the routine manufacturing process exposure time
  • RABS gloves remain installed for the duration of the campaign
  • Cleaning/disinfection practices occurring inside the RABS during routine campaign manufacturing should be simulated
  • Certain circumstances when performing an APS related to a production hold and/or interruptions
  • Taking of sterility samples after significant critical interventions, if justified

The approach to APS with a "piggyback" qualification is not considered a best practice as setup is missing in the final APS and not representative of the full campaign.

Rationale

Contamination of an aseptic process is primarily an outcome of events rather than time. Therefore, the duration of the APS should be sufficient to assess the performance of those activities identified in the risk assessment as having the potential to introduce contamination.

The APS is not sensitive enough to uncover the flaws, weaknesses, or failures in the integrity of the RABS, the environmental quality, or the ability of the personnel to perform activities over time.

The APS should challenge and confirm the effectiveness of these contamination control measures. This means that the maintenance of environmental conditions should be assured and assessed by the RABS system design and EM. Good process design and well-controlled activities required between the batches within a campaign (e.g., fill needles or glove replacement), RABS gloves ergonomics, training and qualification of personnel, assessment of human factors, and scheduling of breaks and rest periods for operators are better tools for controlling the complexity of RABS campaign operations than extended APS runs.

Q 8.3 — Worst-Case Conditions in RABS APS Q 8.3 — RABS （APS）

Original Text

Q 8.3. What are the special considerations for determining worst-case conditions in a restricted access barrier system (RABS) aseptic process simulation (APS)?

Recommendation

Based on the features of the operation, the worst-case scenarios chosen for inclusion in an APS should be predefined. The best way to determine the suitable worst-case scenarios is to evaluate the APS, taking into account the pertinent elements of the process and the aseptic process risk assessment. The worst-case condition should be considered within the expected operational boundary. Worst-case does not mean creation of artificial conditions or environments that exceed allowed operating conditions and can force a system failure.

"Note: APS is not used to validate bad practices."

A few examples of worst-case conditions include, but are not limited to:

  • Open-door interventions (if required) in addition to the setup
  • Closed-door interventions (inherent/corrective)

The following parameters generally have a lower impact on the product-contamination risk, since the Grade A RABS interior environment with exposed product/critical surfaces are separated from the lower-classified environment and operators:

  • Hold time of sterile materials (e.g., filtration and filling equipment, filtered media solution, sterile garments/goggles)
  • Filling duration
  • Simulation of the maximum or minimum number of personnel
  • Speed of the filling machine (i.e., slowest speed for largest container considering the maximum-duration vial remains open online and the highest speed for the smallest container considering difficulties in handling)

Any intervention required via opening the door shall be considered along with the frequency and duration for the simulation in the APS.

Interventions that do not require handling via an open door but need manual adjustment via RABS gloves and tools shall be simulated in a similar way.

A risk-based approach shall be followed in order to determine the frequency, duration, and criticality of the intervention.

Rationale

Open-door interventions represent the highest risk for product contamination and therefore should be the focus of the predefined worst-case conditions in the APS. In addition, the design, barrier properties, and the closed-door interventions of RABS might provide other worst-case conditions, which should be considered in the design of APS. Companies should conduct thoughtful risk assessments to help determine which conditions and configurations represent the most useful challenge to the process and how to most effectively include them in the APS.

Q 8.4 — Setup and Assembly Best Practices Q 8.4 —

Original Text

Q 8.4. What is the best practice when performing the setup and assembly of a restricted access barrier system (RABS) filling line?

Recommendation

There are two setup conditions for RABS: Initially, with an open barrier door, and then, readiness for production operations with the barrier door closed where full EM is implemented and critical tasks involving sterilized parts are completed, for example, positioning of filling needles (if single-use). As much as possible, set-up should be performed with the doors closed so careful design and understanding of the equipment is essential.

The open-door set-up process presents the highest risk of contamination when transferring in or assembling sterilized parts or components.

Based on risk knowledge and the role First Air takes in preventing contamination ingress into the Grade A zone, operators need to position themselves so protective airflow passes over body parts inserted into the RABS (such as hands and sleeves) and into the surrounding cleanroom. Positioning of materials ready for transfer should also consider protective airflow exiting the RABS Grade A zone and from the overhead or over-open-door UDAF. AVS should demonstrate the appropriate airflow from the interior of the RABS to the surrounding area.

Materials and parts should be presented for transfer at working position, typically on carts.

A secondary operator could support positioning parts and materials provided they do not compromise the protective airflow.

Good aseptic technique (e.g., transfer of inner bag at the RABS interface by double-bag methods) is required on open-barrier-door entry, and the operator's reach should be limited to the required task without touching other surfaces, critical or otherwise. Practices of hand disinfection prior to open-door interventions should be based on the contact time recommended and qualified practices. (See PDA TR-62 for additional information.)

During the intervention, EM sampling locations in the proximity of the intervention should be subject to continuous EM (i.e., viable and nonviable). The surrounding cleanroom should also be monitored at the door opening/operator interface. (See Topic 5.)

Installation of sterilized RABS gloves after open-door setup is commonly done as the final procedure. (See Topic 9.)

Before and at the time barrier doors are closed, the inside of the barrier door should be disinfected. The RABS gloves should be drawn back so only the RABS glove port entry interface should require disinfection (in some cases, covers may be inserted to prevent RABS glove contamination at open-door interventions).

With the barrier door closed, full EM should be implemented and should be ready for monitoring throughout the production-readiness setup.

Installation setup of all critical parts requires good aseptic technique, possibly with the use of tools (e.g., tweezers, tongs) and throughout the procedure. First Air protection should not be compromised.

Rationale

As interventions are such a high-risk activity, best practice is needed in all cases to mitigate risks to an acceptable level.

Q 9.1 Aseptic Techniques for Closed- and Open-Door Interventions Q 9.1

Original Text

Q 9.1. Is it necessary to use the same aseptic techniques and practices when performing closed- and open-door interventions in the restricted access barrier system (RABS)?

Recommendation

To ensure product sterility, operators performing aseptic operations must adhere to aseptic techniques for both closed- and open-door interventions. As part of a company's comprehensive CCS, a risk assessment must be developed to ensure all intervention steps are appropriately defined, including required mitigation actions, for qualified interventions. All open-door interventions are to be minimized and should have a qualified maximum time limit.

When assessing the risk of an intervention, the following should be considered:

  • Overall contamination risk to product (e.g., open units, exposed components), based on personnel location, movement, and/or RABS glove proximity
  • Maintenance of First Air
  • Including the accessibility of the area to perform the intervention
  • Supported with AVS
  • Consideration of line clearance (e.g., removal of compromised units)
  • Disinfection
  • Area where the intervention takes place needs to be accessible for disinfection, if required (Disinfection prior to EM should be avoided.)
  • Monitoring the intervention
  • Viable (e.g., surface(s), operator(s),) and total particulates
  • Start and end time of intervention (i.e., duration)
  • Before resuming operation, appropriate evaluation and line clearances performed (e.g., disinfection, verified removal of compromised units)

Note: See Topic 4 for clarification on recommended aseptic techniques.

Furthermore, while a RABS with closed doors provides an improved separation between the operator and the product, it decreases the likelihood of nonsterile product; therefore, the risk is still present and should be appropriately controlled.

Rationale

While a RABS with closed doors provides enhanced protection of the product between the operator and extraneous contamination sources (e.g., operator, environment), proper aseptic technique is still required to aid in the overall control of the product. A risk assessment should be documented outlining the necessary controls required to maintain product sterility.

Q 9.2 Qualification and Approval of Interventions Q 9.2

Original Text

Q 9.2. How should interventions be qualified and approved for use in routine production?

Recommendation

All interventions should be included in a documented risk assessment associated with a comprehensive CCS. This documented risk assessment should consider the following risk factors:

  • Duration of the intervention (in minutes)
  • Complexity of the intervention (number of steps)
  • Proximity of the intervention to sterile components (equipment, product)
  • Exposure of personnel

Additional factors may be considered when assessing the risk associated with execution of an intervention. The assessment must consider the use of sterile equipment and tools (e.g., forceps) to minimize impact on the RABS environment when performing an intervention.

All qualified interventions should have the airflow visualization recorded and documented to ensure that the impact of performing the interventions are thoroughly understood. This includes inherent and corrective interventions that can be performed through RABS gloves and open-door activities. First Air principles shall be included in this airflow visualization and must not be ignored during execution of interventions. Q 9.3 Interventions that Disrupt First Air Q 9.3

Original Text

Q 9.3. Should interventions that disrupt First Air, as defined in EU Annex 1, be permitted?

Recommendation

The placement of RABS glove positions and the aseptic operation should be designed to avoid the disruption of unidirectional airflow above or in the proximity of exposed sterile product or critical surfaces. Interventions should be designed in a manner that prevents the disruption of First Air to critical surfaces or exposed product. If the intervention is occurring above a critical surface, sterile tools and/or equipment must be utilized for execution of the intervention.

All interventions should be designed following appropriate aseptic techniques as recommended in EU Annex I: Manufacture of Sterile Medicinal Products, FDA Guidance for Industry: Sterile Drug Products Produced by Aseptic Processing-Current Good Manufacturing Practice, or PDA PTC-1. Intervention design must consider equipment limitations and the placement of required equipment (e.g., EM) within the RABS. The use of sterile equipment and tools (e.g., forceps) must be well thought out to not add further risks to intervention execution.

Rationale

The process described above would ensure proper design of aseptic interventions and inclusion in the QRM program through a documented risk assessment to be included in a comprehensive CCS. All interventions shall be designed to meet First Air principles and include proper mitigation if those principles are impacted due to mechanical or physical limitations. The proper design followed by risk analysis and airflow visualization will provide the appropriate understanding of the intervention to approve for use, during routine production, and for execution in a firm's APS program.

Q 9.4 RABS Glove Contact with Critical Surfaces Q 9.4 RABS

Original Text

Q 9.4. Should contact between restricted access barrier system (RABS) gloves and critical surfaces during setup or operations be permitted?

Recommendation

RABS gloves should not be permitted to contact critical surfaces during setup or during operation.

Portions of equipment would only be considered as a "critical surface" when coming into direct contact with product or components. The handle and exterior surface of the stopper bowl are not "critical surfaces" and may be contacted by RABS or operator gloves during setup.

Aseptic technique should be applied throughout the manufacturing process by trained and qualified operators. (See Question 9.7 for more information on glove installation.) Written procedures are to be established to avoid the risks of RABS gloves contacting direct or indirect surfaces and, if contact occurs, what action(s) are to be taken.

Rationale

While the CCS for RABS gloves should maintain proper aseptic conditions throughout the campaign, there is an inherent risk to the product when touching critical surfaces, as the environment and glove materials are not sterile. Proper controls (e.g., process flow, equipment design) and use of sterile tools should be in place to mitigate the need to touch critical surfaces.

Q 9.5 Post Open-Door Intervention Actions Q 9.5

Original Text

Q 9.5. What should be performed after open-door interventions?

Recommendation

RABS should be designed and operated with doors closed during aseptic processing; however, the process specificities may require identification, qualification, and approval of interventions performed with the doors open. Interventions performed with open RABS doors (e.g., manual setup) are to be minimized, and mitigation controls must be identified and implemented.

When a RABS door must be opened, those interventions need to be assessed for risk of contamination ingress. These interventions must be part of the approved list and must incorporate additional control measures based on the risk of microbiological contamination that the intervention may produce.

Such additional control measures may include, but are not limited to:

  • Additional disinfection and/or sporicidal disinfection of nonproduct-contact surfaces, including the interior of doors and glove ports exposed or touched during the activity.
    Note: The firm is responsible for documenting and defining a validated agent as part of its CCS.
  • Identification of the appropriate units that are to be manually removed (i.e., discarded) as part of the intervention.
    Note: The firm is responsible for validating all automatic unit removal performed by the filling machine.
  • Collection of sterility samples may be taken, if warranted in the CCS
  • Door access with recorded intervention alarms and an effective means of documenting the following:
    • Start and end time of intervention
    • Description of the qualified intervention
    • Participants
    • Documentation of units removed (i.e., discarded)
    • Documentation of required EM
    Note: The firm is responsible for determining the appropriate risk-based monitoring related to the open-door intervention.
  • Monitoring of operator gloves and additional risk-based locations (e.g., forearms, chest, head, etc.), immediately after completion of the open-door intervention. (See Topic 2 for additional EM recommendations.)

Rationale

Personnel are a significant source of microbiological contamination and, therefore, pose a risk to product sterility. Efforts to separate personnel from sterile products and product-contact surfaces reduce the risk of microbiological contamination. Barrier systems, such as RABS, provide a physical separation between personnel and the external environment and exposed product or product-contact surfaces, which helps prevent microbiological contamination of the sterile product by personnel and the environment.

In the case of open-door interventions, there is no longer a physical separation; therefore, additional control measures are required, as described in this section.

Q 9.6 How Should Interventions Be Performed? Q 9.6 ？

Original Text

Q 9.6. How should interventions be performed?

Recommendation

Prior to performing an intervention, all product and empty containers are to be cleared, as applicable, then the filling process is stopped. The operator(s) will then don the RABS glove in a slow and deliberate manner, which should immediately be followed by a qualified visual inspection process. The operator(s) then executes the intervention, as described in an approved procedure, following good aseptic practices and the use of a sterilized tool, where appropriate, based upon the intervention.

Upon completion of the intervention, the operator(s) returns the sterile tool, where appropriate, to its designated stand or location within the Grade A RABS. Prior to retracting the hands from the RABS glove, the operator(s) will perform a second qualified visual inspection of the glove. The operator(s) then ensures the glove is correctly placed to minimize stress and ensures the glove does not activate any light curtain, present. The filler can then be reactivated to continue the manufacturing process. When open-door RABS intervention is required, operators should work as a team, with a primary operator performing the task under Grade A environment and a secondary operator assisting the primary operator in the intervention and to ensure proper segregation.

This collaboration between primary and secondary operator(s) during Grade A open-door intervention aims to prevent any contact between the primary operator's gloves and any surfaces exposed to the surrounding environment (i.e., RABS door opening, material transfer). Intrusion of the primary operator and the duration of the intervention into the Grade A RABS should be minimized.

Rationale

Each intervention needs to be performed using a sterile tool to minimize the likelihood of the RABS glove contacting critical equipment and/or damaging the glove. Minimizing the impact to sterile components, equipment, and sterile product, along with the RABS environment, is critical when executing an intervention. Each firm needs to have a robust discard strategy, based on approved airflow visualization, to discard product and open primary packaging components that have potentially been impacted when an intervention is required. It is important to ensure the operator(s) understands and follows good aseptic practices, as they can be a significant source of contamination. The integrity of the RABS gloves is an important part of the comprehensive CCS to minimize hazard ingress; therefore, the RABS gloves need to be inspected prior to and after every use. This process must be proceduralized, and each operator is to be qualified to perform the inspection process. Each intervention needs to be performed using a sterile tool to minimize the likelihood of the RABS glove contacting critical equipment and/or damage the glove.

Q 9.7 RABS Glove Installation Methods Q 9.7 RABS

Original Text

Q 9.7. How and when should restricted access barrier system (RABS) gloves be installed?

Recommendation

RABS gloves are sterilized before installation in the RABS barrier wall (glove port), and subsequently, they must be wrapped and sterilized within an integral bag for protection against contamination through transfer within the Grade B area. Prior to installation, RABS gloves are required to be sterile. Typically, this is accomplished via autoclave or gamma-irradiation. There are multiple ways to install RABS gloves and, while this document outlines three industry approaches, other options may be considered based on the company's equipment and risk assessment.

Note: In all the approaches listed below, there should be no contact of the RABS glove to any Grade A surface with the exception of sterile tools.

Approach 1: RABS Door Closed

  1. RABS door is closed.
  2. Glove port inside is decontaminated by spraying and/or wiping with validated agents (e.g., sporicidals).
  3. Wrapped glove is introduced through the glove port; the glove remains covered.
    Note: The wrapped RABS glove does not leave the UDAF (e.g., within cart) or UDAF zone of protection using a UDAF cart, and it is transferred into the RABS under the UDAF zone of protection.
  4. Upper part of the glove bag is opened using sterile tools (outside of RABS).
  5. RABS glove is connected by the attachment of the o-ring on the outside of the RABS door.
  6. Glove wrapping is aseptically removed inside the RABS.
  7. Glove remains inverted (i.e., turned "inside-out").
  8. Glove wrapping is then taken out of the RABS by using the opening below the RABS doors for oRABS. For cRABS another method must be used.

Q 9.8 Disinfection of RABS Gloves During Operations Q 9.8 RABS

Original Text

Q 9.8. Are restricted access barrier system (RABS) gloves treated with sporicidal agents and/or otherwise disinfected during operations?

Recommendation

The use of disinfection, including sporicidal disinfection, should be defined within a CCS. In general, there is no need to conduct a sporicidal disinfection of RABS glove(s) during aseptic operations.

Note: Disinfection is only required if the RABS gloves come in contact with nonsterilized surfaces.

RABS gloves should be installed, operated, and maintained in a manner in which they will not become contaminated by exposure to the zone of protection outside the RABS (Grade B environment), by contact with personnel during door opening, or by contact with nonsterilized surfaces within the RABS.

This requires that the RABS gloves be inverted (turned "inside out") to the RABS outside when leaving the RABS gloves with the operator hands (See Figure 1.4-1 for visual example of inversion). Leaving the RABS gloves inserted and hanging down inside the RABS should be prevented as they may be contaminated by direct contact with nonsterilized surfaces.

When there are door openings as part of setup or as a qualified intervention during a filling operation, the RABS gloves should also be inverted and should not be directly exposed to the zone of protection outside the RABS (Grade B environment).

RABS glove disinfection or sporicidal disinfection is only required if the RABS glove has come in contact with potentially contaminated surfaces, which are locations below the working level, for example, for interventions where an equipment-part must be fixed. The disinfection or sporicidal disinfection should be performed by wiping; spraying disinfectants inside the Grade A RABS during filling should be prevented.

Rationale

RABS gloves are sterilized before installation and should be installed in a manner that prevents contamination. (See Question 9.7.) Proper aseptic techniques should always be used when working with RABS gloves during closed-door conditions. If the door is not opened, the RABS glove is not exposed to the Grade B environment, and there is no contact with nonsterilized surfaces. There is no requirement for an additional wiping, via disinfection or sporicidal disinfection, during operations. If a contamination risk results as described above (e.g., RABS glove exposure in Grade B, contact with non-sterilized surfaces, etc.) mitigation should follow the recommendations from Questions 9.4 and 9.5.
