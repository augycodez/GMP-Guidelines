Environmental Risk Assessment

Three-tier RA system: facility / process / EM risk assessments •

4.0 Environmental Risk Assessment

Several risk assessments need to be performed as part of an overall facility risk assessment; these can include a facility (also called an environmental) risk assessment, a process risk assessment, and an EM risk assessment. The environment (or facility) risk assessment should determine the suitability of the facility or environment in which the process is conducted. A process risk assessment should include identification of potential sources of contamination and associated inherent risks to the process from an operational perspective, including points of potential contamination. The EM risk assessment determines how the contamination risks should be addressed within an effective EM control program. All are part of the overall contamination control strategy for a facility.

This technical report focuses on the EM risk assessment that may be performed to:

  • Develop or expand programs used to identify, control, and/or monitor contamination risks to determine:
    • Appropriate level of facility control for the process being performed
    • EM locations, sampling frequency, and sampling methodology
    • Appropriate environmental controls to be employed in manufacturing areas based on the type of processing performed
    • Develop or revise area and material transfer or cleaning and/or disinfection practices
  • Determine gowning strategy and controls required to prevent contamination by personnel
  • Determine appropriate personnel, material, and waste flows
  • Develop or expand a bioburden and endotoxin-burden monitoring program

EM risk assessments can also be used to investigate a contamination event or trend, such as:

  • Microbiological contamination in a process train or critical utility system
  • Adverse data trend in a microbial measurement system (i.e., bioburden/endotoxin, EM, or critical utility system monitoring)
  • Identification and evaluation of a potential source of contamination or atypical recovery

General Guidelines

Process flow map • Multifunctional team • Facility tours •

4.1 General Guidelines

Risk assessments highlight manufacturing process steps or room locations that exhibit the greatest opportunity for introduction of potential contamination to the product or process stream, a critical utility system, or the environment. A process flow map based on production records, applicable standard operating procedures (SOPs), personnel/equipment/material flow diagrams, and piping and instrumentation diagrams is used to identify individual process steps. Using a process flow map helps ensure that each risk assessment is performed systematically and that all key aspects of the operations or systems are considered.

As a best practice, the system-owner maps out the process or systems prior to development of the risk assessment by the multifunctional team to ensure that the scope of the risk assessment is clearly identified.

Tours of the facility and manufacturing operations using the process flow map are also recommended to collect information and determine the criticality factors relevant to the risk assessment being performed (see Table 4.3.1-1🔍 and Table 4.3.1-2🔍). Only the criticality factors that apply to the scope of the risk assessment should be selected.

4.2

Criticality Factors

Eight sources of contamination • Personnel / Materials / Facility Design •

4.2 Criticality Factors

Criticality factors assist in identifying contamination hazards by determining how each factor might impact the potential for product, process, or environmental contamination. The criticality factors are based on the various sources of contamination.

Eight major potential sources of adventitious microbial contamination sources and their relevance to the risk assessment are discussed in Sections 4.2.1–4.2.3.5, topics that should be considered applicable to any facility and addressed through the risk assessment.

4.2.1 Personnel

The human body is the source of greatest risk of contamination in the manufacturing environment. The design of the EM program should consider the risk to the various environments based on the engineering and procedural controls designed to minimize the impact of personnel on the environment. Some potential controls are:

  • Establishing maximum personnel load for the room or area
  • Facility designs that eliminate the need for entrance into manufacturing areas (e.g., viewing galleries)
  • Use of facility-dedicated shoes and clothing
  • Gowning and/or personal protective equipment requirements and glove/sleeve sanitization procedures, as dictated by the specific process or process step
  • Unidirectional vs. bidirectional personnel flows
  • Use of good aseptic working practices
  • Personnel training and qualification

4.2.2 Materials/Waste

The second major source of potential contamination is the transfer of materials into and waste out of the manufacturing and support areas and, potentially, into exposed product. All ingredients, processing aids, filters, components, labels, packaging materials, etc., must be disinfected (or sterilized if determined appropriate) and managed in a manner that reduces the risk of transferring contamination to the manufacturing and support areas. Waste should be managed in a manner that ensures a low risk of microbial and/or product cross-contamination. A comprehensive EM program must include sampling locations and frequencies to provide timely indications that materials and waste are being transferred and handled appropriately.

4.2.3 Facility Design and Construction

The design of the manufacturing and support areas and the implementation of the following minimum requirements are needed to facilitate cleaning and disinfection activities, as well as minimize the potential for contamination of those areas and the product (this list is not exhaustive):

  • All surfaces should be smooth, impervious, nonabsorbent, unbroken, and compatible with appropriate agents
  • The amount of exposed surface area should be minimized to the extent possible (e.g., pipes should be contained in interstitial spaces wherever possible)
  • Construction materials should resist chipping, flaking, oxidizing, or other deterioration
  • Wall and floor junctions should be curved, rather than meeting at sharp angles
  • All flooring materials should be seamless
  • Any recesses should be reachable and cleanable
  • Ledges, shelves, cupboards, and equipment where particles or microbes can settle and accumulate should be minimized
The facility design and construction should be appropriate for the proposed manufacturing process and product. If during the execution of the risk assessment, the facility is found to be inappropriate in any way, the deficiency should be addressed through appropriate mitigation and the efficacy verified through the EM program.

4.2.3.1 Facility Cleaning and Disinfection

The types of cleaning and disinfection agents (i.e., sanitizers, disinfectants, and sporicides), methodology, and frequency should be considered when designing an EM program, and the frequency of cleaning and disinfection activities should be justified based on historical EM data for existing facilities. For new facilities, this frequency should be determined through appropriate in-situ qualification as defined in PDA Technical Report No. 70: Fundamentals of Cleaning and Disinfection Programs for Aseptic Manufacturing Facilities.

4.2.3.2 Process Design

The design of the process should be considered when establishing the EM program. The degree of manual manipulations, number and complexity of required steps, and the process duration all affect the potential for microbial contamination to enter the system. Therefore, each step of the process should be considered individually in the risk assessment. For example, open operations may provide more opportunity for product contamination, whereas closed or hard-piped systems and isolators can significantly reduce the risk of contamination as they prevent exposure of the process stream to the environment and personnel. From an operational standpoint, additional bioburden reduction steps, such as filtration, should be considered.

4.2.3.3 Nature of the Product

The nature of in-process intermediates and final product should also be considered, such as the potential for growth-promoting characteristics. For example, upstream cell culture media would be highly sensitive to microbial proliferation whereas potential downstream purification materials with high salt buffers may prohibit microbial proliferation.

4.2.3.4 Environmental Air

Controlling air as a source of contamination is an important factor to consider when designing an EM program. Points to consider include, but are not limited to:

  • Heating, ventilation, and air conditioning design
  • Number of air changes per hour
  • Use of localized filtered air (e.g., UDFs)
  • Use of pressurized airflow cascades
  • Use of transitional spaces or rooms (e.g., airlocks and gowning rooms)
  • Results of airflow visualization studies (e.g., smoke studies)

4.2.3.5 Utilities

The main types of utilities used in controlled environments are water, compressed air or gases, vacuums, and drains. Risk of potential contamination from exposure to these utilities should be considered when determining sampling locations and frequencies for an EM program. How these utilities impact the process and product should also be considered, as they impact the quality and design of the resulting EM program.

4.3 Risk Assessment Design

Many methods are available for designing and implementing a risk assessment. The selection of the method should be based on the process in question and the relevant criticality factors selected. For the purposes of illustration, Sections 4.4 and 4.5 present examples of hypothetical risk assessment designs for a typical mammalian cell culture process (upstream).

Each risk-ranking is performed in accordance with the risk tool selected. The risk control activities are performed according to the criteria specified in the applicable risk tool methodology. If a location or process step is not well controlled and/or monitored and considered a high risk, then that location or process step automatically becomes a potential source of microbial ingress, and tightening contamination controls should be considered.

4.3.1 Criticality Factors

Criticality factors are criteria that are divided into three main groups: process step, potential contributors to contamination, and unusual/unexpected sources of contamination. Tables 4.3.1-1 and 4.3.1-2 provide examples of elements that aid in determining the level of risk—High, Medium, or Low—and the criticality factors of each. These elements may change based on the manufacturing operations, equipment, facility design, or other components. The criteria may be numbers or values but need to be able to allow the user to evaluate the risk on a scaled basis. The criticality and weighting for each criterion may vary depending on the area and process or other factors.

4.3.1.1 Group 1 – Process Step

Each stage of the manufacturing process is considered a process step. The process steps shown in Tables 4.3.1-1 and 4.3.1-2 are examples of those that would be used by the risk assessment team. These may differ based on the products being manufactured, manufacturing process, stage of the process, equipment set-up, facility age, and controls, among other elements.

4.3.1.2 Group 2 – Potential Contributors to Contamination

These criticality factors include the manufacturing process, environmental factors, facility or equipment design, and contamination control programs implemented, any of which may contribute to the potential for contamination. For the factors in this group, enhanced contamination controls may be identified during risk mitigation. In this mammalian cell culture example, the potential sources of contamination are shown in Tables 4.3.1-1 and 4.3.1-2.

4.3.1.3 Group 3 – Unusual/Unexpected Sources of Contamination

Group 3 evaluates sources of contamination that are extraneous to the design intent of the environment, equipment, or process. Any unusual or unexpected source of contamination is considered a high risk that must be mitigated. This may include faulty or leaking equipment or utility connections or breaches in the integrity of the manufacturing environment that are detected during a facility walk-through. Since the source of this hazard is unexpected and must be mitigated, controls should be implemented, and it should be monitored until the unexpected source of contamination has been eliminated. Examples include, but are not limited to, a reverse in pressure differentials due to a power failure, presence of contractors and their equipment, damage to the facility surfaces, or condensation on piping.

4.3.4 Conclusion

EM is an important element in bioburden control strategies for environments used for manufacturing processes that require bioburden control. Data derived from the environment and the process provide assurance that processes are being exposed to consistent and suitable environments commensurate with process requirements. Current guidance recommends using a risk-based approach to develop an EM program suitable to support manufacturing steps. The assessment of the critical factors identified in this technical report and presented methodology for executing a structured and robust risk assessment provide a scientifically sound approach to developing a risk-based EM program.
