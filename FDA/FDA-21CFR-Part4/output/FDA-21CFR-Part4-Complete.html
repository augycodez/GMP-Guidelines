0.1

What Are Combination Products? — ？

Statutory definition, four product types, and real-world examples

Regulatory Text & Summary (English)

Statutory Definition

Under FD&C Act §3(g) (21 U.S.C. §353(g)), the term "combination product" includes:

  • A product comprising any combination of a drug and a device
  • A product comprising a combination of a biological product and a device
  • A product comprising a combination of a drug and a biological product
  • A product comprising a combination of a drug, device, and a biological product

The product must have two or more regulated article types working together to achieve the product's intended use. The combination may be physical, chemical, or purely functional (cross-labeling).

Four Types of Combination Products

  1. Single-Entity Combination Product — Two or more regulated components physically, chemically, or otherwise combined or mixed and produced as a single entity. The components cannot be separated without affecting the product's function.
  2. Co-Packaged Combination Product — Two or more separate products packaged together in a single container or as a unit, where each component retains its own regulatory identity but is intended to be used together.
  3. Cross-Labeled Combination Product — Two or more separate products manufactured separately, distributed separately, but labeled for use together. The labeling of one product references the other as required for safe and effective use.
  4. Investigational Combination Product — Any of the above types when used under an Investigational New Drug (IND) application or Investigational Device Exemption (IDE). Regulatory requirements apply even in the investigational stage.

0.2

Why Part 4 Was Created — Part 4？

Historical context, regulatory gaps, and the creation of OCP

Regulatory Text & Summary (English)

The Problem: Three Centers, One Product

Before Part 4, FDA had three separate centers with distinct regulatory standards, review timelines, and post-market requirements:

  • CDER (Center for Drug Evaluation and Research) — governed drugs under 21 CFR 210/211
  • CDRH (Center for Devices and Radiological Health) — governed medical devices under 21 CFR 820 and the Quality System Regulation
  • CBER (Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research) — governed biologics under 21 CFR 600–680

Combination products fell into regulatory gaps where no center had clear jurisdiction, or they faced duplicative review by multiple centers. This created:

  • Prolonged and unpredictable review timelines
  • Inconsistent CGMP requirements across similar products
  • Manufacturer confusion about which submission pathway to use (NDA, BLA, PMA, 510(k))
  • Post-market surveillance and adverse event reporting ambiguities

Legislative Solution

The Safe Medical Devices Act of 1990 created §3(g) of the FD&C Act, which required FDA to:

  • Designate a lead center (primary regulatory jurisdiction) for each combination product
  • Base the designation on the product's primary mode of action (PMOA)
  • Ensure consistency and predictability in the designation process

21 CFR Part 4 operationalizes §3(g) by establishing the regulatory procedures for designation, including the formal Request for Designation (RFD) process.

Office of Combination Products (OCP)

The Medical Device User Fee and Modernization Act (MDUFMA) of 2002 established the Office of Combination Products (OCP) within the FDA Commissioner's Office. OCP:

  • Manages the RFD process and issues binding jurisdictional determinations
  • Resolves inter-center disputes about jurisdiction
  • Develops and updates guidance on combination product regulation
  • Monitors combination product reviews to ensure timely, consistent decisions
  • Reports annually to Congress on combination product activities
"The purpose of Part 4 is to achieve timely and effective premarket review of combination products and to ensure consistent and appropriate post-market regulation of these products." — 21 CFR Part 4, Preamble

Why Part 4 Matters for CDMOs — Part 4 CDMO

CGMP overlap, quality agreements, and the mAb autoinjector scenario

Regulatory Text & Summary (English)

Four Critical Questions for CDMO Operations

A CDMO manufacturing a sterile injectable drug for a combination product must answer four questions before beginning manufacturing:

  1. Which FDA center leads regulation? — Determines which submission type applies (NDA vs. PMA vs. BLA), which division within FDA reviews the product, and which reviewers have authority over manufacturing-related questions.
  2. Which CGMP applies — or does dual CGMP apply? — Under 21 CFR Part 4 Subpart B (the 2013 CGMP rule), manufacturers of combination products may be subject to both 21 CFR Part 211 (drug CGMP) and 21 CFR Part 820 (Quality System Regulation for devices). The CDMO must know which applies to their specific operations.
  3. How do quality agreements allocate compliance responsibility? — The sponsor (drug/device brand owner) and CDMO must explicitly allocate who is responsible for CGMP compliance for each constituent part. Ambiguity in quality agreements is a direct FDA 483 observation risk.
  4. When do manufacturing changes require prior approval vs. notification? — A change to the drug formulation may require a Prior Approval Supplement (PAS), while the same change to device labeling may only require an Annual Report. The CDMO must follow the change control requirements of the lead center.

CDMO Scenario: mAb Autoinjector Sterile Fill-Finish

A CDMO performs sterile fill-finish for a monoclonal antibody (mAb) in a prefilled autoinjector pen. The product is a single-entity combination product: biologic drug (mAb) + device (autoinjector mechanism + prefilled syringe).

Common CDMO Trap

Assuming only drug CGMP (21 CFR 211) applies when manufacturing the drug constituent for a combination product. Under 21 CFR Part 4 Subpart B, if the CDMO also assembles or tests device constituent parts, the quality system must also satisfy relevant provisions of 21 CFR 820. This is frequently flagged in FDA inspections of CDMOs filling combination product presentations.

Relationship to Other Regulations —

Cross-reference map: Part 4 → Part 3 → CGMP regulations; what Part 4 does NOT govern

Regulatory Text & Summary (English)

Cross-Reference Map

21 CFR Part 4 does not operate in isolation. It is part of a regulatory framework that must be read in conjunction with several other parts of the CFR:

What Part 4 Does NOT Govern

Part 4 is frequently misunderstood as covering the entire regulatory lifecycle of combination products. It does not:

  • Govern approval pathways — NDA (21 CFR Part 314), BLA (21 CFR Part 601), PMA (21 CFR Part 814), and 510(k) (21 CFR Part 807) are governed by their respective regulations. Part 4 only determines which center manages those pathways.
  • Establish submission formats — The Common Technical Document (CTD) format and electronic submission requirements are set by the lead center, not Part 4.
  • Define safety standards — Biocompatibility (ISO 10993), sterility, container-closure integrity, and other technical standards are governed by USP, ISO, and center-specific guidance, not Part 4.
  • Override other CFR requirements — Part 4 operates alongside existing requirements; it does not supersede 21 CFR 211, 820, or 600.

21 CFR Part 4 Subpart B (2013 CGMP Rule)

The most operationally significant addition to Part 4 was the 2013 final rule establishing Subpart B: Current Good Manufacturing Practice Requirements for Combination Products. Key provisions:

  • Manufacturers must comply with the CGMP requirements applicable to each constituent part
  • A manufacturer complying with one set of CGMP requirements (e.g., 21 CFR 820) may demonstrate compliance with certain overlapping requirements from another set (e.g., 21 CFR 211) using its own CGMP system
  • The rule provides a streamlined compliance pathway to reduce dual-CGMP burden while maintaining safety and quality
  • Applies to all combination products subject to FDA premarket review

Section Summary —

Key takeaways from Section 0

Key Takeaways (English)

What You Should Know After This Section

1. Definition and Types

A combination product under FD&C Act §3(g) is any product combining two or more of the following: drug, device, biological product. There are four operational types: single-entity, co-packaged, cross-labeled, and investigational.

2. Why Part 4 Exists

Part 4 was created to resolve the pre-1990 problem of combination products falling between FDA's three separate centers (CDER, CDRH, CBER). The 1990 Safe Medical Devices Act mandated lead center designation based on primary mode of action. OCP (est. 2002) enforces this through the RFD process.

3. CGMP Complexity for Manufacturers

Under Part 4 Subpart B (2013), combination product manufacturers may need to comply with both 21 CFR 211 and 21 CFR 820. The streamlined compliance pathway allows a single QMS to satisfy both if equivalence is documented. CDMOs must never assume only drug CGMP applies.

4. What Part 4 Does NOT Do

Part 4 designates jurisdiction — it does not set approval pathways (NDA/BLA/PMA), technical standards, or override other CFR requirements. It is a jurisdictional and procedural rule, not a manufacturing or submission standard.

5. CDMO Practical Implication

Before accepting any combination product contract, a CDMO must confirm: lead center designation, applicable CGMP regulations, quality agreement allocation of responsibilities, and change control routing for device constituent parts.

1.1 §4.1 — What Does This Part Cover?（Part 4 ）

Original Text

§4.1 — Scope.

(a) This subpart applies to combination products as defined in §3.2(e) of this chapter. It establishes, for purposes of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act and the Public Health Service Act, which current good manufacturing practice (CGMP) requirements in parts 4, 210, 211, 600 through 680, and 820 of this chapter apply to combination products, and provides for the assignment of combination products to a component of FDA.

(b) This subpart does not cover biological products that are regulated solely under the Public Health Service Act and that do not have a drug or device constituent part.

(c) Agreements and decisions made under this part regarding which CGMP requirements apply to a specific combination product are intended to promote the efficient and timely review of applications and the timely compliance of manufacturers with applicable CGMP requirements.

1.2 §4.2 — Key Definitions（）

Original Text

§4.2 — Definitions.

The following definitions apply to this subpart:

Combination product means a product comprised of any combination of a drug and a device; a biological product and a device; a drug and a biological product; or a drug, a device, and a biological product. A combination product includes: (1) A product comprised of two or more regulated components that are physically, chemically, or otherwise combined or mixed and produced as a single entity (single-entity combination product); (2) two or more separate products packaged together in a single package or as a unit and comprised of drug and device products, device and biological products, or biological and drug products (co-packaged combination product); (3) a drug, device, or biological product packaged separately that according to its investigational plan or proposed labeling is intended for use only with an approved individually specified drug, device, or biological product where both are required to achieve the intended use, indication, or effect and where upon approval of the proposed product the labeling of the approved product would need to be changed, e.g., to reflect a change in intended use, dosage form, strength, route of administration, or significant change in dose (cross-labeled combination product); (4) any investigational drug, device, or biological product packaged separately that according to its proposed labeling is for use only with another individually specified investigational drug, device, or biological product where both are required to achieve the intended use, indication, or effect. (See §3.2(e) of this chapter.)

Constituent part means a drug, device, or biological product that is part of a combination product.

Primary mode of action (PMOA) means the single mode of action of a combination product that provides the most important therapeutic action of the combination product. The most important therapeutic action is the mode of action expected to make the greatest contribution to the overall intended therapeutic effects of the combination product.

Lead center (also referred to as designated center) means the center within FDA that has primary jurisdiction for the premarket review and regulation of a combination product, as determined under §3.4 of this chapter.

Requesting party means any person who submits a request for designation (RFD) under §3.7 of this chapter or an agreement/determination request under this part.

Office of Combination Products (OCP) means the office within the Office of the Commissioner that administers the combination product designation provisions of section 503(g) of the act.

1.3 §4.3 — Which CGMP Regulations Apply?（ CGMP ）

Original Text

§4.3 — Applicable CGMP requirements.

(a) Combination products with a drug constituent part. The CGMP requirements of parts 210 and 211 of this chapter apply to combination products that include a drug constituent part.

(b) Combination products with a device constituent part. The CGMP requirements of part 820 of this chapter (quality system regulation) apply to combination products that include a device constituent part.

(c) Combination products with a biological product constituent part. The CGMP requirements of parts 600 through 680 of this chapter apply to combination products that include a biological product constituent part.

(d) Combination products with an HCT/P constituent part. The requirements of part 1271 of this chapter apply to combination products that include a human cell, tissue, or cellular or tissue-based product (HCT/P) constituent part.

(e) Single-entity and co-packaged combination products. For single-entity or co-packaged combination products, manufacturers must comply with the CGMP requirements applicable to each constituent part. The combination product is subject to all of those requirements unless a streamlined approach is agreed upon under §4.4.

Note on Streamlined Compliance (§4.4): A manufacturer of a single-entity or co-packaged combination product containing a device and either a drug or biological product may petition the lead center to demonstrate compliance using the primary constituent part's CGMP framework, provided the manufacturer can show that the other applicable CGMP requirements are also satisfied through that framework.

"For single-entity or co-packaged combination products, manufacturers must comply with the CGMP requirements applicable to each constituent part."
— 21 CFR §4.3(e)

Cross-labeled combination products: Cross-labeled combination products are subject to the CGMP requirements applicable to their individual constituent parts as regulated independently. Each constituent part must comply with its own applicable CGMP regime.

2.1 §4.5 + §3.4 — How Do You Know Which Center Regulates?（）

Original Text

§4.5 — Agreement, Determination, and Designation Procedures.

Under 21 CFR §3.4 and §4.5, a manufacturer or sponsor seeking to determine which FDA center has primary jurisdiction over a combination product has three procedural options:

Option 1 — Informal OCP Consultation (Non-binding)
A sponsor may contact OCP informally to discuss product classification questions. OCP staff will provide informal guidance based on precedent products and existing regulatory determinations. This path is fastest and costs no application time, but the response is not a formal designation and does not bind FDA.

Option 2 — Pre-RFD Meeting
Before filing a formal RFD, a sponsor may request a pre-RFD meeting with OCP to discuss the product's constituent parts, proposed PMOA, and likely center assignment. This meeting clarifies whether a formal RFD is warranted and helps the sponsor prepare a stronger submission. Pre-RFD meetings are especially useful when the PMOA is scientifically ambiguous.

Option 3 — Formal Request for Designation (RFD)
A sponsor files a written RFD with OCP under §3.7. FDA must respond within 60 calendar days. The designation issued is binding on FDA and establishes the lead center for the product's lifecycle. Filing an RFD is strongly recommended before submitting the first marketing application (IND, NDA, BLA, or PMA).

When to file formal RFD vs. proceed directly:

  • File formal RFD when: PMOA is unclear; no clear precedent product exists; the product spans two or more center jurisdictions; sponsor needs binding confirmation for regulatory strategy
  • Proceed directly to lead center when: prior OCP informal guidance is consistent; the product is nearly identical to a previously designated combination product; the primary constituent part classification is unambiguous

Original Text

§3.7 — Request for Designation.

(a) Any person may submit a request for designation (RFD) to the Office of Combination Products (OCP) to obtain a formal designation of the center that will have primary jurisdiction over a combination product.

When to File: An RFD should be filed early in product development — ideally before initiation of the first clinical study or before submission of the first marketing application. All FDA centers must honor the designation. If the sponsor later seeks to change the designation, it must demonstrate a substantial change in the product's intended use, constituent parts, or mode of action.

2.3 §3.7(a) — Required RFD Content（RFD ）

Original Text

§3.7(a) — Content of an RFD.

A complete RFD must contain the following seven elements. Failure to include any element may result in a Refuse to Review action, restarting the 60-day clock.

Element 1 — Product Description and Constituent Parts: A complete description of the combination product, including identification of each constituent part (drug, device, biological product), how they are physically or functionally combined, and the overall product configuration.

Element 2 — Regulatory Status of Each Constituent: For each constituent part, a statement of its current regulatory status — whether it is currently approved/cleared, under investigation, or not yet regulated — and any existing FDA regulatory history.

Element 3 — Proposed PMOA with Scientific Rationale: The sponsor's proposed primary mode of action, supported by a detailed scientific rationale explaining why the proposed PMOA provides the most important therapeutic action. This is the most technically demanding element.

Element 4 — Intended Use, Patient Population, Route of Administration: The intended clinical indication, target patient population, and route of administration (e.g., intravascular, subcutaneous, topical), as this directly affects which center has most relevant expertise.

Element 5 — Manufacturing Process Description: A description of the manufacturing process sufficient to understand how the constituent parts are combined, the order of manufacturing steps, and any relevant in-process controls. Not required to be a full batch record, but must be substantive enough to assess process complexity.

Element 6 — Packaging Description (for co-packaged products): If the combination product is co-packaged, a description of the packaging configuration, how the drug and device components are arranged, and how they are intended to be used together by the end user.

Element 7 — Prior FDA Interactions: A summary of any prior communications with FDA regarding this product, including meeting minutes, prior submissions, informal feedback from any FDA center, and any written FDA correspondence relevant to product classification or regulatory status.

2.4 §3.7(b) + §4.3 — PMOA Determination（）

Original Text

§3.7(b) — Primary Mode of Action (PMOA) Determination.

The primary mode of action (PMOA) is the single mode of action of a combination product that provides the most important therapeutic action of the combination product. The most important therapeutic action is the mode of action expected to make the greatest contribution to the overall intended therapeutic effects of the combination product. (21 CFR §3.2(m))

Five Mode of Action Categories:

  • Pharmacological — the product achieves its effect through chemical interaction with biological receptors or biochemical pathways → leads to CDER jurisdiction
  • Immunological — the product achieves its effect through stimulation, suppression, or modulation of the immune system → leads to CBER jurisdiction
  • Metabolic — the product achieves its effect by altering metabolic processes (e.g., gene therapy affecting cellular metabolism) → typically leads to CBER
  • Mechanical — the product achieves its effect through physical force, structural support, or mechanical function (e.g., stents, scaffolds, delivery devices) → leads to CDRH
  • Physical — the product achieves its effect through physical means other than mechanical force (e.g., thermal, electrical, photochemical) → leads to CDRH or CDER depending on specifics

3-Step Analytical Framework:

  1. Step 1 — List all MOAs: Enumerate every mode of action present in the combination product. Be comprehensive; include both primary and secondary effects of each constituent part.
  2. Step 2 — Determine which MOA provides the primary therapeutic effect: Which single MOA makes the greatest contribution to achieving the intended clinical outcome? Consider clinical evidence, mechanism studies, and the product's labeling claims.
  3. Step 3 — Apply tie-breaker if MOA is unclear (§3.8/§4.8(b)): If two MOAs are scientifically indistinguishable in their contribution to therapeutic effect, OCP applies a tie-breaker: (a) assign to the center with the most expertise in the predominant safety/effectiveness questions raised by the combination product; (b) if still tied, assign to the center with the most relevant regulatory experience with the most similar products.

2.5 §4.9 / §3.9 — Center Assignment & Consequences（ CGMP ）

Original Text

§3.9 / §4.9 — Designation of Center with Primary Jurisdiction.

Lead Center (Designated Center): The center identified in the RFD designation has primary jurisdiction over the combination product. The lead center's responsibilities include:

  • Primary reviewer of the first marketing application (IND/NDA for CDER; PMA/510(k) for CDRH; BLA for CBER)
  • Primary point of contact for all regulatory correspondence
  • Coordinates with consulting center(s) on cross-jurisdictional issues
  • Issues final decisions on approval, labeling, and post-market obligations

Consulting Center: The center(s) not designated as lead retain authority to review aspects of the combination product within their area of expertise. The consulting center:

  • Reviews its specific constituent part (e.g., CDRH reviews device constituent even if CDER leads)
  • Provides binding comments to lead center on safety/effectiveness questions within its expertise
  • May request additional data specific to its regulatory area
  • Does not issue independent approvals — all decisions flow through lead center

CGMP Trigger — Direct Link to §4.3: The center assignment directly determines which CGMP obligations apply:

  • CDER leads → 21 CFR Parts 210/211 are the primary CGMP; 21 CFR Part 820 (QMSR) applies to device constituent
  • CDRH leads → 21 CFR Part 820 (QMSR) is primary; 21 CFR Parts 210/211 apply to drug constituent
  • CBER leads → 21 CFR Parts 600–680 plus either 210/211 or 820 depending on constituent parts

Manufacturers may seek a streamlined CGMP approach under §4.4, but the center assignment established by RFD always sets the baseline obligations.

3.1 §4.4 — Combination Product CGMP Requirements: The Streamlined Approach 3.1 §4.4 — CGMP ：

Regulatory Framework

§ 4.4 Current good manufacturing practice requirements.
21 CFR Part 4 Subpart B establishes CGMP requirements for combination products. The 2013 Final Rule (effective December 22, 2013) clarified how manufacturers demonstrate compliance when a combination product includes both drug and device constituents.

§4.4(a) — Default Dual Compliance Rule

The default rule is straightforward: a combination product must comply with the CGMP regulations applicable to each constituent part.

  • If the combination product includes a drug constituent part → must comply with 21 CFR Parts 210 and 211 (drug CGMP)
  • If the combination product includes a device constituent part → must comply with 21 CFR Part 820 (Quality System Regulation / QSR)
  • If the combination product includes a biological product constituent part → must also comply with 21 CFR Parts 600–680
  • If a combination product includes all three types → all three sets of CGMP requirements apply

§4.4(b) — The Streamlined Approach

The streamlined approach is the practical pathway for most manufacturers. Under §4.4(b), a manufacturer may comply with only one set of CGMP regulations — either Part 211 or Part 820 as the primary framework — provided the manufacturer demonstrates that the controls established under the primary CGMP framework satisfy the requirements of the other applicable CGMP regulation.

Key operational requirement: The manufacturer must document how each applicable requirement from the non-primary CGMP set is satisfied by the primary system. This documentation is the gap analysis crosswalk — FDA will review it during inspection.

§4.4(b) — What "Streamlined" Does NOT Mean

Critically, "streamlined" does not mean the manufacturer can ignore requirements that exist only in one regulation and not the other. If a requirement is unique to one regulation with no functional equivalent in the other, that unique requirement must still be implemented. The streamlined approach only allows avoiding literal duplication of procedures when the same outcome is already achieved through an existing system.

"A person who manufactures or processes a combination product may satisfy the current good manufacturing practice requirements applicable to the combination product by complying with either the drug current good manufacturing practice regulations in parts 210 and 211 of this chapter or the device quality system regulation in part 820 of this chapter, provided the person demonstrates that compliance with that regulation satisfies the requirements of the other applicable regulation(s)."
— 21 CFR §4.4(b), the streamlined compliance option

2013 Final Rule — FDA's Operational Guidance

The 2013 Final Rule clarified several key implementation questions:

  • FDA confirmed that the lead center's CGMP framework should typically serve as the primary CGMP system
  • The crosswalk documentation does not need to be a separate standalone document — it may be embedded in SOPs, quality agreements, or the Quality Manual
  • FDA's "Guidance for Industry and FDA Staff: Current Good Manufacturing Practice Requirements for Combination Products" (January 2017) provides detailed guidance on implementing the streamlined approach
  • FDA confirmed that both CDRH and CDER/CBER may conduct inspections of a single site manufacturing combination products

3.3 Post-Market Reporting Obligations — Dual Reporting Pathways 3.3 —

Post-Market Regulatory Framework

Adverse Event Reporting: Which System?

Post-market reporting for combination products follows the lead center assignment, but manufacturers must understand when both reporting systems apply simultaneously.

Drug-Primary Combination Products (CDER/CBER lead):

  • MedWatch / FAERS reporting under 21 CFR §310.305 (OTC drugs) and §314.81 (NDA holders) for adverse drug experiences (ADE)
  • 15-day expedited reports for serious unexpected adverse events; periodic reports for expected events
  • NDA holder bears primary reporting responsibility; CDMO must promptly notify NDA holder of any complaint or adverse event received

Device-Primary Combination Products (CDRH lead):

  • Medical Device Reporting (MDR) under 21 CFR Part 803 — mandatory reporting for device malfunctions, serious injuries, or deaths
  • 30-day reports for most MDR events; 5-day reports for events requiring remedial action to prevent unreasonable risk to public health
  • Device manufacturer (which may include the CDMO) is independently responsible for MDR reporting regardless of NDA/BLA holder reporting

When BOTH Systems Apply:

For certain combination products where the drug AND device aspects each contribute to an adverse event, reporting under BOTH MedWatch/FAERS and MDR Part 803 may be required. FDA's 2019 guidance "Submission of Quality Metrics Data" and the combination products post-market guidance address this dual reporting scenario.

Example: An autoinjector (drug-primary) fails to deliver the correct dose due to a device malfunction that also caused an injection site reaction classified as an adverse drug experience. The NDA holder must file an ADE report; the device manufacturer must evaluate MDR reportability independently.

Recalls

Recall procedures under 21 CFR Part 7 apply regardless of product type. The lead center coordinates the recall, but the recall strategy must address both the drug and device aspects of the combination product. Both CDER (or CBER) and CDRH may be involved in recall classification and strategy approval for combination products.

Post-Market Studies

Lead center requirements drive post-market study obligations:

  • Drug-primary combo: Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy (REMS) may be required; post-marketing commitments under 21 CFR §314.81(b)(2)(vii) for NDA holders
  • Device-primary combo: Post-approval study (PAS) conditions may be imposed; post-market surveillance study under §522 of the FD&C Act for certain class II/III devices

Labeling Changes

Labeling changes follow the lead center's submission pathway: NDA supplement (Prior Approval Supplement or Changes Being Effected) for drug-primary; PMA supplement or 510(k) for device-primary. Changes affecting the device constituent of a drug-primary combo product may require CDRH consultation even if filed through CDER.

3.4 CDMO Responsibilities in Combination Product Manufacturing 3.4 CDMO

CDMO Regulatory Scope

Scenario 1: CDMO as Drug Constituent Manufacturer

When a CDMO manufactures only the drug constituent part (e.g., API, drug formulation, or filled drug vials that will later be assembled with a device by another party):

  • 21 CFR Parts 210/211 apply to the drug manufacturing operations
  • If the CDMO's operations also involve contact with the device constituent (e.g., filling drug into a prefilled syringe barrel that is also a primary container/device component) → Part 820 requirements also apply to those operations
  • The CDMO is responsible for its portion of the BPR/DHR

Scenario 2: CDMO as Device Constituent Manufacturer

CDMOs providing contract sterilization, contract device assembly, or device testing services:

  • 21 CFR Part 820 applies to device manufacturing operations
  • If the device constituent has drug contact (e.g., drug-eluting stent assembly, drug-coated device), Part 211 also applies
  • Contract sterilization specifically: §820.75 process validation requirements for sterilization, plus Part 211 sterilization records if drug product is sterilized

Scenario 3: Split-Site Manufacturing

The most complex scenario for combination products involves multiple manufacturing sites:

  • Site A (Drug Constituent): Drug formulation and fill-finish → Part 211 applies
  • Site B (Device Constituent): Device component fabrication/sterilization → Part 820 applies
  • Site C (Final Assembly/Labeling): Assembly of drug + device constituent → BOTH Part 211 and Part 820 apply; this site bears dual CGMP compliance responsibility for the integrated product

The NDA/PMA/510(k) holder must ensure all three sites are in compliance; the registration and listing obligations under 21 CFR Part 807 and Part 207 must be fulfilled by each site.

Quality Agreement — Essential Content for Combination Products

Quality agreements for combination product manufacturing must address the following elements that are unique to the combination product context:

  1. Constituent part scope: Which constituent parts does each party manufacture? What are the exact physical boundaries of each party's manufacturing responsibility?
  2. Record ownership: Who maintains which portions of the BPR/DHR? How are records consolidated before batch release?
  3. Lead center interface: Which party holds the regulatory submissions? Who is the primary contact with CDER vs. CDRH?
  4. Adverse event reporting: Who reports to which center? Within what timeframe must CDMO notify the client?
  5. Change management authority: Which party holds approval authority for manufacturing changes? Which changes require prior FDA notification (and to which center)?
  6. CGMP compliance scope: Which set(s) of CGMP regulations does the CDMO implement for this product? Who maintains the crosswalk documentation?
  7. Inspection support: If FDA inspects the CDMO related to a combination product, who provides regulatory support? How are inspection findings communicated to the client?
  8. Right to audit: Frequency and scope of client audits of CDMO's combination product manufacturing operations

3.5 FDA Inspection Implications for Combination Products 3.5 FDA

Inspection Framework

Lead Center Coordination

For combination product manufacturing sites, the lead center assigned to the combination product typically coordinates inspection scheduling. However, the FDA's Office of Combination Products (OCP) facilitates cross-center inspections when needed.

Dual-Center Inspections

Both CDER and CDRH may inspect a single combination product manufacturing site:

  • CDER investigators focus on drug CGMP compliance (Part 211): batch records, laboratory controls, process validation, complaint handling (ADE focus)
  • CDRH investigators focus on device QSR compliance (Part 820): design controls, DHR, §820.100 CAPA, MDR procedures
  • Both center investigators may be present simultaneously, or may conduct separate inspections
  • FDA expects the manufacturer to demonstrate compliance with both sets of requirements regardless of which investigator is present

OAI — Official Action Indicated

A critical consequence unique to combination product sites: an Official Action Indicated (OAI) classification by either center can block product approval or trigger enforcement action for the combination product as a whole.

  • OAI from CDER → may result in application hold or Warning Letter citing Part 211 deficiencies → blocks drug approval pathway
  • OAI from CDRH → may result in PMA hold or 510(k) refuse-to-accept → blocks device approval pathway
  • For the combination product: either OAI alone may be sufficient to prevent market authorization

Inspection Focus — What FDA Checks for Combination Products

Based on FDA Warning Letters and 483 observations specific to combination products, inspectors focus on:

  1. Is dual CGMP compliance documented? The crosswalk between Part 211 and Part 820 — does it exist? Is it current? Does it address actual operations?
  2. Are complaint handling procedures dual-capable? Does the SOP include both ADE and MDR evaluation steps?
  3. Is CAPA implemented per §820.100? Even for drug-primary combo products, FDA expects a formal CAPA system.
  4. Are DHR and BPR properly integrated? Can the investigator trace a specific unit of combination product through both sets of records?
  5. Are all manufacturing sites registered? Site A, B, and C must each comply with applicable registration/listing requirements.
  6. Are design controls applied? For device constituents: §820.30 design history file (DHF) must be maintained even if the device constituent is purchased.
