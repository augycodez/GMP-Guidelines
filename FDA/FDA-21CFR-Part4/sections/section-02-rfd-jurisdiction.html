2.1 §4.5 + §3.4 — How Do You Know Which Center Regulates?（）

Original Text

§4.5 — Agreement, Determination, and Designation Procedures.

Under 21 CFR §3.4 and §4.5, a manufacturer or sponsor seeking to determine which FDA center has primary jurisdiction over a combination product has three procedural options:

Option 1 — Informal OCP Consultation (Non-binding)
A sponsor may contact OCP informally to discuss product classification questions. OCP staff will provide informal guidance based on precedent products and existing regulatory determinations. This path is fastest and costs no application time, but the response is not a formal designation and does not bind FDA.

Option 2 — Pre-RFD Meeting
Before filing a formal RFD, a sponsor may request a pre-RFD meeting with OCP to discuss the product's constituent parts, proposed PMOA, and likely center assignment. This meeting clarifies whether a formal RFD is warranted and helps the sponsor prepare a stronger submission. Pre-RFD meetings are especially useful when the PMOA is scientifically ambiguous.

Option 3 — Formal Request for Designation (RFD)
A sponsor files a written RFD with OCP under §3.7. FDA must respond within 60 calendar days. The designation issued is binding on FDA and establishes the lead center for the product's lifecycle. Filing an RFD is strongly recommended before submitting the first marketing application (IND, NDA, BLA, or PMA).

When to file formal RFD vs. proceed directly:

  • File formal RFD when: PMOA is unclear; no clear precedent product exists; the product spans two or more center jurisdictions; sponsor needs binding confirmation for regulatory strategy
  • Proceed directly to lead center when: prior OCP informal guidance is consistent; the product is nearly identical to a previously designated combination product; the primary constituent part classification is unambiguous

2.2 §3.7 — Request for Designation (RFD)（）

Original Text

§3.7 — Request for Designation.

(a) Any person may submit a request for designation (RFD) to the Office of Combination Products (OCP) to obtain a formal designation of the center that will have primary jurisdiction over a combination product.

When to File: An RFD should be filed early in product development — ideally before initiation of the first clinical study or before submission of the first marketing application. Filing early ensures that the correct center's requirements are applied throughout development and manufacturing scale-up.

FDA 60-Day Response Clock: Upon receipt of a complete RFD, FDA has 60 calendar days to issue a designation. If FDA fails to act within 60 days, the product is automatically assigned to the center that has primary jurisdiction over the most significant mode of action (§3.9(c)). The designation issued by OCP is binding on FDA.

Binding Effect: Once a designation is issued, the lead center identified in the designation is responsible for the premarket review and regulation of the combination product. All FDA centers must honor the designation. If the sponsor later seeks to change the designation, it must demonstrate a substantial change in the product's intended use, constituent parts, or mode of action.

Submissions: Office of Combination Products, WO32-2340, 10903 New Hampshire Ave, Silver Spring, MD 20993

2.3 §3.7(a) — Required RFD Content（RFD ）

Original Text

§3.7(a) — Content of an RFD.

A complete RFD must contain the following seven elements. Failure to include any element may result in a Refuse to Review action, restarting the 60-day clock.

Element 1 — Product Description and Constituent Parts: A complete description of the combination product, including identification of each constituent part (drug, device, biological product), how they are physically or functionally combined, and the overall product configuration.

Element 2 — Regulatory Status of Each Constituent: For each constituent part, a statement of its current regulatory status — whether it is currently approved/cleared, under investigation, or not yet regulated — and any existing FDA regulatory history.

Element 3 — Proposed PMOA with Scientific Rationale: The sponsor's proposed primary mode of action, supported by a detailed scientific rationale explaining why the proposed PMOA provides the most important therapeutic action. This is the most technically demanding element.

Element 4 — Intended Use, Patient Population, Route of Administration: The intended clinical indication, target patient population, and route of administration (e.g., intravascular, subcutaneous, topical), as this directly affects which center has most relevant expertise.

Element 5 — Manufacturing Process Description: A description of the manufacturing process sufficient to understand how the constituent parts are combined, the order of manufacturing steps, and any relevant in-process controls. Not required to be a full batch record, but must be substantive enough to assess process complexity.

Element 6 — Packaging Description (for co-packaged products): If the combination product is co-packaged, a description of the packaging configuration, how the drug and device components are arranged, and how they are intended to be used together by the end user.

Element 7 — Prior FDA Interactions: A summary of any prior communications with FDA regarding this product, including meeting minutes, prior submissions, informal feedback from any FDA center, and any written FDA correspondence relevant to product classification or regulatory status.

2.4 §3.7(b) + §4.3 — PMOA Determination（）

Original Text

§3.7(b) — Primary Mode of Action (PMOA) Determination.

The primary mode of action (PMOA) is the single mode of action of a combination product that provides the most important therapeutic action of the combination product. The most important therapeutic action is the mode of action expected to make the greatest contribution to the overall intended therapeutic effects of the combination product. (21 CFR §3.2(m))

Five Mode of Action Categories:

  • Pharmacological — the product achieves its effect through chemical interaction with biological receptors or biochemical pathways → leads to CDER jurisdiction
  • Immunological — the product achieves its effect through stimulation, suppression, or modulation of the immune system → leads to CBER jurisdiction
  • Metabolic — the product achieves its effect by altering metabolic processes (e.g., gene therapy affecting cellular metabolism) → typically leads to CBER
  • Mechanical — the product achieves its effect through physical force, structural support, or mechanical function (e.g., stents, scaffolds, delivery devices) → leads to CDRH
  • Physical — the product achieves its effect through physical means other than mechanical force (e.g., thermal, electrical, photochemical) → leads to CDRH or CDER depending on specifics

3-Step Analytical Framework:

  1. Step 1 — List all MOAs: Enumerate every mode of action present in the combination product. Be comprehensive; include both primary and secondary effects of each constituent part.
  2. Step 2 — Determine which MOA provides the primary therapeutic effect: Which single MOA makes the greatest contribution to achieving the intended clinical outcome? Consider clinical evidence, mechanism studies, and the product's labeling claims.
  3. Step 3 — Apply tie-breaker if MOA is unclear (§3.8/§4.8(b)): If two MOAs are scientifically indistinguishable in their contribution to therapeutic effect, OCP applies a tie-breaker: (a) assign to the center with the most expertise in the predominant safety/effectiveness questions raised by the combination product; (b) if still tied, assign to the center with the most relevant regulatory experience with the most similar products.

2.5 §4.9 / §3.9 — Center Assignment & Consequences（ CGMP ）

Original Text

§3.9 / §4.9 — Designation of Center with Primary Jurisdiction.

Lead Center (Designated Center): The center identified in the RFD designation has primary jurisdiction over the combination product. The lead center's responsibilities include:

  • Primary reviewer of the first marketing application (IND/NDA for CDER; PMA/510(k) for CDRH; BLA for CBER)
  • Primary point of contact for all regulatory correspondence
  • Coordinates with consulting center(s) on cross-jurisdictional issues
  • Issues final decisions on approval, labeling, and post-market obligations

Consulting Center: The center(s) not designated as lead retain authority to review aspects of the combination product within their area of expertise. The consulting center:

  • Reviews its specific constituent part (e.g., CDRH reviews device constituent even if CDER leads)
  • Provides binding comments to lead center on safety/effectiveness questions within its expertise
  • May request additional data specific to its regulatory area
  • Does not issue independent approvals — all decisions flow through lead center

CGMP Trigger — Direct Link to §4.3: The center assignment directly determines which CGMP obligations apply:

  • CDER leads → 21 CFR Parts 210/211 are the primary CGMP; 21 CFR Part 820 (QMSR) applies to device constituent
  • CDRH leads → 21 CFR Part 820 (QMSR) is primary; 21 CFR Parts 210/211 apply to drug constituent
  • CBER leads → 21 CFR Parts 600–680 plus either 210/211 or 820 depending on constituent parts

Manufacturers may seek a streamlined CGMP approach under §4.4, but the center assignment established by RFD always sets the baseline obligations.

2.6 RFD Timeline & Dispute Resolution（RFD ）

Original Text

RFD Timeline Overview:

Dispute Resolution — §3.10:

If a sponsor disagrees with OCP's designation, the following dispute resolution pathway applies:

  • Step 1 — Request for Reconsideration: Sponsor may submit a written request for reconsideration to OCP within 15 calendar days of receiving the designation. The request must identify specifically what is disputed and provide new scientific or regulatory arguments.
  • Step 2 — OCP Review: OCP reviews the reconsideration request and responds within 15 calendar days of receipt.
  • Step 3 — Appeal to Office of the Commissioner: If the sponsor remains dissatisfied, it may appeal to the Office of the Commissioner within 30 days of the OCP reconsideration decision. This is the final internal FDA level of review.

Note: While a dispute is pending, the original designation remains in effect unless OCP explicitly issues a stay. Sponsors may proceed with development under the original designation while pursuing dispute resolution.

Section 2 Summary

Key Regulatory Points

  • Three paths to center assignment: (1) Informal OCP consultation — fastest, non-binding; (2) Pre-RFD meeting — preparatory, non-binding; (3) Formal RFD — binding designation, 60-day FDA response obligation
  • RFD requires 7 elements (§3.7(a)): Product description, regulatory status of each constituent, PMOA with scientific rationale (most critical), intended use/population/route, manufacturing process, packaging (if co-packaged), and prior FDA interactions
  • PMOA 3-step framework: List all MOAs → identify the MOA making the greatest therapeutic contribution → apply tie-breaker (center with most expertise/experience) if indeterminate
  • Center assignment directly triggers CGMP obligations (§4.3): CDER leads → Parts 210/211 primary; CDRH leads → Part 820 primary; CBER leads → Parts 600–680 primary; secondary CGMP applies to the other constituent part regardless
  • Dispute resolution: 15-day reconsideration window → 15-day OCP response → 30-day appeal to Office of the Commissioner; original designation remains in effect during disputes
